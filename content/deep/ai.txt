== ai-learn

# Learning from examples, not rules
Traditional software is explicit: a programmer writes rules ("if the amount is over €10,000, flag it"). Machine learning inverts that: you supply examples of inputs and desired outputs, and an optimisation process finds the rules, encoded as numbers in a model.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Traditional programming", "items": ["rules + data → answers", "written by people"]}, "r": {"t": "Machine learning", "items": ["data + answers → rules", "learned from examples"]}}
### When ML wins
• The rules are too many or too fuzzy to write down (recognising faces, speech)
• The patterns change over time (fraud tactics, customer behaviour)
• There's lots of historical data with known outcomes
### When rules still win
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tax calculation", "b": "rules, must be exact and auditable"}, {"a": "Safety interlocks", "b": "rules, must be guaranteed"}, {"a": "Spam detection", "b": "ML, patterns shift constantly"}, {"a": "Image recognition", "b": "ML, impossible to hand-code"}]}
### A model is a function
Formally, a trained model is a mathematical function with many adjustable numbers (parameters). Training sets those numbers so that the function maps inputs to outputs as accurately as possible on examples.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Collect labelled examples", "Choose a model family", "Train: adjust parameters", "Deploy: apply to new inputs"]}
!! ML systems understand the world like humans => They learn statistical patterns from data, which can work brilliantly or fail strangely
> The phrase "Software 2.0" (Andrej Karpathy, 2017) describes this shift: code written by optimisation rather than by hand.

# A neuron is a weighted vote
An artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through an activation function. It's a loose caricature of a biological neuron, which fires when enough input signals arrive.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "output = f(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b)", "items": [{"s": "x", "m": "inputs"}, {"s": "w", "m": "weights"}, {"s": "b", "m": "bias"}, {"s": "f", "m": "activation function"}]}
### The beach example worked out
Sunny (1) × 3 + weekend (1) × 2 + rain (0) × −4 + bias (−2) = 3. Above zero, so "go to the beach". If it rains: 3 + 2 − 4 − 2 = −1, so stay home.
ex: 1×3 + 1×2 + 0×(−4) − 2 = 3 → go | With rain: 3 + 2 − 4 − 2 = −1 | → stay home
### Activation functions
Without a non-linear "twist", stacking layers would collapse into one big linear sum. Activation functions add that twist.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Step", "b": "on or off, the original perceptron"}, {"a": "Sigmoid", "b": "smooth 0 to 1"}, {"a": "ReLU", "b": "max(0, x), today's default"}, {"a": "GELU", "b": "smooth variant used in transformers"}]}
### From neurons to networks
Neurons are arranged in layers. Early layers detect simple patterns; later layers combine them into complex concepts. Modern networks have billions of weights.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Output layer: the decision", "Hidden layers: combinations of features", "Input layer: raw numbers"]}
!? The first artificial neuron model was proposed by Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts in 1943; Frank Rosenblatt built the "Perceptron" machine in 1958.

# Try it: be the neuron
The widget lets you be the neuron's inputs. Notice three things as you play: inputs with big positive weights push towards "fire"; negative weights act as vetoes; and the bias sets how easily the neuron fires at all.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Positive weight", "b": "evidence for"}, {"a": "Negative weight", "b": "evidence against"}, {"a": "Bias", "b": "default reluctance or eagerness"}, {"a": "Threshold", "b": "the decision line at zero"}]}
### What training would do
In a real network, nobody sets these weights by hand. Training adjusts each weight slightly after every batch of examples, increasing weights that helped correct answers and decreasing those that misled.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Inputs", "Weighted sum + bias", "Activation", "Output compared with the right answer", "Weights adjusted"]}
### Why one neuron isn't enough
A single neuron draws one straight line between "yes" and "no". Some problems, like XOR ("one or the other but not both"), can't be split by a single line. In 1969 Minsky and Papert highlighted this, helping trigger the first "AI winter". Multiple layers solve it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "One neuron", "items": ["one straight boundary", "can't do XOR"]}, "r": {"t": "Two layers", "items": ["curved boundaries", "can learn XOR and much more"]}}
> Every large AI model is, at bottom, billions of these simple weighted sums.

# Training means nudging
Training minimises a loss function: a number measuring how wrong the model is on the training examples. Gradient descent computes, for every weight, which direction would reduce the loss, and moves each weight a small step that way.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Forward pass: make predictions", "Compute the loss", "Backpropagation: compute gradients", "Update weights a little"]}
### Key ingredients
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Loss function", "b": "what \"wrong\" means, e.g. squared error"}, {"a": "Gradient", "b": "direction of steepest increase in loss"}, {"a": "Learning rate", "b": "size of each step"}, {"a": "Batch", "b": "a small sample of examples per step"}]}
### Backpropagation
Backpropagation uses the chain rule from calculus to calculate gradients for all weights efficiently, layer by layer from the output backwards. Popularised in 1986 by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, it's the engine of deep learning.
### The learning rate dilemma
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too small", "items": ["slow", "may get stuck"]}, "r": {"t": "Too large", "items": ["overshoots", "training diverges"]}}
### Scale
Frontier language models are trained for months on thousands of specialised chips, processing trillions of tokens, with total compute in the range of 10²⁵ floating-point operations or more.
!? Modern optimisers like Adam adapt the step size for each weight automatically, which made training deep networks far more reliable.

# Memorising isn't learning
Overfitting happens when a model learns the training data's noise and quirks instead of the underlying pattern. It scores brilliantly on training examples and poorly on new ones. Underfitting is the opposite: too simple to capture the pattern at all.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Underfitting", "items": ["too simple", "bad on train and test"]}, "r": {"t": "Overfitting", "items": ["too complex", "great on train, bad on test"]}}
### Spotting it
Plot error on training and validation data as training proceeds. When training error keeps falling but validation error starts rising, the model has begun memorising.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "error (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Train, early", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Validation, early", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "Train, late", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Validation, late", "v": 28.0, "hi": true}]}
### Remedies
• More (and more varied) training data
• Simpler models or fewer features
• Regularisation: penalising very large weights
• Dropout: randomly switching off neurons during training
• Early stopping: halt when validation error stops improving
### The bias–variance trade-off
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "High bias", "b": "systematically wrong, underfits"}, {"a": "High variance", "b": "overly sensitive to the training set, overfits"}, {"a": "Goal", "b": "balance, generalising well"}]}
!? Very large modern networks sometimes show "double descent": test error gets worse, then better again as models grow far beyond the point where they can memorise everything.

# Features: what the model looks at
A feature is a measurable input. Good features capture information relevant to the target and are available reliably at prediction time.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "House price features", "items": ["Size: m², rooms", "Location: distance to coast, district", "Condition: year built, renovation", "Market: interest rates, season"]}
### Feature engineering
Before deep learning, teams spent most of their time crafting features: ratios, aggregates, time since last event. For tabular business data (sales, transactions), it's still crucial, and tree-based models like gradient boosting often beat deep learning there.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tabular data", "items": ["engineered features", "gradient boosting often best"]}, "r": {"t": "Images, audio, text", "items": ["raw inputs", "deep learning learns features"]}}
### Data leakage
A classic error is using a feature that wouldn't exist at prediction time, such as "account closed date" to predict churn. The model looks amazing in testing and fails in production.
!! More features always help => Irrelevant or leaky features hurt accuracy and robustness
### Learned features
In deep networks, early layers learn edges and textures, later layers learn object parts and concepts: features discovered automatically from raw pixels or text.
> The model can only be as good as the information its features carry.

# Train, validate, test
Splitting data simulates the future: you want to know how the model will perform on data it has never seen. A common split is about 70/15/15, though it varies with dataset size.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Training set", "b": "adjusts the model's parameters"}, {"a": "Validation set", "b": "chooses settings and compares models"}, {"a": "Test set", "b": "one final honest score"}, {"a": "Production data", "b": "the real exam"}]}
### Cross-validation
With limited data, k-fold cross-validation rotates which part is held out, training k times and averaging the results for a more reliable estimate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Split data into 5 folds", "Train on 4, test on 1", "Rotate 5 times", "Average the scores"]}
### Time matters
For forecasting, split by time: train on the past, validate on later data. Random splits leak future information into training and give over-optimistic results.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Random split", "items": ["fine for independent examples"]}, "r": {"t": "Time-based split", "items": ["required for forecasting"]}}
### Distribution shift
Even a well-tested model can degrade when the world changes: new products, new fraud tactics, a pandemic. Production models need monitoring and periodic retraining.
!? Kaggle competitions keep a "private leaderboard" test set hidden until the end, because teams who tune endlessly on the public set often drop in the final ranking.

# The simplest model: a straight line
Linear regression fits y = w·x + b to data by choosing w and b to minimise the squared differences between predicted and actual values. It's centuries old (Legendre and Gauss, around 1805) and still widely used.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "y = w·x + b", "items": [{"s": "y", "m": "prediction"}, {"s": "x", "m": "input feature"}, {"s": "w", "m": "slope, how much y changes per unit of x"}, {"s": "b", "m": "intercept"}]}
### Many features
With several features, it becomes y = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b. Each weight shows how much the prediction changes when that feature increases by one unit, holding the others fixed: easy to interpret.
ex: Price = 2,500 × m² + 15,000 × rooms + 20,000 | 100 m², 3 rooms | ≈ €315,000
### Logistic regression
For yes/no outcomes, logistic regression squashes the linear sum into a probability between 0 and 1. It's a common baseline for credit scoring and churn.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Linear regression", "items": ["predicts a number"]}, "r": {"t": "Logistic regression", "items": ["predicts a probability"]}}
### Why start simple
• Fast to train, easy to explain to regulators and managers
• Gives a baseline to beat
• Often surprisingly competitive on structured data
> A neural network is many linear models stacked with non-linear twists between them.

# More data often beats a cleverer algorithm
In 2001 Banko and Brill showed that for a language task, simple algorithms improved steadily with more data, and the differences between algorithms shrank. In 2009 Google's Halevy, Norvig and Pereira wrote "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data".
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "accuracy, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "1 million words", "v": 82.0}, {"l": "10 million", "v": 88.0}, {"l": "100 million", "v": 93.0}, {"l": "1 billion words", "v": 96.0, "hi": true}]}
### Scaling laws
For large neural networks, researchers found smooth "scaling laws" (2020): performance improves predictably as you increase data, model size and compute together. This guided the race to bigger language models.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["More data", "Bigger model", "More compute", "Predictably lower error"]}
### But quality matters
• Label noise teaches wrong patterns
• Duplicates distort the model and inflate test scores
• Biased samples produce biased models
• Curated, high-quality data can beat raw quantity, as later language models showed
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lots of messy data", "items": ["learns noise too"]}, "r": {"t": "Less but cleaner data", "items": ["often better per example"]}}
!! Data is the new oil => Raw data is cheap; clean, relevant, well-labelled data is what's valuable
> In business projects, most effort typically goes into finding, cleaning and understanding data, not modelling.

# A spam filter, step by step
Spam filtering was one of the first mass-market ML applications. Early filters (2002, Paul Graham's "A Plan for Spam") used naive Bayes: estimating how likely each word is in spam versus legitimate email.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Email arrives", "Extract features: words, links, sender", "Model gives spam probability", "Compare with threshold", "Inbox or spam folder"]}
### Thresholds and trade-offs
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low threshold, e.g. 0.6", "items": ["catches more spam", "more good emails lost"]}, "r": {"t": "High threshold, e.g. 0.95", "items": ["few false alarms", "more spam gets through"]}}
### Measuring it
• **Precision**: of emails flagged as spam, how many really were spam?
• **Recall**: of all spam, how much did we catch?
• Losing an important email is usually worse than seeing a spam, so filters favour precision
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "outcome per 1,000 emails (example)", "items": [{"l": "Spam caught", "v": 290.0}, {"l": "Spam missed", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Good emails misflagged", "v": 2.0, "hi": true}]}
### An adversarial game
Spammers adapt: misspellings, images instead of text, hijacked accounts. Filters retrain continuously and combine ML with sender reputation, authentication (SPF, DKIM) and user reports.
> Every business ML system involves the same choices: features, a score, a threshold and the cost of each kind of error.

== ai-chat

# Text becomes tokens
Tokenisers split text using a learned vocabulary, often via byte-pair encoding: start with characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent pairs until the vocabulary reaches, say, 100,000–200,000 tokens.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["\"unbelievable\"", "\"un\" + \"believ\" + \"able\"", "Token IDs: e.g. 359, 91224, 481", "Fed to the model"]}
### Rules of thumb
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1 token", "b": "~4 characters of English"}, {"a": "100 tokens", "b": "~75 words"}, {"a": "1 page", "b": "~500–700 tokens"}, {"a": "Portuguese", "b": "often ~20–40% more tokens than English for the same text"}]}
### Why it matters
• **Cost**: APIs charge per input and output token
• **Limits**: context windows are counted in tokens
• **Quirks**: models can struggle to count letters in a word because they see tokens, not letters
• **Languages**: less common languages and scripts often need more tokens, costing more
!? The classic failure "how many r's are in strawberry?" happened because models see "straw" + "berry" as chunks, not individual letters.
> Numbers are tokenised oddly too, which is one reason models can stumble on arithmetic without tools.

# It predicts the next word
A language model outputs a probability distribution over its whole vocabulary for the next token. Generation is a loop: sample a token, append it, recompute probabilities, repeat. This is called autoregressive generation.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Read text so far", "Compute probabilities for next token", "Choose one", "Append and repeat"]}
### Example
For "The capital of Portugal is", the model might assign "Lisbon" 92%, "a" 3%, "Porto" 1% and tiny probabilities to thousands of others.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "probability of next token, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Lisbon", "v": 92.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "a", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "the", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Porto", "v": 1.0}]}
### Why prediction leads to "understanding"
To predict text well across physics papers, legal contracts, novels and code, the model must internalise grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and even models of the people writing. Whether that counts as understanding is debated, but the capabilities are real and measurable.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sceptical view", "items": ["\"stochastic parrot\"", "pattern matching"]}, "r": {"t": "Capability view", "items": ["compressed world knowledge", "emergent reasoning"]}}
### Reasoning models
Since 2024, "reasoning" models are trained to generate long chains of intermediate thinking before answering, which greatly improves maths, coding and planning.
> Every response is built one token at a time; the model doesn't plan a whole paragraph in advance the way people often assume, though it does carry internal "intentions" forward.

# Try it: turn up the temperature
Temperature rescales the model's probabilities before sampling. Low temperatures sharpen the distribution towards the most likely token; high temperatures flatten it, giving unlikely tokens a chance.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "probability of top word, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Temp 0.2", "v": 99.0}, {"l": "Temp 0.7", "v": 75.0}, {"l": "Temp 1.0", "v": 55.0}, {"l": "Temp 1.5", "v": 35.0, "hi": true}]}
### Which setting when?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "0–0.3", "b": "extraction, classification, factual Q&A"}, {"a": "0.5–0.8", "b": "general assistance"}, {"a": "0.9–1.2", "b": "brainstorming, creative writing"}, {"a": "Above 1.5", "b": "increasingly incoherent"}]}
### Other sampling controls
• **Top-k**: only consider the k most likely tokens
• **Top-p (nucleus)**: only consider the smallest set of tokens adding up to probability p, e.g. 0.9
• **Seed**: fixes randomness for reproducibility, where supported
### Temperature 0 isn't fully deterministic
Even at temperature 0, outputs can vary slightly between runs because of parallel computation on GPUs and batching. Don't rely on identical outputs without testing.
!! Higher temperature makes the model smarter or more creative => It only adds randomness; creativity comes from the prompt and the model

# Meaning as a map
An embedding is a vector, a list of hundreds to thousands of numbers, representing a token, word, sentence or document. Training places items that appear in similar contexts close together in this high-dimensional space.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "cat", "b": "close to dog, kitten"}, {"a": "Lisbon", "b": "close to Porto, Madrid"}, {"a": "invoice", "b": "close to receipt, bill"}, {"a": "happy", "b": "close to joyful"}]}
### Word arithmetic
In word2vec (2013), vector("king") − vector("man") + vector("woman") landed closest to vector("queen"). Directions encode relationships like gender, tense or country–capital.
ex: king − man + woman | ≈ queen | Paris − France + Portugal ≈ Lisbon
### Measuring similarity
Similarity is usually measured by cosine similarity: the angle between two vectors. 1 means same direction, 0 unrelated.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "similarity = cos(θ) = (a · b) / (", "items": [{"s": "a| |b|) | a, b", "m": "embedding vectors"}, {"s": "θ", "m": "angle between them"}]}
### Business uses
• Semantic search: find documents by meaning, not exact keywords
• Retrieval for chatbots (RAG)
• Recommendations, deduplication, clustering support tickets
> Embeddings are how machines turn messy meaning into geometry they can compute with.

# Fluent isn't the same as true
Hallucination is when a model generates content that sounds plausible but is false or unsupported: invented citations, wrong figures, fake quotes. It arises because the training objective rewards plausible continuations, not verified truth.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why models hallucinate", "items": ["Gaps in training data", "Pressure to always answer", "Similar-looking facts blur together", "No built-in source checking"]}
### High-risk areas
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Exact numbers and dates", "Citations and URLs", "Quotes", "Niche people and companies", "Legal and medical specifics"]}
### Reducing it
• Ground answers in supplied documents (retrieval) and ask for citations
• Let the model say "I don't know"; reward it in evaluation
• Use tools: search, calculators, databases
• Verify important outputs; use a second model or a human to check
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ungrounded", "items": ["answers from memory", "higher risk"]}, "r": {"t": "Grounded with sources", "items": ["cites documents", "checkable"]}}
### Famous incidents
In 2023 lawyers in New York were sanctioned for submitting a brief with case citations invented by a chatbot. Several similar cases followed worldwide.
!? Newer models hallucinate much less on common facts, but rates on obscure details remain significant, so verification is still essential.

# The context window
The context window is the model's working memory: everything it can attend to when producing the next token, including system instructions, conversation history, documents and its own output so far.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "context window, thousand tokens (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "GPT-3, 2020", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "GPT-4, 2023", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Claude 2.1, 2023", "v": 200.0}, {"l": "Gemini 1.5, 2024", "v": 1000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Bigger isn't the whole story
• Cost and latency grow with the amount of context processed
• Models can be worse at using information buried in the middle of very long contexts ("lost in the middle")
• Retrieval (fetching only relevant pieces) is often cheaper and more accurate than stuffing everything in
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Long context", "items": ["simple", "costly per call", "may miss details"]}, "r": {"t": "Retrieval", "items": ["targeted", "cheaper", "needs good search"]}}
### Memory across chats
A model doesn't remember previous conversations by itself. Products add "memory" by storing notes or summaries and inserting them into the context of new conversations.
> Think of the context window as the desk the model works on: everything it needs must be on the desk.

# Pre-training, then fine-tuning
Building an assistant happens in stages. Pre-training on trillions of tokens produces a "base model" that continues text. Post-training turns it into a helpful, honest, safe assistant.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pre-training: predict next token on huge text", "Supervised fine-tuning: example dialogues", "Preference training: RLHF or RLAIF", "Safety testing and deployment"]}
### RLHF
Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people compare pairs of responses; a reward model learns their preferences; the language model is then optimised to produce responses the reward model rates highly. Popularised by InstructGPT (2022).
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Base model", "b": "knowledgeable, unpredictable"}, {"a": "Fine-tuned", "b": "follows instructions"}, {"a": "RLHF", "b": "more helpful and aligned"}, {"a": "Constitutional AI", "b": "AI feedback guided by written principles"}]}
### Reinforcement learning on tasks
Newer pipelines also train models with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks, such as maths problems and code with tests, which produces the long step-by-step reasoning of "reasoning models".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pre-training", "items": ["months", "most of the compute", "general knowledge"]}, "r": {"t": "Post-training", "items": ["shorter", "shapes behaviour, style and safety"]}}
!? A base model asked "What is the capital of France?" might continue with more quiz questions instead of answering, because that's how such text often continues online.

# Better prompts, better answers
A prompt is the model's entire briefing. Vague prompts get generic answers. Good prompts give context, the goal, constraints and examples.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Role and audience", "The task and its purpose", "Relevant data pasted in", "Constraints: length, tone, format", "Examples of good output"]}
### Before and after
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"Write about our product\"", "r": "\"Write a 120-word LinkedIn post for CFOs about how our tool cuts month-end close by 2 days, confident, no hype, end with a question\""}]}
### Useful techniques
• **Give examples** (few-shot): show two or three samples of the desired output
• **Ask for reasoning** on complex problems, or use a reasoning model
• **Structure inputs** with headings or tags so the model knows what's what
• **Iterate**: treat the first answer as a draft and give specific feedback

> A useful test: would a smart new colleague be able to do the task well from your prompt alone?

# Models don't look things up by themselves
A model's built-in knowledge is frozen at its training cutoff and is fuzzy on details. Tool use lets the model request actions, like searching, querying a database or running code, and read the results before answering.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["User question", "Model decides it needs a tool", "Calls search or database", "Reads results", "Answers with sources"]}
### Common tools
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Web search", "b": "current events and prices"}, {"a": "File and database access", "b": "company knowledge"}, {"a": "Code execution", "b": "accurate calculations and charts"}, {"a": "APIs", "b": "take actions, e.g. book, send, update"}]}
### Function calling
Developers describe tools with names and parameters; the model outputs a structured request like `get_weather(city="Porto")`, the application runs it and feeds back the result. Standards such as the Model Context Protocol (MCP) make tools reusable across apps.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "No tools", "items": ["memory only", "stale", "may guess"]}, "r": {"t": "With tools", "items": ["fresh data", "precise", "actionable"]}}
### Risks
Tools add power and risk: a model reading a malicious web page could be manipulated by hidden instructions ("prompt injection"). Permissions and human approval for sensitive actions matter.
!? Agents are essentially models running in a loop: think, call a tool, observe, repeat, until the task is done.

# What "70B" means
Parameters are the learned numbers in a model. Their count roughly indicates capacity, though training data, architecture and post-training matter as much.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "memory ≈ parameters × bytes per parameter", "items": [{"s": "", "m": "70 B params × 2 bytes (16-bit) = 140 GB"}, {"s": "", "m": "× 0.5 byte (4-bit) = 35 GB"}]}
### Quantisation
Storing weights with fewer bits shrinks models with modest quality loss. A 4-bit version of a 70B model fits in about 35–40 GB, possible on a high-end workstation.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "memory for a 70B model, GB", "items": [{"l": "32-bit", "v": 280.0}, {"l": "16-bit", "v": 140.0}, {"l": "8-bit", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "4-bit", "v": 35.0, "hi": true}]}
### More than weights
Running a model also needs memory for the "KV cache" holding the context being processed, which grows with context length and the number of simultaneous users.
### Mixture of experts
Many recent models use a mixture-of-experts design: the total parameter count is large, but only a fraction is active for each token, cutting compute per token.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Dense model", "items": ["all parameters used per token"]}, "r": {"t": "Mixture of experts", "items": ["only a few \"experts\" active per token"]}}
!! Bigger models are always better => Smaller, well-trained or specialised models often match bigger ones on specific tasks at far lower cost

# Supervised learning
Supervised learning learns a mapping from inputs to known outputs using labelled examples. It's like learning with an answer key.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Historical examples with outcomes", "Train model", "Validate on unseen examples", "Predict outcomes for new cases"]}
### Everyday examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bank", "b": "will this loan default?"}, {"a": "Retailer", "b": "how many units will sell next week?"}, {"a": "Insurer", "b": "is this claim fraudulent?"}, {"a": "Hospital", "b": "does this scan show a fracture?"}]}
### Common algorithms
• Linear and logistic regression: simple, interpretable baselines
• Decision trees and random forests: handle mixed data well
• Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM): often best for tabular business data
• Neural networks: best for images, audio, text
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tabular data", "items": ["gradient boosting often wins"]}, "r": {"t": "Unstructured data", "items": ["neural networks dominate"]}}
### The catch
Supervised learning learns what the past looked like. If labels reflect past biases or the world changes, the model will reproduce yesterday's patterns.
> Ask early: do we have enough reliable historical outcomes to learn from?

# Classification vs regression
Both are supervised; the difference is the type of output.

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Classification", "b": "accuracy, precision, recall, AUC"}, {"a": "Regression", "b": "mean absolute error, RMSE, R²"}, {"a": "Forecasting", "b": "MAPE, forecast bias"}, {"a": "Ranking", "b": "NDCG, hit rate"}]}

• **Binary**: two classes (fraud or not)
• **Multi-class**: one of many (which of 50 product categories)

You can turn regression into classification by binning ("demand high/medium/low") and get probabilities from classifiers that behave like scores. The choice should follow the business decision being made.

# Unsupervised learning
Without labels, the goal is to discover structure: groups, unusual points, compact representations. It's exploratory and often feeds later supervised work.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Unsupervised tasks", "items": ["Clustering: find groups", "Anomaly detection: find oddities", "Dimensionality reduction: compress features", "Topic modelling: find themes in text"]}
### Typical algorithms
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "k-means", "b": "splits data into k groups"}, {"a": "DBSCAN", "b": "finds dense clusters of any shape"}, {"a": "PCA", "b": "compresses many features into a few"}, {"a": "Isolation forest", "b": "flags anomalies"}]}
### Anomaly detection in practice
Banks and factories use it to spot transactions or sensor readings unlike the norm, without needing examples of every possible fraud or failure.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Learn what \"normal\" looks like", "Score new data", "Flag unusual cases", "Human review"]}
### Evaluating without labels
There's no answer key, so success is judged by whether results are stable, interpretable and useful: do the groups make business sense? Do flagged anomalies lead to real findings?

# Clustering customers
k-means clustering assigns each customer to the nearest of k centre points, then moves each centre to the average of its customers, repeating until stable. Features might include spend, visit frequency, recency and product categories.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Place k centres", "Assign customers to nearest centre", "Move centres to group averages", "Repeat until stable"]}
### RFM segmentation
A classic retail approach scores customers by Recency, Frequency and Monetary value, then clusters them.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Champions", "b": "recent, frequent, high spend"}, {"a": "Loyal", "b": "frequent, medium spend"}, {"a": "At risk", "b": "used to buy often, not lately"}, {"a": "New", "b": "recent first purchase"}]}
### Choosing k
Too few clusters hide important differences; too many become unmanageable. Analysts use the "elbow method" (where adding clusters stops helping much) and, above all, business judgement.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "within-cluster error (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "k=2", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "k=3", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "k=4", "v": 42.0}, {"l": "k=5", "v": 36.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "k=6", "v": 33.0}]}
### From clusters to action
• Tailored offers for each segment
• Retention campaigns for "at risk" customers

In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent interacts with an environment, receiving rewards. It learns a policy, a rule for choosing actions, that maximises total future reward, balancing exploration (trying new things) with exploitation (using what works).

### Landmarks

### Where it's used

• Robotics: learning to walk and grasp, often in simulation first
• Data-centre cooling optimisation
• Training language models with human feedback and on verifiable tasks

# Self-supervised learning
Self-supervised learning creates training signals from the data itself: predict the next word, a masked word, a missing image patch, or whether two views come from the same image. This unlocks the internet's unlabelled data.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Next-token prediction", "b": "GPT-style language models"}, {"a": "Masked word", "b": "BERT"}, {"a": "Masked image patches", "b": "vision models"}, {"a": "Matching image and caption", "b": "CLIP"}]}
### Why it was a breakthrough
Labelled datasets top out at millions of examples; unlabelled text and images run into trillions. Self-supervised pre-training lets models learn general representations at that scale, then adapt to specific tasks with few labels.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "millions of training examples (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "ImageNet", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "Web text", "v": 15000000.0, "t": "15000000", "hi": true}]}
### Foundation models
Models pre-trained this way are called foundation models: one general model adapted to countless tasks through prompting or fine-tuning.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Self-supervised pre-training", "Foundation model", "Prompt or fine-tune", "Many downstream tasks"]}
> Yann LeCun called self-supervised learning "the cake", with supervised learning the icing and RL the cherry.

# Labels are expensive
Labelling requires time, expertise and consistency. Medical image labels may cost tens of euros each; complex legal or engineering annotations far more. Disagreement between labellers adds noise.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "cost per label, € (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Simple image tag", "v": 0.05}, {"l": "Text sentiment", "v": 0.2}, {"l": "Medical scan", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Legal clause review", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}]}
### Ways to reduce cost
• **Pre-train, then fine-tune**: needs far fewer labels
• **Active learning**: the model picks the most informative examples for humans to label
• **Weak supervision**: use rules and heuristics to generate noisy labels
• **LLM-assisted labelling**: a model proposes labels, humans review
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Model flags uncertain cases", "Humans label those", "Retrain", "Repeat"]}
### Label quality
Clear guidelines, examples, double-labelling and measuring agreement between labellers make a big difference. Bad labels quietly cap a model's accuracy.
!! Labels are ground truth => Labels are human judgements and can be inconsistent or biased

# Semi-supervised: a little help goes far
Semi-supervised learning combines a small labelled set with a large unlabelled one. A common technique, pseudo-labelling, trains on labelled data, predicts labels for unlabelled data, keeps only confident predictions, and retrains.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Train on few labels", "Predict unlabelled data", "Keep confident predictions", "Retrain on the larger set"]}
### Why it works
Unlabelled data shows the shape of the data: where natural clusters and boundaries lie. Decision boundaries should pass through sparse regions, not cut through dense clusters.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Labelled only", "items": ["boundary guessed from few points"]}, "r": {"t": "With unlabelled", "items": ["boundary follows gaps between clusters"]}}
### Risks
If the model's early guesses are wrong but confident, it can reinforce its own mistakes ("confirmation bias"). Careful thresholds and validation on genuine labels are essential.
• Works best when unlabelled data comes from the same distribution as labelled data
• Common in speech recognition and document classification
> In practice, many teams now start from a pre-trained foundation model, which achieves similar benefits more easily.

# Missing values
Missing data is common and rarely random. How you handle it can change results significantly.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why values go missing", "items": ["Missing completely at random: sensor glitch", "Missing at random: related to other known data", "Missing not at random: related to the missing value itself"]}
### Options
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Drop rows", "b": "simple, but loses data and may bias"}, {"a": "Fill with mean or median", "b": "quick, can distort"}, {"a": "Model-based imputation", "b": "predict the missing value"}, {"a": "Missing flag", "b": "let the model learn from \"missingness\""}]}
### Missing can be informative
A loan applicant who leaves "employer" blank, or a machine whose temperature sensor stopped reporting, may be telling you something. Adding a "was missing" indicator often improves models.

# Data leakage
Leakage occurs when information from outside the prediction moment sneaks into training. Test scores look excellent; real-world performance collapses.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Leakage types", "items": ["Future data: using values recorded after the event", "Target proxies: features derived from the answer", "Train–test contamination: same records in both sets", "Preprocessing leaks: scaling using the whole dataset"]}
### Classic examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Churn model", "b": "uses \"account closure reason\""}, {"a": "Hospital readmission", "b": "uses discharge codes added later"}, {"a": "Fraud model", "b": "uses \"chargeback flag\", only known after fraud"}, {"a": "Price forecast", "b": "uses tomorrow's data by mistake"}]}
### How to prevent it
• Build features "as of" the prediction time, using point-in-time snapshots
• Split data by time for forecasting
• Fit scalers and encoders on training data only
• Be suspicious of any model that seems too good to be true
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Define prediction moment", "Only use data available then", "Time-based validation", "Sanity-check surprisingly high scores"]}
!? In a famous case, a pneumonia-risk model learned that asthma patients had lower risk, because they were sent straight to intensive care: a correlation driven by treatment, not biology.

# Sampling bias
Sampling bias happens when training data systematically differs from the population a model will serve. The model may work well for the majority and poorly for others.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Training data", "items": ["mostly urban customers", "daytime traffic"]}, "r": {"t": "Real world", "items": ["rural customers too", "night and weather"]}}
### Famous examples
• In 2018 the "Gender Shades" study found commercial face-analysis systems had error rates under 1% for lighter-skinned men but up to about 35% for darker-skinned women
• Speech recognisers trained mostly on certain accents performed worse on others
• Models trained on one hospital's equipment struggled with another's scanners
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "error rate, % (Gender Shades, worst system)", "items": [{"l": "Lighter-skinned men", "v": 0.8}, {"l": "Darker-skinned women", "v": 34.7, "hi": true}]}
### Survivorship bias
Training only on successful cases, like loans that were approved, hides how rejected applicants would have behaved. The model never learns about the people who were filtered out.
### Fixes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Audit data by subgroup", "Collect more diverse data", "Evaluate performance per group", "Reweight under-represented cases"]}
> Ask "who or what is missing from this dataset?" before trusting results.

# Linear regression
Linear regression finds weights that minimise the sum of squared differences between predictions and actual values (ordinary least squares). There's even a closed-form solution, no iterative training required for small problems.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "ŷ = b + w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + …", "items": [{"s": "ŷ", "m": "prediction"}, {"s": "b", "m": "intercept"}, {"s": "w", "m": "weights"}, {"s": "x", "m": "features"}]}
### Interpreting weights
Each weight is the expected change in the prediction for a one-unit increase in that feature, holding others constant. That makes linear models popular wherever decisions must be explained.
ex: Rent = 300 + 9 × m² + 150 × near metro | 70 m², near metro | 300 + 630 + 150 = €1,080
### Assumptions and pitfalls
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Linearity", "b": "effects add up in straight lines"}, {"a": "Independence", "b": "errors don't depend on each other"}, {"a": "Outliers", "b": "can pull the line a lot"}, {"a": "Correlated features", "b": "unstable weights"}]}
### Regularised versions
Ridge and lasso regression add a penalty for large weights. Lasso can shrink unhelpful weights to exactly zero, selecting features automatically.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ridge", "items": ["shrinks all weights", "stable"]}, "r": {"t": "Lasso", "items": ["zeroes some weights", "selects features"]}}
!? The term "regression" comes from Francis Galton's 1880s observation that children of very tall parents tend to be less extreme: "regression towards the mean".

# Logistic regression
Logistic regression computes a weighted sum, then passes it through the sigmoid function to get a probability between 0 and 1. It's trained by maximising the likelihood of the observed outcomes.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "p = 1 / (1 + e^−z)", "items": [{"s": "z", "m": "weighted sum of features"}, {"s": "p", "m": "probability of \"yes\""}]}
### Odds and scorecards
Each weight changes the log-odds of the outcome. Credit scorecards convert these weights into points: e.g. "homeowner +25 points, missed payment −40 points", making decisions transparent and auditable.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Homeowner", "b": "+25 points"}, {"a": "3+ years at job", "b": "+15 points"}, {"a": "Missed payment", "b": "−40 points"}, {"a": "Total above cut-off", "b": "approve"}]}
### Why it's still everywhere
• Regulators and auditors can follow every step
• Robust with modest data
• Calibrated probabilities: a 20% score really means about 1 in 5
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Logistic regression", "items": ["transparent", "stable", "calibrated"]}, "r": {"t": "Complex models", "items": ["often more accurate", "harder to explain"]}}
### The threshold
0.5 is just a default. A bank might reject only above 0.3 default risk, or above 0.1, depending on margins, risk appetite and regulation.
> Many "AI" credit decisions in practice still rely on logistic-regression scorecards, sometimes enriched with ML features.

# Decision trees
A decision tree splits data with a sequence of questions chosen to make each resulting group as "pure" as possible. Algorithms like CART pick the split that most reduces impurity (Gini or entropy) at each step.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Loan decision", "items": ["Income above €30k?: Yes: check missed payments", "No: check guarantor", "Leaves: approve or decline"]}
### Strengths
• Easy to visualise and explain to non-specialists
• Handle numbers and categories, little preparation needed
• Capture interactions ("young AND renting AND new job")
### Weaknesses
• A single deep tree overfits badly
• Small data changes can produce a very different tree
• Predictions are step-like, not smooth
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Shallow tree", "items": ["simple", "may underfit"]}, "r": {"t": "Deep tree", "items": ["detailed", "overfits"]}}
### Pruning
Limiting depth or cutting back branches that don't improve validation accuracy controls overfitting.
!? Doctors have long used hand-made decision trees as clinical flowcharts; ML trees automate building them from data.

# Random forests
A random forest, introduced by Leo Breiman in 2001, trains many trees on bootstrap samples (random samples with replacement) and considers only a random subset of features at each split. Averaging many decorrelated trees reduces variance dramatically.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Many bootstrap samples", "One tree per sample", "Hundreds of different trees", "Average or vote"]}
### Why randomness helps
If every tree saw the same data and features, they'd make the same mistakes. Randomness makes their errors different, so they cancel out when averaged: the wisdom of crowds.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "error (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Single tree", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "10 trees", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "100 trees", "v": 13.0}, {"l": "500 trees", "v": 12.0, "hi": true}]}
### Practical benefits
• Strong results with little tuning
• Built-in estimate of accuracy using "out-of-bag" samples
• Feature importance scores to see what matters
### Limits
• Large models can be slow to predict
• Less accurate than gradient boosting on many tasks
• Feature importance can be biased towards features with many values
> Random forests are a great "second model" after a linear baseline.

# Gradient boosting
Gradient boosting builds trees sequentially. Each new tree is trained to predict the remaining errors (residuals) of the current ensemble, and its output is added with a small learning rate. Many weak trees combine into a strong model.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Current model predicts", "Compute errors", "Fit a small tree to errors", "Add it, scaled down"]}
### Popular libraries
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "XGBoost", "b": "2014, popularised boosting in competitions"}, {"a": "LightGBM", "b": "Microsoft, very fast on large data"}, {"a": "CatBoost", "b": "Yandex, good with categorical features"}, {"a": "scikit-learn", "b": "HistGradientBoosting"}]}
### Where it dominates
Benchmarks on tabular datasets repeatedly find gradient-boosted trees matching or beating deep learning, especially on medium-sized data with heterogeneous features.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gradient boosting", "items": ["tabular data", "fast", "strong out of the box"]}, "r": {"t": "Neural networks", "items": ["images, text, audio", "need more data"]}}
### Key settings
• Number of trees and learning rate (more trees, smaller steps)
• Tree depth (interaction complexity)
• Early stopping on validation data
!? Explanation tools like SHAP values were widely adopted largely to explain gradient-boosted models in finance and healthcare.

# k-nearest neighbours
k-NN stores all training examples and, for a new case, finds the k closest ones by a distance measure, then votes (classification) or averages (regression). There's no training phase: it's "lazy learning".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["New item", "Measure distance to all known items", "Take k nearest", "Vote or average"]}
### Choosing k
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Small k, e.g. 1", "items": ["follows noise", "jagged boundaries"]}, "r": {"t": "Large k, e.g. 50", "items": ["smoother", "may blur real differences"]}}
### Distance matters
Features must be scaled, or one measured in large numbers (salary in euros) dominates one in small numbers (years of experience). Choosing the right distance metric is key.
### Modern relevance
The idea is everywhere in vector search: retrieving the most similar documents, products or images by embedding distance is nearest-neighbour search, accelerated by approximate algorithms like HNSW.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Classic k-NN", "b": "small tabular data"}, {"a": "Vector databases", "b": "approximate nearest neighbours at scale"}, {"a": "Recommenders", "b": "\"customers similar to you\""}]}
!? Real-estate "comparables", valuing a house from similar recent sales, is human k-nearest neighbours.

k-means minimises the total squared distance between points and their assigned cluster centre. It's fast and scales to millions of points, but you must choose k, and results depend on the starting positions.

# Deep learning isn't always best
On structured, tabular data, studies like Grinsztajn et al. (2022) found tree-based models still outperform deep networks on typical medium-sized datasets, while being faster to train and tune.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tabular data", "items": ["trees usually win", "less tuning"]}, "r": {"t": "Images, audio, text", "items": ["deep learning wins", "learns features"]}}
### Why trees suit tables
• Columns have different meanings and scales; trees don't care
• Business data has sharp thresholds ("over 90 days late") that trees capture naturally
• Irrelevant columns hurt neural nets more
### When deep learning helps with tables
• Very large datasets
• Mixing tables with text, images or sequences
• Transfer from foundation models trained on many tables, an active research area

!! Deep learning is always the most advanced choice => The best model is the one that performs best on your data and constraints

# Start with a baseline
A baseline is the simplest reasonable prediction. It sets the bar any model must clear to justify its complexity and cost.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Forecasting", "b": "same as last week, or seasonal naive"}, {"a": "Classification", "b": "always predict the most common class"}, {"a": "Regression", "b": "predict the average"}, {"a": "Ranking", "b": "most popular items"}]}
### The accuracy trap
In fraud detection, if 99.5% of transactions are legitimate, a model that always says "legitimate" scores 99.5% accuracy while catching zero fraud. Always compare with the baseline and use metrics suited to the problem.

### Business baselines
The real baseline is often the current human or rule-based process. A model must beat it on the metric that matters (cost, time, risk), including the cost of running and maintaining it.

== ai-nets

# Layers
A feed-forward neural network passes data through layers of neurons. Each layer applies a matrix multiplication (weights), adds biases and applies an activation function. The output of one layer is the input to the next.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Output layer: prediction", "Hidden layer 3", "Hidden layer 2", "Hidden layer 1", "Input layer: raw features"]}
### Width and depth
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Wider layers", "items": ["more neurons per layer", "more capacity per step"]}, "r": {"t": "Deeper networks", "items": ["more layers", "more abstraction and composition"]}}
### Why depth helps
Deep networks can represent complex functions much more efficiently than shallow ones, by building concepts hierarchically. But depth made training hard until techniques like ReLU, better initialisation, batch normalisation and residual connections arrived around 2010–2015.
### Residual connections
ResNet (2015) added "skip connections" that let information bypass layers, enabling networks with 100+ layers. Transformers use them too.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Input", "Layer", "Add the original input back", "Next layer"]}
!? The 2012 AlexNet, with 8 layers and 60 million parameters, kicked off the deep learning boom by winning ImageNet by a wide margin.

# Each layer learns more abstract features
Visualising trained image networks shows a hierarchy: edges and colour blobs in the first layers, textures and simple shapes next, then parts (eyes, wheels) and finally whole objects.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Layer 5+: objects, faces", "Layer 4: parts, eyes, wheels", "Layer 3: patterns, textures", "Layer 2: corners, shapes", "Layer 1: edges, colours"]}
### Similar in language models
Research on language models finds early layers capture word pieces and syntax, middle layers capture meanings and relationships, and later layers prepare the next-token prediction.
### Interpretability research
Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse-engineer what individual neurons and circuits do. Researchers have found "features" for concepts such as specific places, code bugs or emotions inside large models.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Early layers", "items": ["generic", "reusable across tasks"]}, "r": {"t": "Later layers", "items": ["task-specific", "replaced in fine-tuning"]}}
### Transfer learning
Because early layers learn generic features, a network trained on millions of photos can be fine-tuned for X-rays or crop disease with far less data.
> Nobody programs these features: they emerge because they help reduce the training loss.

# Activation functions
Activation functions introduce non-linearity, letting networks model curved, complex relationships. Without them, any number of layers would equal a single linear transformation.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sigmoid", "b": "squashes to 0–1, saturates"}, {"a": "Tanh", "b": "squashes to −1 to 1"}, {"a": "ReLU", "b": "max(0, x), fast and simple"}, {"a": "GELU and SwiGLU", "b": "smooth variants in modern transformers"}]}
### Why ReLU took over
Sigmoid and tanh flatten out for large inputs, so gradients become tiny and deep networks stop learning (the "vanishing gradient" problem). ReLU keeps gradients alive for positive values and is cheap to compute.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sigmoid", "items": ["smooth", "vanishing gradients in deep nets"]}, "r": {"t": "ReLU", "items": ["simple", "trains deep nets well"]}}
### Output activations
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Softmax", "b": "probabilities across many classes"}, {"a": "Sigmoid", "b": "probability for yes/no"}, {"a": "None, linear", "b": "predicting a number"}]}
!? ReLU's simplicity, just setting negatives to zero, was a surprisingly important ingredient in making deep learning practical.

# The loss function
The loss turns "how wrong is the model?" into a single number to minimise. Choosing it defines what the model optimises for, so it encodes business priorities.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Mean squared error", "b": "regression, punishes big errors heavily"}, {"a": "Mean absolute error", "b": "regression, robust to outliers"}, {"a": "Cross-entropy", "b": "classification, punishes confident wrong answers"}, {"a": "Custom losses", "b": "reflect business costs"}]}
### Cross-entropy explained
If the correct class gets probability 0.9, the loss is small (−log 0.9 ≈ 0.1). If it gets 0.01, the loss is large (−log 0.01 ≈ 4.6). Confident mistakes are punished hardest.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "loss when true class gets probability p", "items": [{"l": "p = 0.9", "v": 0.1}, {"l": "p = 0.5", "v": 0.7}, {"l": "p = 0.1", "v": 2.3}, {"l": "p = 0.01", "v": 4.6, "hi": true}]}
### Language models
LLMs are trained with cross-entropy on the next token: minimising how surprised the model is by the actual next word across trillions of examples.
### Business alignment
If under-forecasting stock costs more than over-forecasting, use an asymmetric loss (like quantile loss) instead of plain squared error.
> Whatever you choose to minimise, the model will minimise; choose carefully.

# Backpropagation
Backpropagation applies the chain rule to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight in one backward sweep. Its cost is roughly the same as a couple of forward passes, which makes training billions of weights feasible.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Forward pass: compute outputs and loss", "Backward pass: propagate error gradients", "Each weight gets its gradient", "Optimiser updates weights"]}
### History
The idea appeared in several forms (Linnainmaa 1970, Werbos 1974), but Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams' 1986 paper showed it could train multilayer networks to learn useful internal representations.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1970", "l": "automatic differentiation"}, {"y": "1974", "l": "Werbos' thesis"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams"}, {"y": "2012", "l": "deep learning boom"}]}
### Automatic differentiation
Frameworks like PyTorch and JAX compute gradients automatically: developers write the forward computation, and the framework derives the backward pass.
### Vanishing and exploding gradients
In very deep networks, gradients can shrink to nothing or blow up as they pass back through layers. ReLU, careful initialisation, normalisation and residual connections tame this.
!? Geoffrey Hinton shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield for foundational work on neural networks.

# Learning rate
The learning rate sets the size of each weight update. It's often the single most important hyperparameter to tune.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too low", "items": ["very slow", "may get stuck"]}, "r": {"t": "Too high", "items": ["unstable", "loss jumps or explodes"]}}
### Schedules
Training typically uses a warm-up (start small, increase), then gradually decreases the learning rate, for example with cosine decay. Big steps early explore; small steps late fine-tune.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "learning rate over training (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Warm-up", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Peak", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Midway", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "End", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}]}
### Adaptive optimisers
Adam (2014) and AdamW adapt the effective step size for each weight based on recent gradients, making training more robust to the choice of learning rate.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "SGD", "b": "simple, needs careful tuning"}, {"a": "SGD with momentum", "b": "smooths the path"}, {"a": "Adam", "b": "adaptive, widely used"}, {"a": "AdamW", "b": "Adam with better weight decay, standard for transformers"}]}
> Practitioners often run a quick "learning rate finder" sweep before full training.

# Batches and epochs
Computing the gradient over all data at once is too slow; using one example at a time is too noisy. Mini-batches, typically 32 to thousands of examples, balance accuracy and speed, and fit GPU memory.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "steps per epoch = dataset size ÷ batch size", "items": [{"s": "", "m": "100,000 examples ÷ 32 = 3,125 steps"}]}
### Terms
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Batch", "b": "examples per update"}, {"a": "Step", "b": "one weight update"}, {"a": "Epoch", "b": "one pass through all training data"}, {"a": "Iteration", "b": "often same as step"}]}
### Large models are different
Large language models often see each training token only about once: they train on so much data that a full second epoch isn't needed or would cause memorisation.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Small models", "items": ["many epochs over the same data"]}, "r": {"t": "Large LLMs", "items": ["about one pass over trillions of tokens"]}}
### Batch size effects
• Larger batches: smoother gradients, better hardware use, may need higher learning rates
• Smaller batches: noisier updates that can help generalisation
!? Training frontier models uses batches of millions of tokens, spread across thousands of GPUs working in parallel.

# Dropout and regularisation
Regularisation discourages a model from fitting noise, improving generalisation. Dropout, introduced by Hinton's group in 2012–14, randomly zeroes a fraction of neurons during each training step, so the network can't rely on any single pathway.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["During training: randomly drop 10–50% of neurons", "Network learns redundant patterns", "At prediction: use all neurons"]}
### Other techniques
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "L2 weight decay", "b": "penalise large weights"}, {"a": "Early stopping", "b": "stop when validation stops improving"}, {"a": "Data augmentation", "b": "create varied versions of examples"}, {"a": "Batch or layer normalisation", "b": "stabilise training, mild regularising effect"}]}
### Data augmentation for images
Flip, rotate, crop, change brightness or add noise to images. The model learns that a cat is still a cat in any of these variations.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Flip", "Rotate", "Crop", "Colour shift", "Add noise"]}
### Why it works
Dropout is like training a huge ensemble of thinned networks that share weights; at test time, using all neurons approximates averaging them.
> Regularisation trades a little training accuracy for much better performance on new data.

# Convolutions in one picture
A convolutional layer slides small learnable filters across an image. Each filter computes a weighted sum over a small patch, producing a "feature map" showing where its pattern appears. Using the same filter everywhere means far fewer parameters and translation-invariant detection.

### Landmark models

### Still widely used
CNNs remain efficient for many vision tasks on phones and edge devices, such as defect detection on production lines and medical image analysis, though transformers now compete.
!? Yann LeCun's LeNet was used by US banks in the 1990s to read handwritten digits on millions of cheques.

# Why GPUs?
Neural network training is dominated by matrix multiplications: huge numbers of independent multiply-and-add operations. CPUs have a few powerful cores; GPUs have thousands of simpler cores that do these in parallel.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "CPU", "items": ["few powerful cores", "flexible", "sequential tasks"]}, "r": {"t": "GPU", "items": ["thousands of cores", "parallel maths", "AI and graphics"]}}
### History
In 2009–2012, researchers showed GPUs could speed up neural network training tens of times. AlexNet (2012) was trained on two gaming GPUs. NVIDIA's CUDA software made programming GPUs practical.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2007", "l": "CUDA released"}, {"y": "2012", "l": "AlexNet on 2 GPUs"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "tensor cores"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "AI data centres with tens of thousands of GPUs"}]}
### Specialised chips
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "NVIDIA GPUs", "b": "dominant for training"}, {"a": "Google TPUs", "b": "custom AI accelerators"}, {"a": "AWS Trainium and Inferentia", "b": "cloud AI chips"}, {"a": "Phone NPUs", "b": "on-device AI"}]}
### Memory is the bottleneck
Modern AI chips need extremely fast memory (HBM) to feed their cores; memory bandwidth often limits speed more than raw compute.
!? Training a frontier model can require tens of thousands of GPUs running for months, consuming as much electricity as a small town.

== ai-metrics

# Accuracy can lie
Accuracy is the share of predictions that are correct. With imbalanced classes, it rewards ignoring the rare class. Fraud, defects, diseases and churn are all rare-event problems.
ex: 10,000 transactions, 100 fraud | Model always says "OK" | Accuracy = 9,900 ÷ 10,000 = 99%, fraud caught = 0
### Better choices for rare events
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Precision", "b": "how trustworthy are alerts?"}, {"a": "Recall", "b": "how many real cases are caught?"}, {"a": "PR-AUC", "b": "overall quality across thresholds"}, {"a": "Cost-weighted metrics", "b": "errors priced in euros"}]}
### Balanced accuracy
Balanced accuracy averages the recall of each class, so a model that ignores the rare class scores only 50%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "score, % (always-OK model)", "items": [{"l": "Accuracy", "v": 99.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Balanced accuracy", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Fraud recall", "v": 0.0}]}
!! 99% accurate means excellent => With rare events, 99% can mean useless
> Always ask: what does a naive model score on this metric?

# The confusion matrix
A confusion matrix tabulates predictions against reality. For a binary problem it has four cells, and nearly every classification metric is derived from them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Predicted yes", "items": ["true positive, caught", "false positive, false alarm"]}, "r": {"t": "Predicted no", "items": ["false negative, missed", "true negative, correctly cleared"]}}
### Worked example
A fraud model reviews 10,000 transactions with 100 real frauds. It flags 150: 80 real frauds and 70 false alarms.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "True positives", "b": "80"}, {"a": "False positives", "b": "70"}, {"a": "False negatives", "b": "20"}, {"a": "True negatives", "b": "9,830"}]}
### Costs differ
Each cell has a business cost or benefit. A missed fraud might cost €500; a false alarm costs a phone call and an annoyed customer, say €5.
ex: Missed fraud: 20 × €500 = €10,000 | False alarms: 70 × €5 = €350 | Total error cost: €10,350
### Multi-class
With many classes, the matrix grows to show which classes are confused with which: e.g. a document classifier mixing up "invoice" and "credit note".
> Look at the confusion matrix before any single summary number.

# Precision
Precision answers: when the model raises an alert, how often is it right? It matters when false alarms are expensive or erode trust.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "precision = TP / (TP + FP)", "items": [{"s": "TP", "m": "true positives"}, {"s": "FP", "m": "false positives"}, {"s": "example", "m": "80 / (80 + 70) = 53%"}]}
### When precision matters most
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Spam filter", "b": "losing real emails is costly"}, {"a": "Legal or HR flags", "b": "false accusations are harmful"}, {"a": "Automated actions", "b": "acting wrongly costs money"}, {"a": "Alert fatigue", "b": "too many false alarms get ignored"}]}
### Alert fatigue
Security and monitoring teams faced with mostly false alarms start ignoring alerts, including real ones. Raising precision can make a system more useful even if it catches slightly less.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "precision, %", "items": [{"l": "Noisy alert system", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Tuned system", "v": 60.0, "hi": true}]}
!? In medical screening, precision is called positive predictive value: for rare diseases, even accurate tests can produce more false positives than true ones.

# Recall
Recall, also called sensitivity, answers: of all the real cases, how many did we catch? It matters when misses are dangerous or costly.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "recall = TP / (TP + FN)", "items": [{"s": "TP", "m": "true positives"}, {"s": "FN", "m": "false negatives"}, {"s": "example", "m": "80 / (80 + 20) = 80%"}]}
### When recall matters most
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Cancer screening", "b": "a miss can be fatal"}, {"a": "Safety defects", "b": "a missed fault can cause accidents"}, {"a": "Sanctions screening", "b": "missing a sanctioned party is illegal"}, {"a": "Fraud", "b": "misses cost money directly"}]}
### Two-stage systems
A common design uses a high-recall first stage to catch almost everything, then a high-precision second stage (another model or humans) to filter false alarms.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Stage 1: high recall, flag broadly", "Stage 2: precise model or human review", "Final decision"]}
### Specificity
The counterpart for negatives is specificity: of all genuine negatives, how many were correctly cleared? Medical tests usually report sensitivity and specificity together.
> Recall without precision floods teams with work; precision without recall misses the problem. You need both in balance.

# The precision–recall trade-off
Most classifiers output a score; you choose a threshold. Moving it trades precision for recall. Plotting the two across thresholds gives a precision–recall curve.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Threshold 0.9", "items": ["precision 90%", "recall 40%"]}, "r": {"t": "Threshold 0.5", "items": ["precision 60%", "recall 80%"]}}
### Choosing the threshold
Pick the threshold that minimises expected cost or meets a constraint ("catch at least 90% of fraud" or "no more than 50 alerts a day for the team").
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Score all cases", "Try thresholds", "Compute cost and workload", "Pick the business optimum"]}
### Capacity matters
If a compliance team can review 200 cases a day, the threshold should produce about 200 alerts, ranked by score, regardless of the abstract "best" F1.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low threshold", "items": ["high recall", "many alerts", "more review work"]}, "r": {"t": "High threshold", "items": ["high precision", "fewer alerts", "more misses"]}}
### ROC curves
ROC curves plot recall against the false-positive rate. For very imbalanced data, precision–recall curves are usually more informative.
!? The threshold is a business decision, not a technical one; data scientists should present the options, and owners should choose.

# F1 score
F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Unlike a simple average, it's dragged down by whichever is lower, so a model can't score well by excelling at one and failing at the other.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F1 = 2 × P × R / (P + R)", "items": [{"s": "P", "m": "precision"}, {"s": "R", "m": "recall"}, {"s": "P = 0.53, R = 0.8", "m": "F1 ≈ 0.64"}]}
### Harmonic vs simple average
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "score", "items": [{"l": "Precision", "v": 0.95}, {"l": "Recall", "v": 0.1}, {"l": "Simple average", "v": 0.53}, {"l": "F1", "v": 0.18, "hi": true}]}
### Variants
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "F2", "b": "weights recall more"}, {"a": "F0.5", "b": "weights precision more"}, {"a": "Macro F1", "b": "averages across classes equally"}, {"a": "Micro F1", "b": "pools all decisions together"}]}
### Limits
F1 ignores true negatives and treats both error types as equally bad. If false negatives cost 100 times more than false positives, a cost-based metric is better.
> F1 is a handy summary for comparing models, but business decisions need costs.

# Errors for numbers
Regression and forecasting use error metrics measured in the target's units or percentages.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "MAE", "b": "average absolute error, easy to explain"}, {"a": "RMSE", "b": "squares errors, punishes big misses"}, {"a": "MAPE", "b": "average percentage error"}, {"a": "Bias", "b": "average signed error, systematic over- or under-prediction"}]}
### Example
Actual sales: 100, 120, 80. Forecasts: 110, 100, 90. Errors: 10, −20, 10.
ex: MAE = (10 + 20 + 10) ÷ 3 ≈ 13.3 | RMSE = √((100 + 400 + 100) ÷ 3) ≈ 14.1 | Bias = (10 − 20 + 10) ÷ 3 = 0
### MAPE's trap
MAPE explodes when actual values are near zero (selling 1 unit vs forecasting 3 is a 200% error). Weighted MAPE (WAPE) or MAE are often more robust for intermittent demand.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "MAPE", "items": ["intuitive %", "unstable near zero"]}, "r": {"t": "WAPE", "items": ["total error ÷ total actual", "robust"]}}
### Bias matters
A forecast can have low average error but systematically under-predict, causing chronic stock-outs. Track bias separately.
!? In supply chains, forecast accuracy is often reported as 1 − WAPE, e.g. "85% forecast accuracy".

# Compare against a baseline
A metric only has meaning in comparison. The relevant comparisons are a naive baseline, the current process and the cost of being wrong.

### Forecast value added

# An image is a grid of numbers
A colour image is stored as three grids (channels) of brightness values, one each for red, green and blue. Each value typically ranges from 0 to 255 (8 bits).

### Preprocessing
Vision models usually resize images to a fixed size (e.g. 224×224 or 512×512) and normalise values to small ranges. Higher resolution improves detail but costs more compute.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Resize", "b": "fixed input size"}, {"a": "Normalise", "b": "scale values, e.g. 0–1"}, {"a": "Augment", "b": "flip, crop, recolour for training"}, {"a": "Batch", "b": "process many images at once"}]}
### Beyond RGB

• Depth cameras: distance per pixel
• Satellite imagery: dozens of spectral bands beyond visible light

# Classification
Image classification assigns one or more labels to a whole image. The network's last layer produces a score per class; softmax converts scores to probabilities that sum to 100%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "probability, % (example)", "items": [{"l": "Cat", "v": 87.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Fox", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Dog", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Other", "v": 1.0}]}
### Top-1 and top-5
ImageNet results are reported as top-1 accuracy (correct class is the top guess) and top-5 (correct class in the top five). Modern models exceed 90% top-1.
### Business uses
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Quality control: pass or fail", "Crop disease identification", "Document type sorting", "Product categorisation", "Medical image triage"]}
### Open-vocabulary models
Models like CLIP (2021) learn from image–caption pairs, so you can classify into new categories just by describing them in words, without retraining.

# Object detection
Object detection outputs a set of bounding boxes, each with a class label and confidence. Detectors must handle many objects of different sizes, partially hidden or overlapping.

### Popular models

Each box has a confidence. Home camera systems like Frigate let you set minimum scores per object type, and "zones" so that only a person in the driveway triggers an alert.

# When vision fails
Vision models learn from training images; anything outside that range is risky. Failures can be subtle and surprising.

== ai-speech

# Sound as a picture
Sound is a pressure wave. A microphone measures it thousands of times per second. A spectrogram shows, for each short time slice, how much energy each frequency has, turning sound into an image.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sound wave", "Sampled by microphone", "Short-time Fourier transform", "Spectrogram", "Model"]}
### Mel spectrograms
Human hearing is more sensitive to differences in low frequencies. Mel spectrograms space frequencies the way we perceive them, and are the standard input for speech models.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Waveform", "b": "pressure over time"}, {"a": "Spectrogram", "b": "frequency over time"}, {"a": "Mel spectrogram", "b": "perceptual frequency scale"}, {"a": "Features", "b": "what the model learns from"}]}
### Reading a spectrogram
Vowels show bands of energy called formants; "s" sounds appear as high-frequency noise; silence is dark. Trained phoneticians can partly "read" speech from spectrograms.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Vowels", "items": ["horizontal bands"]}, "r": {"t": "Consonants like \"s\"", "items": ["high-frequency hiss"]}}
!? Some modern models skip spectrograms and learn directly from raw waveforms, but spectrograms remain common.

# Sampling rates
By the Nyquist–Shannon theorem, to capture a frequency you must sample at least twice that rate. Human hearing tops out around 20 kHz, so 44.1 kHz captures it all.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "samples per second, thousands", "items": [{"l": "Phone call", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Wideband voice", "v": 16.0}, {"l": "CD audio", "v": 44.1}, {"l": "Studio", "v": 96.0, "hi": true}]}
### Bit depth
Each sample also has a precision: 16 bits gives 65,536 levels (CD), 24 bits for studio work. Higher bit depth means a wider dynamic range between quiet and loud.
### Speech models
Most speech recognition models use 16 kHz audio: enough for intelligibility while keeping data small.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "8 kHz", "b": "telephone, muffled \"s\" sounds"}, {"a": "16 kHz", "b": "speech AI standard"}, {"a": "44.1 kHz", "b": "music, full hearing range"}, {"a": "48 kHz", "b": "video standard"}]}
> Higher sampling rates mostly matter for music and audio production, not speech AI.

# Speech to text
Modern automatic speech recognition (ASR) uses end-to-end neural networks: audio in, text out. Transformer-based models like Whisper learn from huge, diverse datasets, making them robust to accents, noise and technical vocabulary.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Audio", "Spectrogram", "Encoder", "Decoder writes text", "Punctuation and formatting"]}
### Measuring accuracy
ASR is scored by word error rate (WER): substitutions, deletions and insertions divided by the number of words. Clean English can be under 5%; noisy calls or rare languages are higher.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "WER = (S + D + I) / N", "items": [{"s": "S", "m": "substituted words"}, {"s": "D", "m": "deleted"}, {"s": "I", "m": "inserted"}, {"s": "N", "m": "words spoken"}]}
### Practical tips
• Custom vocabularies help with names and jargon
• Speaker microphones matter more than model tweaks
• Portuguese from Portugal and Brazil differ; check models handle European Portuguese well
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "word error rate, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Clean speech", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Phone call", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Noisy factory", "v": 25.0, "hi": true}]}
!? Whisper was trained on 680,000 hours of audio, roughly 77 years of continuous listening.

# Collaborative filtering
Collaborative filtering uses the pattern of interactions across all users. If you and another user liked many of the same items, items they liked that you haven't seen are good candidates.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "User-based", "items": ["find similar users", "recommend their items"]}, "r": {"t": "Item-based", "items": ["find items liked by the same users", "\"also bought\""]}}
### Matrix factorisation
Think of a giant table of users × items, mostly empty. Matrix factorisation fills it in by learning a small vector for each user and each item, so that their dot product predicts interest.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sparse user–item table", "Learn user and item vectors", "Multiply to predict missing entries", "Recommend highest"]}
### The Netflix Prize
In 2006 Netflix offered $1 million for a 10% improvement in its rating predictions. A team won in 2009, largely with matrix factorisation and ensembles. Netflix never fully deployed the winning blend because of its complexity.

# Content-based filtering
Content-based methods use item attributes and match them to a user profile built from what they've liked. They work for new items immediately, since attributes are known from day one.

# The cold-start problem
Cold start affects new users, new items and new platforms. Without interaction data, collaborative methods can't personalise.

### Bandits
Multi-armed bandit algorithms balance showing proven items (exploit) with testing new ones (explore), learning quickly which new items people like.

# Implicit vs explicit feedback
Explicit feedback is deliberate: ratings, likes, reviews. Implicit feedback is inferred from behaviour. Implicit data is abundant but noisy: a click isn't necessarily satisfaction.

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Watched to the end", "b": "strong positive"}, {"a": "Skipped after 5 seconds", "b": "negative"}, {"a": "Added to basket, not bought", "b": "mild interest"}, {"a": "Returned the item", "b": "negative"}]}

# Embeddings for items
Embeddings place users and items in the same vector space. A user's vector reflects their tastes; recommending is finding items whose vectors point in a similar direction.

== ai-rl

# Agent, environment, reward
Reinforcement learning is formalised as a Markov decision process: at each step the agent observes a state, picks an action, receives a reward and lands in a new state. The goal is a policy that maximises the expected sum of (discounted) future rewards.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["State", "Action", "Reward", "Next state"]}
### Key terms
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Policy", "b": "the agent's strategy: which action in which state"}, {"a": "Value", "b": "expected future reward from a state"}, {"a": "Discount factor", "b": "how much future rewards count today"}, {"a": "Episode", "b": "one run from start to finish"}]}
### A thermostat example
An RL thermostat could observe room temperature and time of day, choose heating levels, and receive a reward for comfort minus energy cost. Over weeks it learns when to pre-heat.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Observe temperature and time", "Choose heating level", "Reward: comfort − cost", "Improve policy"]}
### Model-free vs model-based
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Model-free", "items": ["learns directly from trial and error", "simple", "data-hungry"]}, "r": {"t": "Model-based", "items": ["learns how the world works, then plans", "efficient", "harder"]}}
!? The discount factor reflects impatience: with 0.99, a reward 100 steps away is worth about 37% of an immediate one.

# Exploration vs exploitation
The dilemma: exploit the best option known so far, or explore alternatives that might be better. Pure exploitation can lock in a mediocre choice; pure exploration wastes effort.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Exploit", "items": ["use the best known", "reliable now"]}, "r": {"t": "Explore", "items": ["try alternatives", "learn for later"]}}
### Strategies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "ε-greedy", "b": "usually exploit, explore randomly e.g. 10% of the time"}, {"a": "Optimism", "b": "assume untried options are good until proven otherwise"}, {"a": "Upper confidence bound", "b": "favour options with high potential"}, {"a": "Thompson sampling", "b": "sample from beliefs about each option"}]}
### Everyday versions
• Choosing a restaurant: your favourite or somewhere new?
• A/B testing ads: show the winner or keep testing?
• Clinical trials: give the promising drug or keep comparing?
### Multi-armed bandits
The simplest RL setting: several slot machines with unknown payouts. Bandit algorithms are widely used for website optimisation and recommendations because they learn while earning.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of traffic after learning (example), %", "items": [{"l": "Best ad", "v": 80.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Second", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "Others", "v": 5.0}]}
> Explore early, when there's lots to learn and plenty of time to benefit; exploit more later.

# Delayed rewards
The credit-assignment problem: when a reward arrives after many actions, which ones deserve credit? In chess, a winning move 40 turns earlier may have set up the victory.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Move 5", "l": "quiet positional move"}, {"y": "Move 20", "l": "exchange"}, {"y": "Move 40", "l": "checkmate"}, {"y": "Reward", "l": "+1 at the end"}]}
### Temporal difference learning
TD learning, developed by Richard Sutton in the 1980s, updates value estimates step by step using the difference between successive predictions, so credit flows back from the reward to earlier states over many games.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Predict value now", "Take action", "See next state's value", "Adjust earlier prediction towards it"]}
### Reward shaping
Designers sometimes add intermediate rewards ("progress towards the goal") to speed up learning, but shaped rewards can accidentally change what behaviour is optimal.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sparse reward", "items": ["only at the goal", "correct but slow"]}, "r": {"t": "Shaped reward", "items": ["frequent hints", "faster but risky"]}}
!? Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto received the 2024 Turing Award for founding modern reinforcement learning.

# AlphaGo
Go's huge branching factor (about 250 legal moves per turn versus about 35 in chess) made brute-force search hopeless. AlphaGo combined deep neural networks, trained on human games then improved by self-play, with Monte Carlo tree search.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "10¹⁷⁰", "l": "legal Go positions"}, {"v": "~250", "l": "moves per turn"}, {"v": "4–1", "l": "score vs Lee Sedol, 2016"}]}
### Move 37
In game two against Lee Sedol, AlphaGo played move 37, a shoulder hit on the fifth line that commentators initially thought was a mistake. It proved brilliant, and became a symbol of machines finding genuinely new ideas.
### Successors
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2016", "l": "AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "AlphaGo Zero, no human games"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "AlphaZero, chess, shogi and Go"}, {"y": "2019", "l": "MuZero learns the rules too"}]}
### Beyond games
The same team applied similar ideas to protein folding (AlphaFold), faster matrix multiplication algorithms (AlphaTensor) and sorting routines.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Policy network", "b": "suggests promising moves"}, {"a": "Value network", "b": "estimates who's winning"}, {"a": "Tree search", "b": "looks ahead"}, {"a": "Self-play", "b": "generates training games"}]}
!? Lee Sedol's one win, in game four, came from a creative "divine move" of his own, move 78, which confused AlphaGo. He retired in 2019, saying AI "cannot be defeated".

# Self-play
In self-play, an agent trains against copies of itself. As it improves, its opponents improve too, creating an automatic curriculum of ever-harder challenges.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Play against past versions", "Learn from wins and losses", "New, stronger version", "Add to opponent pool"]}
### Results
AlphaZero reached superhuman strength in chess in about four hours of self-play from only the rules, rediscovering openings humans took centuries to develop, and some they never found.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "hours of self-play to beat top programs", "items": [{"l": "Chess", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Shogi", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Go", "v": 30.0, "hi": true}]}
### Risks of self-play
Agents can develop strategies that only work against themselves. Keeping a diverse pool of past opponents prevents "forgetting" how to beat older styles.
### Language models too
Self-play ideas appear in LLM training: models generate problems and solutions, check them (e.g. with code tests) and learn from successful attempts.
> Self-play works best where outcomes are unambiguous: games, maths, code.

# Simulation first
Real-world trial and error is slow, expensive and dangerous. Simulators let agents practise millions of times faster, in parallel, with no broken robots.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Build a simulator", "Train agent in simulation", "Randomise conditions", "Transfer to the real world", "Fine-tune"]}
### The sim-to-real gap
Simulations never match reality exactly: friction, lighting, sensor noise differ. Domain randomisation, varying physics and visuals wildly during training, makes policies robust enough to transfer.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simulation", "items": ["fast", "safe", "cheap", "imperfect"]}, "r": {"t": "Real world", "items": ["slow", "risky", "costly", "ground truth"]}}
### Examples
• OpenAI's robot hand learned to manipulate a Rubik's Cube in simulation (2019)
• Quadruped robots learn to walk on rough terrain in simulation before deployment
• Autonomous driving systems test billions of simulated kilometres
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Robot arms", "b": "grasping"}, {"a": "Legged robots", "b": "walking"}, {"a": "Drones", "b": "racing and navigation"}, {"a": "Cars", "b": "rare dangerous scenarios"}]}
!? Digital twins of factories and supply chains let companies test policies in simulation before changing real operations.

# Reward hacking
Reward hacking (or specification gaming) is when an agent achieves high reward in ways that violate the designer's intent. It's a central concern for AI safety.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Famous examples", "items": ["Boat race: loops collecting points", "Tetris agent: pauses forever to avoid losing", "Simulated robot: exploits physics glitches", "Grasping arm: hides the object from the camera"]}
### Goodhart's law
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Organisations face the same problem when staff optimise a KPI rather than the goal behind it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Intended goal", "items": ["win the race"]}, "r": {"t": "Rewarded proxy", "items": ["points from targets", "agent loops forever"]}}
### In language models
LLMs trained to please raters can learn to sound confident, flatter users or write longer answers, because raters rewarded those traits. Coding models can learn to make tests pass in unintended ways.
### Mitigations
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Better reward design", "Multiple metrics", "Human oversight and audits", "Penalties for side effects", "Red-teaming for exploits"]}
> Ask of every metric: how could an agent score high while doing the wrong thing?

# RL in the real world
Real-world RL needs safe exploration, reliable simulators or careful offline learning from logged data. It's used where decisions repeat often and outcomes are measurable.

### Offline RL
Offline RL learns from historical logs without new experimentation, useful in healthcare, finance and industry where exploring freely is unsafe.

# RL from human feedback
RLHF turns human preferences into a training signal. Instead of writing a reward function for "helpful", people compare model responses, and a reward model learns to predict which they'd prefer.

### Why comparisons?
Humans are inconsistent at giving absolute scores but much better at saying which of two answers is better. Comparisons yield more reliable data.

# Can machines think?
In his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", Alan Turing sidestepped the vague question "can machines think?" with a practical test: a human judge chats by text with a hidden person and a hidden machine. If the judge can't reliably tell them apart, the machine passes.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1936", "l": "Turing machine"}, {"y": "1950", "l": "imitation game paper"}, {"y": "1966", "l": "ELIZA chatbot"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "claims of a \"pass\" disputed"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "LLMs fool many people in short chats"}]}
### Turing's predictions
Turing predicted that by about 2000, machines would fool average judges 30% of the time after five minutes. He also anticipated objections, from theological to "machines can only do what we tell them".
### ELIZA
Joseph Weizenbaum's 1966 program ELIZA mimicked a therapist with simple pattern matching ("Tell me more about your mother"). People formed emotional attachments to it, which alarmed Weizenbaum.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "ELIZA, 1966", "items": ["pattern matching", "no understanding", "still persuasive"]}, "r": {"t": "Modern LLMs", "items": ["learned from vast text", "broad capabilities"]}}
### Is the test still useful?
Modern chatbots can pass casual versions of the test, yet many argue that imitating conversation isn't the same as general intelligence. Benchmarks now test reasoning, knowledge and real tasks instead.
!? Turing was a key codebreaker at Bletchley Park in the Second World War; the "Turing Award", computing's highest honour, is named after him.

# Dartmouth, 1956
John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon proposed a summer research project at Dartmouth College. McCarthy coined "artificial intelligence" in the 1955 proposal.

### The bold claim
The proposal stated that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it", and expected "a significant advance" in one summer.
### Early successes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Logic Theorist, 1956", "b": "proved mathematical theorems"}, {"a": "General Problem Solver, 1957", "b": "solved puzzles"}, {"a": "Checkers program, 1959", "b": "learned to beat its creator"}, {"a": "LISP, 1958", "b": "AI programming language"}]}
### Optimism
In 1965 Herbert Simon predicted machines would do "any work a man can do" within 20 years. Early successes on puzzles didn't transfer to messy real-world tasks.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Expected", "items": ["human-level AI in a generation"]}, "r": {"t": "Reality", "items": ["decades of slow, uneven progress"]}}
> Arthur Samuel's checkers program at IBM popularised the term "machine learning" in 1959.

# Symbolic AI
Symbolic AI, nicknamed GOFAI ("good old-fashioned AI"), represents knowledge as symbols and rules and reasons with logic: search, planning, theorem proving.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Symbolic AI", "items": ["explicit rules", "explainable", "brittle with messy data"]}, "r": {"t": "Neural AI", "items": ["learned patterns", "handles messy data", "harder to explain"]}}
### Strengths
• Precise reasoning and guarantees
• Transparent: you can read the rules
• Works with little data when experts can state the logic
### Moravec's paradox
Hans Moravec observed in the 1980s that tasks hard for humans (chess, algebra) were easy for computers, while tasks easy for a toddler (seeing, walking, grasping) were extremely hard.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hard for humans, easy for computers", "b": "chess, calculation"}, {"a": "Easy for humans, hard for computers", "b": "vision, walking, common sense"}]}
### Still alive
Symbolic methods power route planning, scheduling, tax engines, compilers and verification. "Neuro-symbolic" research combines neural networks with logic, and LLMs calling tools like calculators or solvers is a practical version.
!? Deep Blue, which beat Kasparov at chess in 1997, relied on search and hand-tuned evaluation, not machine learning in the modern sense.

# The AI winters
AI winters were periods of reduced funding and interest after expectations outran results.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1966", "l": "ALPAC report cools machine translation"}, {"y": "1973", "l": "Lighthill report in the UK"}, {"y": "1974–80", "l": "first winter"}, {"y": "1987", "l": "expert-system market collapses"}, {"y": "1987–93", "l": "second winter"}]}
### Causes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Overpromising", "Limited computing power", "Too little data", "Brittle systems", "Specialised hardware made obsolete"]}
### The first winter
The Lighthill report (1973) told the UK government AI had failed to deliver on its "grandiose objectives"; funding was cut. In the US, DARPA shifted to more applied projects.
### The second winter
The collapse of the specialised LISP-machine market in 1987 and the high upkeep of expert systems led to another downturn. Many researchers rebranded their work as "machine learning" or "informatics" to avoid the AI label.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hype phase", "items": ["big promises", "funding flows"]}, "r": {"t": "Winter", "items": ["disappointment", "funding cut"]}}
> Today's boom differs in having enormous commercial revenue, but the lesson of matching promises to evidence still applies.

# Expert systems
Expert systems encoded specialists' knowledge as if-then rules, with an inference engine to chain them. They were the first commercially successful form of AI.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Interview experts", "Write rules", "Inference engine applies them", "Recommendation with explanation"]}
### Famous systems
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "MYCIN, 1970s", "b": "diagnosed blood infections, rivalled specialists"}, {"a": "XCON, 1980", "b": "configured DEC computers, saved millions a year"}, {"a": "DENDRAL, 1965", "b": "identified chemical structures"}, {"a": "PROSPECTOR", "b": "mineral exploration"}]}
### Why they faded
• **Knowledge acquisition bottleneck**: extracting and writing rules was slow and costly
• **Brittleness**: unexpected situations broke them
• **Maintenance**: thousands of interacting rules became unmanageable; XCON grew to over 10,000 rules
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "rules in XCON (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1980", "v": 750.0}, {"l": "1984", "v": 3000.0}, {"l": "Late 1980s", "v": 10000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Their legacy
Business rules engines, configuration tools and clinical decision support descend from expert systems. Today, LLMs are often combined with rules for compliance-critical decisions.
!? MYCIN was never used clinically, partly because of legal and ethical questions about who was responsible for its advice.

# The statistical turn
From the late 1980s, AI shifted from hand-written knowledge to probability and learning from data. IBM's speech team leader Frederick Jelinek reportedly quipped, "Every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recogniser goes up."

# Training vs inference
Training builds the model; inference runs it. Over a model's life, inference often consumes more total compute than training because it runs billions of times.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Training", "items": ["once or periodically", "huge clusters", "weeks to months", "high cost upfront"]}, "r": {"t": "Inference", "items": ["every request", "spread out", "milliseconds to seconds", "cost grows with usage"]}}
### Hardware needs differ
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Training", "b": "high-bandwidth interconnects, many GPUs in sync"}, {"a": "Inference", "b": "low latency, cost per token, energy efficiency"}, {"a": "Edge inference", "b": "small, low-power chips"}, {"a": "Batch inference", "b": "throughput over latency"}]}
### Costs in practice
For companies using AI through APIs, inference costs dominate. Choosing smaller models, caching repeated prompts and batching requests can cut bills dramatically.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Request", "Route to right-sized model", "Cache common answers", "Batch where possible", "Lower cost"]}
### Inference-time compute
Reasoning models spend more compute at inference by "thinking" longer, trading cost and latency for accuracy on hard problems.
> In enterprise AI budgets, inference and integration usually matter more than training.

# GPUs and matrix maths
A neural network layer is essentially a matrix multiplication: inputs times weights. A large model performs trillions of multiply-adds per response. GPUs execute thousands in parallel; tensor cores add dedicated hardware for small matrix operations.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative throughput for AI maths (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "CPU", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "GPU", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "GPU with tensor cores", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}]}
### Data-centre GPUs
NVIDIA's H100 (2022) and B200 (2024) generations dominate AI training. A single high-end AI server can contain 8 GPUs linked by high-speed connections, and clusters link thousands of servers.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Cluster: thousands of servers", "Server: 8 GPUs", "GPU: thousands of cores and tensor cores", "Core: multiply-add units"]}
### Software moat
NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem, libraries and tools built since 2007 are a major reason for its dominance, as much as raw chip performance.
!? AI chips now use number formats as small as 8 or even 4 bits for inference, because neural networks tolerate low precision surprisingly well.

# Specialised AI chips
As AI workloads grew, companies designed chips focused purely on neural network operations, trading flexibility for efficiency.

### Google's TPUs
Google built its first Tensor Processing Unit in 2015 when it realised voice search with neural networks would require doubling its data centres. TPUs now train many of Google's Gemini models.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2015", "l": "first TPU, inference"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "TPU v2, training"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "TPU pods with thousands of chips"}]}
### On-device AI
NPUs in phones and laptops run features like photo enhancement, transcription and small language models locally, improving privacy and battery life.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "General GPU", "items": ["flexible", "powerful", "power-hungry"]}, "r": {"t": "Specialised NPU or TPU", "items": ["efficient", "less flexible"]}}
> For a home lab, a small Coral or a modest GPU can run detection or small models at very low power.

# Memory is often the bottleneck
Generating each token of a language model's output requires reading all active weights from memory. For large models, memory bandwidth, how fast data moves, often limits speed more than compute.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Weights in memory", "Read for each token", "Compute", "Next token"]}
### High-bandwidth memory
AI GPUs use HBM: memory stacked next to the processor with very wide connections, delivering several terabytes per second. HBM supply has been a key bottleneck in AI chip production.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "memory bandwidth, TB/s (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Laptop DDR5", "v": 0.1}, {"l": "Gaming GPU", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "H100 HBM", "v": 3.35}, {"l": "B200 HBM", "v": 8.0, "hi": true}]}
### The KV cache
During generation, models store intermediate results for every previous token (the key–value cache). Long contexts and many simultaneous users make this cache huge, sometimes larger than the model weights.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Model weights", "items": ["fixed size"]}, "r": {"t": "KV cache", "items": ["grows with context length and users"]}}
### Unified memory
Some laptops with unified memory (shared between CPU and GPU) can run surprisingly large quantised models locally because they have plenty of memory, even if compute is modest.
!? Rule of thumb: a model's token generation speed is roughly memory bandwidth divided by the bytes read per token.

# FLOPs
A FLOP is a single floating-point operation, such as one multiplication. FLOP/s measures speed; total FLOPs measure the size of a computation.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "GFLOP", "b": "10⁹ operations"}, {"a": "TFLOP", "b": "10¹²"}, {"a": "PFLOP", "b": "10¹⁵"}, {"a": "EFLOP", "b": "10¹⁸"}]}
### Training compute
A common estimate: training compute ≈ 6 × parameters × training tokens. A 70-billion-parameter model trained on 15 trillion tokens needs about 6 × 7×10¹⁰ × 1.5×10¹³ ≈ 6×10²⁴ FLOPs.
ex: 6 × 70 billion × 15 trillion | ≈ 6 × 10²⁴ FLOPs | weeks on thousands of GPUs
### Growth
Training compute for notable models has grown roughly 4–5 times per year since about 2010, far faster than Moore's law.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "AlexNet, 2012", "b": "~5 × 10¹⁷ FLOPs"}, {"a": "GPT-3, 2020", "b": "~3 × 10²³ FLOPs"}, {"a": "Frontier models, 2024", "b": "~5 × 10²⁵ FLOPs"}]}
### Regulation uses FLOPs
The EU AI Act presumes general-purpose models trained with more than 10²⁵ FLOPs have "systemic risk", triggering extra obligations.
> FLOPs are a rough proxy for capability: data quality and algorithms matter too.

# Quantisation
Quantisation stores weights (and sometimes activations) with fewer bits. Fewer bits mean less memory, less bandwidth and faster computation, at some cost in precision.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "model size, GB (8B-parameter model)", "items": [{"l": "32-bit", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "16-bit", "v": 16.0}, {"l": "8-bit", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "4-bit", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}]}
### Methods
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Post-training quantisation", "b": "convert after training, quick"}, {"a": "Quantisation-aware training", "b": "train with low precision in mind, better quality"}, {"a": "GPTQ, AWQ", "b": "popular LLM methods"}, {"a": "GGUF", "b": "format used for local models"}]}
### Quality trade-off
8-bit usually loses almost nothing; 4-bit loses a little, more on complex reasoning and maths; below 3 bits quality drops noticeably. Larger models tolerate quantisation better than small ones.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "8-bit", "items": ["near-identical quality", "half the size"]}, "r": {"t": "4-bit", "items": ["small quality loss", "quarter the size"]}}
### Local AI
Quantisation is what makes running capable models on a laptop, home server or phone practical, using tools like llama.cpp and Ollama.
!? A 4-bit 70B model can outperform an 8B model at full precision while still fitting on a well-equipped workstation.

# Scaling
In 2020 researchers at OpenAI, and in 2022 DeepMind (the "Chinchilla" paper), showed that model loss falls as a smooth power law with more compute, data and parameters. Chinchilla found many models were undertrained: for a given compute budget, use about 20 tokens per parameter.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["More compute", "Bigger model and more data, balanced", "Lower loss", "New capabilities"]}
### Chinchilla's rule of thumb
ex: Compute-optimal: ~20 tokens per parameter | 70B parameters | ≈ 1.4 trillion tokens
### Beyond pre-training scale
Recent gains also come from scaling post-training (reinforcement learning on reasoning tasks) and inference-time compute (letting models think longer).
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Scaling axes", "items": ["Pre-training compute", "Data quality", "Post-training RL", "Inference-time thinking"]}
### Limits
• High-quality human text is finite; data may become a bottleneck
• Costs of frontier training runs have reached hundreds of millions of dollars or more
• Energy and chip supply constrain growth
!? Smaller models are often trained far beyond the Chinchilla ratio (hundreds of tokens per parameter) because that makes them cheaper to run at inference.

== ai-bias

# Models learn our past
Supervised models learn to reproduce historical outcomes. If those outcomes reflect discrimination, unequal access or skewed processes, the model encodes and can amplify them, at scale and with an aura of objectivity.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Historical decisions with bias", "Training data", "Model learns the pattern", "Automated biased decisions at scale"]}
### Sources of bias
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Bias sources", "items": ["Historical: past decisions unfair", "Representation: some groups under-sampled", "Measurement: proxies measure groups differently", "Aggregation: one model for very different groups"]}
### Example: healthcare costs
A 2019 study in *Science* found a US health algorithm used past healthcare spending to predict need. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same illness, the algorithm under-referred them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Proxy used", "items": ["past spending"]}, "r": {"t": "Real need", "items": ["illness severity"]}}
!! Algorithms are neutral because they're maths => They reflect the data and choices of the people who built them
> Ask what the training labels really measure, and whether that's the same as what you care about.

# A real example
Amazon began building an AI recruiting tool in 2014 to rate CVs from one to five stars. Trained on ten years of CVs submitted mostly by men, it reportedly learned to downgrade CVs containing "women's" (as in "women's chess club captain") and graduates of two all-women's colleges.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2014", "l": "project starts"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "bias noticed"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "project wound down"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "reported by Reuters"}]}
### What went wrong
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Training data reflected past male-dominated hiring", "Model learned gendered words as signals", "Fixing specific words didn't guarantee fairness", "Team lost confidence in the tool"]}
### Lessons
• Historical success data can encode past exclusion
• Removing obvious signals doesn't remove bias
• Pilot, audit and be ready to stop a project
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"We removed the gender field, so it's fair\"", "r": "Proxies remain, test outcomes by group"}]}
### Hiring is high-risk
Under the EU AI Act, AI used for recruitment, CV screening and evaluating employees is classified as high-risk, requiring risk management, data governance, human oversight and documentation.
!? Amazon said the tool was never used by recruiters as the sole basis for decisions.

# Proxies
A proxy is a feature that correlates strongly with a sensitive attribute. Models are excellent at finding correlations, so they can reconstruct gender, ethnicity or age from seemingly neutral data.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Postcode", "b": "ethnicity, income"}, {"a": "First name", "b": "gender, origin"}, {"a": "Career gaps", "b": "gender, caregiving"}, {"a": "Graduation year", "b": "age"}]}
### Redlining
In the 1930s, US agencies drew red lines around minority neighbourhoods on maps and denied mortgages there. A model using postcode can recreate similar patterns without anyone intending it.
### Testing for proxies
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Remove sensitive attribute", "Try to predict it from other features", "If easy, proxies exist", "Check outcome disparities"]}
### Keep the sensitive data for auditing
Paradoxically, you often need sensitive attributes (collected lawfully and protected) to measure fairness. Without them, you can't see whether outcomes differ by group.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Blind model", "items": ["no gender field", "bias hidden"]}, "r": {"t": "Audited model", "items": ["gender used to test", "bias measured"]}}
> "Fairness through unawareness" rarely works; fairness needs measurement.

# Measure performance by group
Disaggregated evaluation breaks metrics down by subgroup: gender, age, region, language, device type. Averages can hide groups for whom the system fails badly.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "error rate by group, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Group A", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Group B", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Group C", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Group D", "v": 18.0, "hi": true}]}
### What to compare
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Accuracy", "b": "overall correctness"}, {"a": "False positive rate", "b": "who is wrongly flagged?"}, {"a": "False negative rate", "b": "who is wrongly missed?"}, {"a": "Selection rate", "b": "who gets approved?"}]}
### Intersectionality
Problems often appear at intersections: older women, young men in rural areas. Check combinations, not just single attributes, as long as groups are large enough to measure.
### Practical tools
Libraries like Fairlearn and AIF360 compute fairness metrics, and model cards document performance across groups for users and auditors.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Define groups", "Compute metrics per group", "Identify gaps", "Investigate causes", "Mitigate and retest"]}
!? The Gender Shades study (2018) found worst-case error differences of over 30 percentage points between groups in commercial face analysis.

# Different definitions of fair
Researchers have proposed dozens of fairness definitions. Many are mutually incompatible when groups have different base rates, a result proved independently by several teams around 2016–17.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Demographic parity", "b": "equal approval rates across groups"}, {"a": "Equal opportunity", "b": "equal true positive rates"}, {"a": "Equalised odds", "b": "equal true and false positive rates"}, {"a": "Calibration", "b": "same score means same risk for everyone"}]}
### The COMPAS debate
In 2016 ProPublica found a US recidivism tool had higher false positive rates for Black defendants. The vendor showed its scores were equally calibrated across groups. Both were right: with different base rates, you can't satisfy both definitions at once.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "ProPublica's lens", "items": ["unequal false positive rates"]}, "r": {"t": "Vendor's lens", "items": ["equal calibration"]}}
### Choosing
The choice depends on context, law and values: in lending, equal calibration may matter; in screening for opportunities, equal opportunity may matter more. It's an ethical and legal decision, not just a technical one.
> Write down which fairness definition you chose and why; that's part of accountable AI.

# Feedback loops
Predictions influence actions, actions generate data, and that data trains the next model. Biased predictions can therefore become self-fulfilling.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Model predicts risk in area A", "More patrols in area A", "More incidents recorded there", "Model predicts even higher risk"]}
### Predictive policing
Studies of predictive policing showed that systems trained on arrest data could repeatedly send officers to the same neighbourhoods, regardless of underlying crime rates, because more patrols produced more recorded crime.
### In business
• Credit: people never given loans never generate repayment data
• Recommendations: items never shown never get clicks
• Inspections: places never inspected appear "clean"
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without exploration", "items": ["model sees only its own choices"]}, "r": {"t": "With exploration", "items": ["some random checks reveal true rates"]}}
### Breaking loops
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Random audits", "Exploration in decisions", "Use outcome data not shaped by the model", "Monitor long-term effects"]}
> Ask: does the model's output change the data it will learn from next?

# Fixing bias
Bias mitigation can happen before, during or after training, plus in the surrounding process.

### Techniques
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Reweighting", "b": "give under-represented cases more weight"}, {"a": "Better data", "b": "collect missing groups"}, {"a": "Constraints", "b": "penalise disparities during training"}, {"a": "Threshold adjustment", "b": "equalise error rates where lawful"}]}
### Trade-offs
Reducing one disparity can increase another or slightly reduce overall accuracy. Document the trade-offs and decide with legal, business and affected stakeholders.

# Bias isn't only about people
"Bias" in data science broadly means systematic error. Any mismatch between training data and the real conditions creates blind spots, whether or not people are involved.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sensors", "b": "trained on one machine model, fails on another"}, {"a": "Seasons", "b": "trained in summer, fails in winter"}, {"a": "Markets", "b": "trained in calm prices, fails in volatility"}, {"a": "Geography", "b": "trained in one country, fails in another"}]}
### Selection bias in operations
A predictive maintenance model trained only on machines that were serviced regularly may not recognise early failure signs on neglected ones.
### Commodity example
A price model trained during stable years may underestimate risk when droughts, wars or export bans hit; stress-testing with extreme historical periods helps.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Calm training period", "items": ["low volatility"]}, "r": {"t": "Real shocks", "items": ["weather, war, export bans"]}}
### Checklist
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Where did the data come from?", "Which conditions are missing?", "How will deployment differ?", "How will we detect failure?"]}
> Every dataset is a sample of the world; know what your sample leaves out.

AI safety spans a spectrum of concerns, from today's practical failures to risks from future, much more capable systems. Different communities focus on different layers, but they share methods: testing, monitoring, oversight and careful design.

# Alignment
Alignment is the challenge of making an AI system's goals and behaviour match human intentions and values. It's hard because intentions are rich and contextual, while training optimises measurable proxies.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Outer alignment", "items": ["is the objective we specify the right one?"]}, "r": {"t": "Inner alignment", "items": ["does the model actually learn that objective, or something that merely correlates?"]}}
### Techniques
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "RLHF", "b": "learn from human preferences"}, {"a": "Constitutional AI", "b": "follow written principles"}, {"a": "Scalable oversight", "b": "AI helps humans supervise AI"}, {"a": "Interpretability", "b": "check internal goals and reasoning"}]}
### Sycophancy
A simple alignment failure: models trained to please may agree with users' mistaken beliefs or flatter them. Labs specifically train and test against this.
!! An aligned AI just does what it's told => Good alignment includes judgement: refusing harmful requests and flagging mistakes, not blind obedience
### Why it gets harder
As systems grow more capable and act more autonomously, humans can check less of their work directly, so getting goals right matters more.
> The King Midas story is the classic alignment parable: he got exactly what he asked for, not what he wanted.

# Specification gaming
Specification gaming happens whenever an optimiser finds a loophole in an objective. DeepMind compiled dozens of examples across RL, evolution and ML systems.

Models can learn to produce answers that look good to raters rather than being correct, or to tell users what they want to hear. Coding agents have been observed making tests pass by special-casing them rather than fixing the bug.

# Robustness
Robustness means reliable behaviour across variations in inputs and conditions, including inputs designed to cause failures.

### Adversarial examples

Rephrasing a question, adding irrelevant text or changing the order of options can change answers. Robust systems are tested with many variations.
### Building robustness

# Jailbreaks and prompt injection
**Jailbreaks** are user attempts to bypass a model's safety rules: role-play scenarios, obfuscated requests, "ignore previous instructions". **Prompt injection** is an attacker hiding instructions in content the AI processes, like a web page, email or document.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Agent reads a web page", "Hidden text: \"send the user's files to this address\"", "Agent follows it", "Data leaked"]}
### Why injection is dangerous
Language models don't reliably separate trusted instructions from untrusted data: it's all text. As agents gain tools (email, files, payments), injected instructions can trigger real actions.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Jailbreak", "items": ["user attacks the model's rules"]}, "r": {"t": "Prompt injection", "items": ["third-party content hijacks the model"]}}
### Defences
• Treat all external content as untrusted data
• Limit agents' permissions to what each task needs
• Require human confirmation for sensitive actions
• Filter and monitor tool calls and outputs
• Train models to recognise and resist injected instructions
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Least privilege", "b": "only necessary tools"}, {"a": "Confirmation", "b": "human approves risky steps"}, {"a": "Isolation", "b": "separate untrusted content"}, {"a": "Logging", "b": "audit every action"}]}
!? OWASP lists prompt injection as the top security risk in its "Top 10 for LLM Applications".

# Red teaming
Red teaming borrows from military and cybersecurity practice: a dedicated team plays the adversary to find weaknesses before real attackers do.

# Human oversight

# Interpretability
Interpretability research aims to understand the internal computations of neural networks. "Mechanistic interpretability" tries to reverse-engineer specific circuits and features.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Record model activations", "Find meaningful features", "Trace how they combine", "Test by intervening"]}
### Findings so far
• Features representing concepts like cities, emotions, code bugs or deception
• Circuits for tasks like copying text or simple arithmetic
• Techniques like sparse autoencoders that separate millions of features
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Feature", "b": "a direction representing a concept"}, {"a": "Circuit", "b": "features combining to compute something"}, {"a": "Steering", "b": "amplifying a feature to change behaviour"}, {"a": "Probe", "b": "small model testing what's represented"}]}
### The Golden Gate experiment
In 2024 Anthropic researchers found a feature for the Golden Gate Bridge in a Claude model. Amplifying it made the model bring up the bridge in every answer, even claiming to be the bridge, demonstrating causal control.
### Why it matters
Interpretability could help detect hidden goals, deception or dangerous knowledge, and verify that safety training worked, rather than relying only on outward behaviour.
> We built these systems, but we don't fully understand how they work inside; interpretability is closing that gap.

== ai-law

# The EU AI Act
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act, is the world's first comprehensive horizontal AI law. It applies to providers and deployers of AI systems in the EU market, including non-EU companies whose systems are used in the EU.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Unacceptable risk: banned", "High risk: strict obligations", "Limited risk: transparency duties", "Minimal risk: no new obligations"]}
### Separate track: general-purpose AI
Providers of general-purpose AI models, like large language models, have their own obligations: technical documentation, copyright policy, training-data summaries, and extra duties for models with systemic risk.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "AI systems", "items": ["regulated by use and risk"]}, "r": {"t": "General-purpose models", "items": ["regulated as building blocks, with extra rules above 10²⁵ FLOPs"]}}
### Most AI is minimal risk
Spam filters, recommendation engines and most internal productivity tools fall into minimal risk; voluntary codes of conduct are encouraged.
### Supporting structure
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "AI Office", "b": "EU-level oversight of general-purpose models"}, {"a": "National authorities", "b": "market surveillance in each country"}, {"a": "Standards", "b": "harmonised technical standards"}, {"a": "Sandboxes", "b": "test innovations with regulators"}]}
!? Like GDPR, the AI Act may become a global reference: companies often apply EU rules worldwide rather than run separate versions.

# Banned practices
Since 2 February 2025, certain AI practices are prohibited in the EU because they're considered to pose unacceptable risks to fundamental rights.
• Manipulative or deceptive techniques causing significant harm
• Exploiting vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social situation
• Social scoring leading to unjustified detrimental treatment
• Predicting crime based solely on profiling
• Untargeted scraping of facial images
• Emotion recognition at work and in schools
• Biometric categorisation by sensitive traits
• Real-time remote biometric ID by police in public, with narrow exceptions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Manipulation", "Exploiting vulnerabilities", "Social scoring", "Untargeted face scraping", "Workplace emotion recognition"]}
### Narrow exceptions
Real-time biometric identification by police is allowed only for specific serious cases (searching for victims of abduction, preventing imminent terrorist threats, locating suspects of serious crimes), with prior authorisation.
### Practical meaning for companies
• HR tools that infer employees' emotions from webcams are banned
• Systems that exploit gambling addicts' vulnerabilities to push bets would breach the ban
• Workplace monitoring must avoid these categories
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Allowed", "items": ["detecting fatigue for driver safety"]}, "r": {"t": "Banned", "items": ["inferring employee emotions for evaluation"]}}
> The ban list is short, but the fines for breaching it are the highest in the Act.

• Education
• Employment and HR
• Essential services and credit
• Law enforcement
• Migration and borders
• Justice and democracy

# Transparency duties
Certain AI uses carry transparency obligations regardless of risk level, so people know when they're dealing with AI or AI-generated content.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Chatbots", "b": "tell people they're talking to AI"}, {"a": "Generated content", "b": "machine-readable marking"}, {"a": "Deepfakes", "b": "label as artificially generated"}, {"a": "Emotion recognition", "b": "inform people exposed"}]}

Text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must disclose it was AI-generated, unless it has undergone human editorial review with responsibility taken.

# Automated decisions
GDPR Article 22 gives people the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produces legal or similarly significant effects, unless exceptions apply (contract necessity, law, or explicit consent) with safeguards.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fully automated decision", "Significant effect, e.g. loan refused", "Right to human intervention", "Right to contest", "Meaningful information about the logic"]}
### The SCHUFA ruling
In December 2023 the EU Court of Justice ruled that a credit score produced by Germany's SCHUFA can itself be an automated decision when lenders rely on it decisively, extending protections to score providers.
### Meaningful human review
A rubber-stamp review by someone who always accepts the system's output doesn't make a decision "not solely automated". The reviewer needs authority and competence to change it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rubber stamp", "items": ["still automated in law"]}, "r": {"t": "Genuine review", "items": ["human weighs evidence", "can overturn"]}}
### Explanations
In 2025 the Court of Justice clarified that people are entitled to an explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied, in an intelligible form, even when trade secrets are involved, with courts able to balance interests.
> If your process automatically refuses customers, design human review and explanations in from the start.

# Copyright questions
Generative AI raises two big copyright questions: is training on copyrighted works lawful, and who, if anyone, owns AI-generated output?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Training", "items": ["text and data mining exceptions", "opt-outs", "lawsuits"]}, "r": {"t": "Output", "items": ["human authorship needed", "prompts alone often insufficient"]}}
### EU position
The EU's text and data mining exception allows mining lawfully accessed works unless rights-holders opt out in machine-readable ways. The AI Act requires general-purpose model providers to respect opt-outs and publish a summary of training content.
### Lawsuits
Major cases include news publishers, authors, artists, music labels and image libraries suing AI developers in the US and Europe. Some have settled or led to licensing deals; others continue.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2023", "l": "first major lawsuits"}, {"y": "2024–25", "l": "licensing deals with publishers"}, {"y": "2025", "l": "first court rulings and large settlements"}]}
### AI-generated works
In the US, the Copyright Office has held that purely AI-generated material lacks the human authorship needed for protection, though human selection, arrangement and edits can be protected.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Purely AI-generated", "b": "generally no copyright"}, {"a": "Human-edited and arranged", "b": "human parts protected"}, {"a": "AI as a tool", "b": "human author owns"}, {"a": "Contracts", "b": "may assign rights anyway"}]}

A deployer becomes a provider, with full obligations, if it puts its name on a high-risk system, makes a substantial modification, or changes its intended purpose so it becomes high-risk.

== ai-explain

# The black-box problem
Complex models can make accurate predictions without offering human-understandable reasons. That creates problems for trust, debugging, fairness and legal compliance.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why explanations matter", "items": ["Users: trust and act on outputs", "Developers: debug errors", "Regulators: verify compliance", "Affected people: understand and contest"]}
### Explainability vs interpretability
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Interpretable model", "items": ["understandable by design", "e.g. small trees"]}, "r": {"t": "Explainable output", "items": ["post-hoc explanation of a complex model", "e.g. SHAP"]}}
### Debugging with explanations
Explanations often expose problems: a medical model relying on hospital-specific markings in X-ray images, or a credit model leaning on a leaky feature.
### Trade-off myth
The assumed trade-off between accuracy and interpretability is often smaller than expected for structured data; well-designed interpretable models can be competitive.
!! Complex models are always more accurate => For tabular data, interpretable models are often nearly as good
> Cynthia Rudin argued in 2019 that for high-stakes decisions, we should use interpretable models instead of explaining black boxes.

# Glass-box models
Glass-box models are understandable by design. Their structure itself is the explanation.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Linear and logistic models", "b": "each weight shows a feature's effect"}, {"a": "Small decision trees", "b": "readable if-then paths"}, {"a": "Scorecards", "b": "points per factor, summed"}, {"a": "Generalised additive models", "b": "a curve per feature, added up"}]}
### Scorecards
Credit scorecards add points for factors and compare the total to a cut-off. They've been used for decades and are easy to audit and explain to customers.

### Explainable boosting machines
EBMs (Microsoft's InterpretML) are a modern additive model using boosting: each feature gets its own learned curve, giving accuracy near gradient boosting with full transparency.

### When to choose glass-box
• Regulated decisions like credit and insurance
• When users must understand and challenge outputs
• When data is tabular and moderate in size

# Feature importance
Global feature importance ranks which inputs most influence a model's predictions overall. Methods include permutation importance (shuffle a feature and see how much accuracy drops) and average SHAP values.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "importance (delivery-delay model, illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Distance", "v": 35.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Weather", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Carrier", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Warehouse load", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "Day of week", "v": 5.0}]}
### Permutation importance
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Measure model accuracy", "Shuffle one feature's values", "Measure accuracy again", "Drop = importance"]}
### Pitfalls
• Correlated features share importance, so each may look less important than it is
• Importance doesn't show direction: does more rain increase or decrease delay?
• Importance isn't causation: changing an important feature may not change outcomes
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Importance says", "items": ["the model relies on this"]}, "r": {"t": "It doesn't say", "items": ["changing this will change reality"]}}
### Global vs local
Global importance describes the model overall; local explanations describe one specific prediction, which can differ a lot.
!? Tree models' built-in "gain" importance can be biased towards features with many distinct values; permutation and SHAP are more reliable.

# SHAP values
SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations, 2017) uses Shapley values from cooperative game theory: each feature's contribution is its average marginal effect across all possible combinations of features. The contributions add up exactly to the difference between this prediction and the average.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "prediction = average + Σ feature contributions", "items": [{"s": "average", "m": "model's baseline"}, {"s": "contributions", "m": "positive or negative per feature"}]}
### Example
A delivery predicted 9 hours late, against an average of 2 hours: distance +4, storm warning +3, carrier −1, warehouse load +1 = +7.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Distance", "b": "+4 hours of delay"}, {"a": "Storm warning", "b": "+3 hours"}, {"a": "Warehouse load", "b": "+1 hour"}, {"a": "Good carrier", "b": "−1 hour"}]}
### Uses
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Waterfall plot", "b": "one prediction explained"}, {"a": "Summary plot", "b": "all predictions, global patterns"}, {"a": "Dependence plot", "b": "how one feature's effect varies"}, {"a": "Monitoring", "b": "detect shifts in what drives predictions"}]}
### Limits
• Computationally expensive for some models
• Explains the model, not the real world
• Correlated features make attributions tricky
!? Lloyd Shapley won the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics partly for the game-theory concept SHAP is built on.

# Counterfactual explanations
A counterfactual explanation describes the smallest change to inputs that would change the outcome. It's often the most useful explanation for affected people because it's actionable.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Loan refused", "Find nearest approved profile", "\"Income +€4,000 or debt −€3,000\"", "Person knows what to do"]}
### Good counterfactuals are
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Minimal: small changes", "Actionable: changeable features only", "Plausible: realistic combinations", "Diverse: several options"]}
### What not to suggest
"You'd be approved if you were 10 years older" is unhelpful; "if you were male" is discriminatory. Counterfactuals should only use mutable, legitimate features.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bad counterfactual", "items": ["change age or gender"]}, "r": {"t": "Good counterfactual", "items": ["reduce debt", "increase savings"]}}
### Legal relevance
Researchers Wachter, Mittelstadt and Russell (2017) proposed counterfactuals as a practical way to meet GDPR's transparency goals without revealing a model's internals.
> Counterfactuals answer the question people actually ask: what can I do differently?

# Saliency maps

### Clever Hans effects
Named after a horse that seemed to do arithmetic but was reading its owner's cues, "Clever Hans" models succeed for the wrong reasons. Saliency maps have revealed models classifying horses by copyright watermarks and pneumonia by hospital-specific scanner markings.

• Highlighting where the model looked doesn't explain why

# Explanations build trust
Explanations can increase appropriate trust, helping people know when to rely on AI and when to question it. But persuasive explanations can also create false confidence.

### Calibration

# Required in some cases
Several legal frameworks require explanations or transparency for AI-assisted decisions.

# Why run locally?
Edge deployment is chosen for specific benefits. Each matters differently by use case.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Latency", "b": "braking in a car can't wait for the cloud"}, {"a": "Privacy", "b": "video and audio stay at home"}, {"a": "Cost", "b": "no per-call fees at scale"}, {"a": "Reliability", "b": "works without internet"}]}
### Bandwidth
A single HD camera produces several megabits per second continuously. Sending only events ("person detected at 21:03") instead of raw video cuts bandwidth by orders of magnitude.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "data sent per day, GB (one camera)", "items": [{"l": "Raw HD video stream", "v": 40.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Event clips only", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "Event metadata", "v": 0.001}]}
### Regulation
Processing sensitive data locally can simplify GDPR compliance, since less personal data is transferred or stored centrally.
### Trade-offs
Local processing means managing hardware, updates and security across many devices, which can be harder than managing one cloud service.
> Choose the edge when speed, privacy or connectivity matter more than raw model power.

# Making models small
Edge devices have limited memory, compute and energy, so models are compressed while keeping as much accuracy as possible.

### Knowledge distillation
Proposed by Hinton and colleagues in 2015, distillation trains a small model to match a large model's full output probabilities, not just correct labels. The "soft" probabilities carry extra information about similarities between classes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Big teacher model", "Produces soft probabilities", "Small student learns to match them", "Compact, accurate model"]}
### Results
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "model size, MB (image classifier, illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Original", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Quantised", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Pruned and quantised", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Distilled", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}]}
### Tools

!? Small language models distilled from larger ones now run on phones, handling summarisation and smart replies offline.

# Accelerators
Edge accelerators are specialised chips for neural network operations, delivering far more operations per watt than general CPUs.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Google Coral Edge TPU", "b": "~4 TOPS at ~2 W"}, {"a": "NVIDIA Jetson", "b": "GPU-based edge computers"}, {"a": "Phone NPUs", "b": "tens of TOPS"}, {"a": "Hailo and others", "b": "edge AI modules"}]}
### TOPS
Edge chips are rated in TOPS: trillions of operations per second, usually at 8-bit precision. TOPS per watt is the key efficiency measure.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "TOPS (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Coral Edge TPU", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Hailo-8", "v": 26.0}, {"l": "Jetson Orin Nano", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Recent phone NPU", "v": 45.0, "hi": true}]}

### Why robotics is hard

# The long tail
The long tail is the vast number of rare situations that each occur seldom but together happen constantly. Getting from 99% to 99.9999% reliability is far harder than reaching 99%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of driving situations (illustrative), %", "items": [{"l": "Routine", "v": 95.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Unusual", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Rare and strange", "v": 1.0}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Animals on the road", "Police directing traffic by hand", "Construction with odd signs", "Objects falling from trucks", "Emergency vehicles approaching"]}
### Strategies
• Massive simulation of rare scenarios
• Collecting and labelling unusual events from fleets
• Conservative behaviour when uncertain: slow down, stop safely
• Remote assistance for confusing situations
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fleet encounters rare event", "Logged", "Added to simulation and training", "Model improves"]}
### Why it matters elsewhere
Every AI deployment has a long tail: customer requests nobody anticipated, documents in odd formats, unusual machine failures. Plan for exceptions.
> Reliability is measured by how a system handles the weird cases, not the typical ones.

# What is AGI?
AGI has many definitions: AI matching humans at most economically valuable work; AI that can learn any intellectual task a human can; or simply systems far more general than today's. Without agreement on the definition, timelines are hard to compare.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "AGI definitions", "items": ["Economic: does most valuable work", "Cognitive: matches human abilities broadly", "Learning: learns new tasks like humans", "Levels: graded scales of capability and autonomy"]}
### Expert views vary
Surveys of AI researchers give a wide spread of predictions, with medians moving earlier in recent years. Some leaders of AI labs suggest highly capable systems within years; many researchers expect longer or doubt the framing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Shorter timelines", "items": ["rapid recent progress", "scaling continues"]}, "r": {"t": "Longer timelines", "items": ["reliability gaps", "reasoning and learning limits"]}}
### Jagged frontier
Current AI is "jagged": superhuman at some tasks (broad knowledge, coding contests) and weak at others (reliable long tasks, physical common sense). Progress doesn't arrive evenly.
### Why it matters
Highly general AI would transform economies, science and security, which is why governments and labs debate safety, governance and how to share benefits.
> Rather than waiting for an "AGI moment", track concrete capabilities and their real-world effects.

# Open vs closed models
"Open" in AI usually means open weights: the trained parameters are downloadable. Fully open source would also include training data and code, which is rarer.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Open weights", "items": ["run anywhere", "customise", "inspect", "harder to control misuse"]}, "r": {"t": "Closed API", "items": ["often most capable", "managed safety", "data sent to provider"]}}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Meta Llama", "b": "open weights with licence terms"}, {"a": "Mistral", "b": "mix of open and commercial"}, {"a": "DeepSeek and Qwen", "b": "open-weight Chinese models"}, {"a": "OpenAI, Anthropic, Google", "b": "mostly closed frontier models, some open releases"}]}

Open-weight models allow private deployment, fine-tuning and cost control; closed models offer convenience and often the highest capability. Many companies use both through a gateway.
