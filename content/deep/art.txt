== ar-painting

# The Renaissance
"Renaissance" means rebirth. The word was popularised by 19th-century historians, but the idea goes back to the painter and writer Giorgio Vasari, who in 1550 described a "rinascita" of the arts after what he saw as the dark Middle Ages. It began in Florence around 1400 and spread across Italy and then Europe over the next two centuries.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.1415", "l": "Brunelleschi: perspective"}, {"y": "1435", "l": "Alberti's On Painting"}, {"y": "1504", "l": "Michelangelo's David"}, {"y": "1527", "l": "Sack of Rome"}]}
### Why Florence, why then?
Several forces came together. Florence was rich from banking and wool, and families like the Medici competed for prestige by paying artists. Scholars rediscovered Greek and Roman texts, a trend boosted when Greek scholars came west around the fall of Constantinople in 1453. The printing press, from the 1450s, spread books and engravings fast.
• **Humanism**: a focus on human ability, history and the classical past, alongside faith
• **Observation**: artists dissected bodies and measured buildings to get things right
• **Status**: painters rose from craftsmen to celebrated intellectuals
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Renaissance art", "items": ["Classical models", "Anatomy", "Perspective", "Rich patrons"]}
### Early, High and beyond
Art historians split it into the Early Renaissance (Masaccio, Donatello, Botticelli) and the High Renaissance, roughly 1490–1527, when Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael worked. The Sack of Rome in 1527 is often taken as the end of that golden moment, followed by the stylised, elongated figures of **Mannerism**.
!! The Middle Ages had no real art => Medieval artists made superb cathedrals, manuscripts and altarpieces; the Renaissance changed goals, not talent
> The Renaissance put the human being, seen accurately, at the centre of art.

# Linear perspective
Linear perspective is a geometric trick for making a flat surface look like a window onto 3D space. The architect Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated it in Florence around 1415–1420, painting the Baptistery on a panel and letting viewers compare it with the real building through a peephole. Leon Battista Alberti wrote the rules down in 1435.
### How it works
You draw a **horizon line** at the viewer's eye level. Lines that are parallel in reality and run away from you (the edges of a road, floor tiles, the tops of walls) are drawn so they meet at a **vanishing point** on that line. Objects shrink in proportion to their distance.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Set horizon at eye level", "Pick a vanishing point", "Run lines to that point", "Shrink objects with distance"]}
ex: A column 4 m away looks a certain height | Move it to 8 m away | It is drawn half as tall
• **One-point** perspective: you face the scene straight on, like a corridor
• **Two-point**: you see a corner, with two vanishing points
• **Three-point**: you look up or down at tall buildings
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Horizon line", "b": "the viewer's eye level"}, {"a": "Vanishing point", "b": "where parallel lines meet"}, {"a": "Orthogonals", "b": "lines running to that point"}]}
### Using it for meaning
Painters used perspective not just for realism but to direct the eye. In Masaccio's *Holy Trinity* (about 1427), the painted chapel seems to cut into the church wall. In Leonardo's *Last Supper*, the vanishing point sits at Christ's head, so every line in the room leads you to him.
!? Before perspective, artists often showed importance by size: a saint might be painted far bigger than the people around them.
> Perspective turned painting into a window, and let artists decide exactly where your eye would land.

# Baroque drama
The Baroque grew partly out of religious conflict. After the Protestant Reformation, the Catholic Church's Council of Trent (1545–1563) asked for art that was clear, emotional and persuasive. Artists answered with theatre: dramatic moments, bodies in motion and light that seems to fall from heaven. Protestant countries like the Dutch Republic used the same skills for portraits, landscapes and everyday scenes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Renaissance", "items": ["calm, balanced", "even light", "ideal moment"]}, "r": {"t": "Baroque", "items": ["dramatic, moving", "deep shadows", "peak of action"]}}
### The masters
• **Caravaggio** (1571–1610) used real people from the streets as models and a harsh spotlight effect, called **tenebrism**
• **Rembrandt** (1606–1669) used light to reveal inner life, as in *The Night Watch* (1642)
• **Velázquez** (1599–1660) played with viewing itself in *Las Meninas* (1656)
• **Rubens** and the sculptor **Bernini** filled churches and palaces with swirling energy
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Bright figure in spotlight", "Mid-tones model the form", "Deep shadow hides the rest"]}
### Baroque in Portugal
In Portugal, the Baroque shows up most in **talha dourada**, carved wood covered in gold leaf that fills churches like São Francisco in Porto. Brazilian gold paid for much of it in the 1700s. Josefa de Óbidos (1630–1684) is the best-known Portuguese Baroque painter, famous for still lifes.
!! Chiaroscuro was invented in the Baroque => Renaissance artists like Leonardo already used light and shade; Baroque painters pushed it to extremes
> Baroque art wants you to feel first and think second.

# Impressionism
In the 1870s a group of painters in Paris grew tired of the official Salon, which favoured polished history scenes. They organised their own exhibition in 1874 in the former studio of the photographer Nadar. Claude Monet showed *Impression, Sunrise*, a hazy view of the port of Le Havre, and the critic Louis Leroy mocked the whole group as "Impressionists". The painters adopted the name.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1841", "l": "Paint in metal tubes"}, {"y": "1872", "l": "Impression, Sunrise"}, {"y": "1874", "l": "First group show"}, {"y": "1886", "l": "Eighth and last show"}]}
### What made it new
• **Painting outdoors** (en plein air), helped by new portable paint tubes
• **Visible brushstrokes**, laid quickly before the light changed
• **Colour in shadows**: shadows painted blue or violet, not brown or black
• **Modern subjects**: cafés, railway stations, boating parties, ballet rehearsals
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Salon painting", "items": ["studio, smooth finish", "history and myth"]}, "r": {"t": "Impressionism", "items": ["outdoors, quick strokes", "everyday modern life"]}}
### Light as the subject
Monet later painted the same haystacks, poplars and Rouen Cathedral facade dozens of times at different hours and seasons. The real subject was how light and atmosphere change what we see. Photography, then spreading fast, also pushed painters away from mere accuracy.
Women were central too: Berthe Morisot showed at seven of the eight exhibitions, and the American Mary Cassatt joined in 1879.
!? Impressionist paintings were once laughed at; today a Monet *Haystacks* canvas has sold for over €100 million at auction (2019).
> Impressionism made fleeting light, not fixed objects, the subject of painting.

# Post-Impressionism
Post-Impressionism isn't a single style. The English critic Roger Fry coined the label in 1910 for an exhibition in London, to group artists who started from Impressionism's bright colour but went in very different directions. What they shared was a turn away from recording what the eye sees towards expressing structure, emotion or symbolism.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Post-Impressionism", "items": ["Cézanne: structure", "Van Gogh: emotion", "Gauguin: symbol", "Seurat: dots"]}
### Four paths
• **Paul Cézanne** rebuilt nature from simple volumes and patches of colour, studying Mont Sainte-Victoire again and again. Cubists saw him as their forerunner.
• **Vincent van Gogh** used thick, swirling paint and intense colour to show feeling.
• **Paul Gauguin** used flat areas of colour and went to Tahiti in 1891 looking for a "primitive" paradise, a view now seen as colonial fantasy.
• **Georges Seurat** built pictures from tiny dots of pure colour (**pointillism**) that blend in the viewer's eye.
### Van Gogh's short career
Van Gogh painted for only about ten years, from 1880 to his death in 1890 at 37. In that time he made roughly 860 oil paintings and over 2,000 artworks in all, many in his last two years in Arles and Auvers. His brother Theo supported him financially, and Theo's widow Jo van Gogh-Bonger later promoted his work to the world.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "about 10", "l": "years as a painter"}, {"v": "about 860", "l": "oil paintings"}, {"v": "about 70", "l": "in his last 70 days"}]}
!! Van Gogh sold only one painting => The Red Vineyard (1890) is the best-documented sale, but he sold or traded a few other works and drawings
> Post-Impressionists used colour and form to say something, not just to show something.

# Cubism
Pablo Picasso, a Spaniard in Paris, painted *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* in 1907. Its five nude women have angular, broken bodies, and two faces look like masks, influenced by ancient Iberian sculpture and African masks Picasso had seen in Paris. Even friends like Georges Braque and Henri Matisse were shocked. The painting was not shown in public until 1916 and is now in New York's Museum of Modern Art.
### Many views at once
Traditional perspective freezes a scene from one fixed point. Cubism argues that we know an object by moving around it. So a Cubist violin might show the front, the side and the scroll at the same time, broken into overlapping planes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Perspective", "items": ["one fixed viewpoint", "illusion of depth"]}, "r": {"t": "Cubism", "items": ["many viewpoints", "flat, broken planes"]}}
### Two phases
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1907", "l": "Les Demoiselles"}, {"y": "1908", "l": "Critic mocks 'cubes'"}, {"y": "1909–12", "l": "Analytic Cubism"}, {"y": "1912–14", "l": "Synthetic, collage"}]}
• **Analytic Cubism**: muted browns and greys, objects dissected into tiny facets
• **Synthetic Cubism**: brighter, simpler shapes, plus pasted paper, newsprint and wallpaper; Picasso's *Still Life with Chair Caning* (1912) is often called the first fine-art collage
Cubism fed directly into abstract art, Futurism and modern design. In Portugal, Amadeo de Souza-Cardoso absorbed it in Paris and made his own colourful version.
!? The name came from the critic Louis Vauxcelles, who in 1908 described Braque's landscapes as made of "little cubes".
> Cubism replaced the single viewpoint with the idea that seeing is something you do over time.

# Surrealism
Surrealism was launched by the French poet André Breton in his 1924 *Manifesto of Surrealism*. Inspired by Sigmund Freud's ideas about dreams and the unconscious, Breton wanted to free creativity from reason and good taste. It grew out of Dada, the anti-art movement born during the First World War.
### Two ways to reach the unconscious
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Automatism", "items": ["draw without planning", "chance and doodles"]}, "r": {"t": "Dream realism", "items": ["precise painting", "impossible scenes"]}}
• **Automatism**: artists like Joan Miró and André Masson let the hand move freely, hoping to bypass the conscious mind
• **Dream realism**: Salvador Dalí and René Magritte painted impossible things in a very realistic way, which makes them more unsettling
### Famous images
Dalí's *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) shows soft, melting pocket watches in a bare landscape. Magritte's *The Treachery of Images* (1929) shows a pipe with the caption that it is not a pipe. His point: it is a picture of a pipe. You can't fill it with tobacco.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Freud: dreams have meaning", "Breton's manifesto, 1924", "Paint the unconscious", "Viewer questions reality"]}
### Surrealism in Portugal
Portugal had its own Surrealist group from 1947, including Mário Cesariny and Artur Cruzeiro Seixas, working under the Estado Novo dictatorship. Their work was also a quiet act of rebellion against censorship.
!? Surrealism shaped advertising and film: Dalí worked with Alfred Hitchcock on a dream sequence for *Spellbound* (1945).
> Surrealism took the logic of dreams and painted it with the precision of reality.

# Abstract art
Abstract art doesn't try to show recognisable objects. Instead, colour, line and shape carry the meaning, as notes do in music. Wassily Kandinsky, a Russian in Munich, is often credited with the first abstract paintings around 1910–1911. The Swedish artist Hilma af Klint was painting abstract works even earlier, from 1906, but kept them private, so her role was only recognised much later.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1906", "l": "Hilma af Klint"}, {"y": "c.1911", "l": "Kandinsky"}, {"y": "1915", "l": "Malevich: Black Square"}, {"y": "1947", "l": "Pollock's drips"}]}
### Different reasons for abstraction
• **Spiritual**: Kandinsky believed colours had emotional and almost musical effects
• **Pure order**: Piet Mondrian reduced painting to black lines and red, yellow and blue rectangles, seeking universal harmony
• **Gesture**: Jackson Pollock laid canvases on the floor and dripped and poured paint, so the painting records his movement
• **Colour field**: Mark Rothko used huge soft rectangles of colour to create a mood
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Kandinsky", "b": "colour as music"}, {"a": "Mondrian", "b": "grid and primary colours"}, {"a": "Pollock", "b": "action painting"}, {"a": "Rothko", "b": "fields of colour"}]}
### A Portuguese voice
Maria Helena Vieira da Silva (1908–1992), born in Lisbon and based in Paris, made shimmering, maze-like paintings on the edge of abstraction. There is a foundation and museum for her and her husband, Árpád Szenes, in Lisbon.
!! A child could do that => Abstract artists usually trained for years in drawing; the challenge is deciding what to leave out and making it work as a whole
> Abstract art asks you to respond to colour and form directly, the way you respond to music.

# Pop Art
Pop Art took the images of consumer society, such as adverts, comics, packaging and film stars, and put them in galleries. It started in Britain: Richard Hamilton's small 1956 collage *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?* is often called the first Pop work. It exploded in New York in the early 1960s.
### The big names
• **Andy Warhol** showed *Campbell's Soup Cans* in 1962: 32 canvases, one for each flavour. He used **silkscreen printing** to repeat images of Marilyn Monroe, Coca-Cola bottles and car crashes.
• **Roy Lichtenstein** blew up comic-strip panels, painting by hand the printing dots (Ben-Day dots) used in cheap comics.
• **Claes Oldenburg** made giant soft sculptures of hamburgers and everyday objects.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Take a mass-media image", "Repeat or enlarge it", "Use print methods", "Hang it in a gallery"]}
### What was the point?
Pop Art reflected a world flooded by mass production and television. Warhol called his studio "the Factory" and liked images that looked machine-made. Was it celebrating consumerism or criticising it? Critics still disagree, and the ambiguity is part of its power.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Abstract Expr.", "items": ["personal gesture", "unique, heroic", "serious emotion"]}, "r": {"t": "Pop Art", "items": ["mass-media image", "repeated, mechanical", "cool and ironic"]}}
!? Lichtenstein's comic-based paintings were sometimes copied closely from specific comic panels, and the original comic artists were rarely credited.
> Pop Art turned the ordinary images we see every day into objects worth looking at twice.

# Portuguese painters
Portuguese painting has a long, distinct story, often overlooked outside the country. Three names from different centuries show its range.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.1470", "l": "Saint Vincent Panels"}, {"y": "1913", "l": "Amadeo in New York"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "Rego's abortion series"}, {"y": "2009", "l": "Casa das Histórias"}]}
### Nuno Gonçalves
The *Saint Vincent Panels*, attributed to Nuno Gonçalves and painted around the 1470s, are six oak panels with about 60 figures: royals, knights, monks, fishermen and clergy, all gathered around Saint Vincent, patron saint of Lisbon. The faces are strikingly individual. They were found in the monastery of São Vicente de Fora in the 1880s, and who exactly is shown is still debated. They hang in Lisbon's Museu Nacional de Arte Antiga.
### Amadeo de Souza-Cardoso
Born in 1887 near Amarante, Amadeo moved to Paris, befriended Modigliani and absorbed Cubism and Futurism. He showed eight works at the 1913 Armory Show in New York, a landmark of modern art in America. He died of Spanish flu in 1918, aged just 30, and was largely forgotten abroad for decades.
### Paula Rego
Paula Rego (1935–2022), born in Lisbon and based in London, painted dark, powerful stories about power, family and women's lives. Her 1998 series on illegal abortion, made after a Portuguese referendum failed to legalise it, is credited with influencing the 2007 vote that did. The Casa das Histórias Paula Rego in Cascais opened in 2009.

!? Almada Negreiros painted his famous portrait of Fernando Pessoa at a café table in 1954, then a mirror-image version in 1964 for the Gulbenkian.
> Portugal's painters have often worked at the edge of Europe's movements, then given them a voice of their own.

# Sound and pitch
Sound is a pressure wave: something vibrates and pushes the air back and forth. Those vibrations reach your eardrum, and the cochlea in your inner ear turns them into nerve signals. **Pitch** is how high or low we hear a sound, and it depends mainly on **frequency**, measured in hertz (Hz), meaning vibrations per second.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["String or reed vibrates", "Air pressure waves", "Eardrum and cochlea", "Brain hears a pitch"]}
### The range of hearing
Young humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. The upper limit drops with age, which is why teenagers can hear some high whines that adults can't. A piano covers about 27.5 Hz to 4,186 Hz.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 20.0, "hi": 20000.0, "log": true, "unit": "Hz", "items": [{"l": "Lowest piano", "v": 27.5}, {"l": "A 440", "v": 440.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Top piano", "v": 4186.0}, {"l": "Hearing limit", "v": 20000.0}]}
### Why 440 Hz?
Tuning used to vary a lot from city to city; historical "A"s ranged from roughly 400 to over 460 Hz. A440 was agreed internationally in 1939 and became an ISO standard in 1955. Many orchestras today tune a little higher, around 442–443 Hz, for a brighter sound.
ex: Sound travels about 343 m per second | A440 vibrates 440 times per second | Wavelength = 343 ÷ 440 ≈ 0.78 m
### More than pitch
Two instruments playing the same 440 Hz note still sound different. That's **timbre**: each instrument adds its own mix of higher frequencies called overtones, which your brain recognises as "violin" or "flute".
!! Bigger instruments are louder => Size mostly sets pitch: longer strings and bigger air columns make lower notes
> Pitch is frequency; the rest of a note's character comes from its overtones.

# The octave
If you double a note's frequency, you get the "same" note, only higher. A at 220 Hz, 440 Hz and 880 Hz all sound like versions of A. This is the **octave**, and almost every musical culture recognises it. A man and a woman singing "the same" tune together are usually an octave apart.
ex: A = 110 Hz | ×2 = 220 Hz | ×2 = 440 Hz | ×2 = 880 Hz
### Why 12 notes?
Ancient Greek thinkers, traditionally Pythagoras, noticed that simple frequency ratios sound pleasant: 2:1 (octave) and 3:2 (a fifth). Stacking fifths almost, but not quite, comes back to the starting note after 12 steps. Western music settled on 12 notes per octave.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Octave", "b": "ratio 2:1"}, {"a": "Perfect fifth", "b": "ratio 3:2"}, {"a": "Major third", "b": "about 5:4"}, {"a": "Semitone", "b": "about 1.0595 : 1"}]}
### Equal temperament
Since roughly the 18th–19th centuries, keyboards have mostly used **equal temperament**: the octave is split into 12 equal steps, each multiplying the frequency by the twelfth root of 2. Every key then works equally well, at the cost of slightly impure intervals.
= semitone ratio = 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.0595
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "frequency ratio of a fifth", "items": [{"l": "Pure fifth", "v": 1.5, "t": "1.500"}, {"l": "Equal temperament", "v": 1.498, "hi": true}]}
The difference is tiny, about 0.1%, but trained ears can hear it, and some choirs and string quartets bend notes towards pure intervals.
!? Not all music uses 12 notes: Arabic and Turkish traditions use intervals smaller than a semitone, and Indonesian gamelan uses scales of 5 or 7 unequal steps.
> The octave is the one interval almost everyone hears as "the same note".

# Scales
A scale is a set of notes chosen from the 12 available, arranged in order. The **major scale** uses seven notes in a fixed pattern of whole steps (two semitones) and half steps (one semitone). Play all the white keys from C to C and you have C major.
= Major: W W H W W W H
= Natural minor: W H W W H W W
### Major and minor
Start the same white keys on A instead and you get A **natural minor**. Same notes, different home, different mood. The key difference is the third note: in major it is four semitones above the root, in minor only three.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "C major", "items": ["C D E F G A B", "bright, settled"]}, "r": {"t": "A minor", "items": ["A B C D E F G", "darker, wistful"]}}
### Is minor really sad?
In Western listeners, minor keys are strongly linked with sadness and major with happiness, and even young children learn this association. But it's partly cultural. Plenty of joyful folk dances are in minor, and tempo matters a lot: a fast minor tune can sound energetic, a slow major one tender.
### Other scales
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Pentatonic: 5 notes, used worldwide", "Blues: pentatonic plus a 'blue' note", "Modes: e.g. Dorian, Mixolydian", "Chromatic: all 12 semitones"]}
ex: Start on G | Follow W W H W W W H | G A B C D E F♯ G (one black key needed)
!? The pentatonic scale (the black keys of a piano) appears in music from Scotland, China, West Africa and the blues, which is why simple tunes on black keys rarely sound "wrong".
> A scale is a pattern of steps; where the small steps fall gives it its mood.

# Chords
A chord is three or more notes sounding together. The simplest is the **triad**, built by stacking thirds: take a scale, play the 1st, 3rd and 5th notes. In C major that gives C, E and G.
### Major and minor chords
The difference is one semitone in the middle note.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "C major", "items": ["C E G", "4 + 3 semitones", "bright"]}, "r": {"t": "C minor", "items": ["C E♭ G", "3 + 4 semitones", "darker"]}}
In a major chord, the frequencies of C, E and G sit close to the simple ratio 4:5:6. Simple ratios mean the sound waves line up regularly, which our ears hear as **consonant**, or smooth.
ex: C ≈ 261.6 Hz | E ≈ 329.6 Hz (ratio ≈ 1.26, near 5:4) | G ≈ 392.0 Hz (ratio ≈ 1.50, i.e. 3:2)
### Building a chord on every note
Build a triad on each note of the C major scale and you get the seven chords of the key, labelled with Roman numerals: capital for major, lower case for minor.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["I = C major, IV = F, V = G", "ii = Dm, iii = Em, vi = Am", "vii° = B diminished", "Capital = major, small = minor"]}
### Adding colour
Add the 7th note and you get **seventh chords**, the backbone of jazz and blues. Diminished and augmented chords sound tense and unstable, so composers use them to build suspense before resolving to a calm chord.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tension: V7 (G7)", "Pull towards home", "Release: I (C)"]}
!! Dissonance is bad => Dissonant chords create tension that makes the resolution feel satisfying; music without it sounds bland
> Chords are built by stacking thirds, and moving from tension to rest is what drives harmony forward.

# Four chords
Take a key, pick chords I, V, vi and IV, and loop them. In C major that's C, G, A minor and F. This progression, and rotations of it, sits under a vast number of pop songs, from "Let It Be" and "With or Without You" to countless modern hits. In 2009 the Australian comedy group Axis of Awesome sang dozens of songs over the same four chords to make the point.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["I: C major", "V: G major", "vi: A minor", "IV: F major"]}
### Why it works
• **I** is home: stable and resolved
• **V** pulls strongly back towards home
• **vi** shares two notes with I but is minor, adding a touch of melancholy
• **IV** feels like a lift, open and hopeful, before the loop restarts
The progression mixes happy and sad colours, so the same chords fit love songs, anthems and ballads. The melody, rhythm and production make songs distinct.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "I–V–vi–IV", "b": "the classic order"}, {"a": "vi–IV–I–V", "b": "starts sad, ends hopeful"}, {"a": "I–vi–IV–V", "b": "1950s doo-wop"}]}
ex: Move it to G major | I = G, V = D, vi = Em, IV = C | Same feel, different pitch
### Is it lazy?
Not really. Classical music used shared patterns too: Pachelbel's Canon, from around 1700, rests on a chord sequence that has been reused endlessly. A familiar frame lets listeners relax and focus on the melody and voice.
!? Because the four chords repeat, a beginner who learns C, G, Am and F on guitar or ukulele can play along with hundreds of songs.
> Four chords give a familiar emotional arc; the song is in what you put on top.

# Rhythm and tempo
Rhythm is how sounds are arranged in time: long and short, strong and weak. Underneath most music is a steady **beat**, the pulse you tap your foot to. Tempo is how fast that beat goes, measured in beats per minute (BPM).
### Metre and time signatures
Beats are grouped into bars. A time signature tells you how many beats are in each bar.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "4/4", "b": "most pop, rock, hip hop"}, {"a": "3/4", "b": "waltz: ONE two three"}, {"a": "6/8", "b": "lilting, two groups of three"}, {"a": "7/8", "b": "Balkan folk, uneven"}]}
### Typical tempos
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "beats per minute (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Slow ballad", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Pop song", "v": 115.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "House music", "v": 125.0}, {"l": "Drum and bass", "v": 172.0}]}
Classical scores use Italian words: *largo* (very slow), *andante* (walking pace), *allegro* (fast and lively) and *presto* (very fast). The mechanical metronome, patented by Johann Maelzel in 1815, let composers like Beethoven write exact BPM numbers.
ex: Song at 120 BPM | 120 ÷ 60 = 2 beats per second | One beat every 0.5 s
### Syncopation and groove
**Syncopation** puts accents where you don't expect them, between the main beats. It's the heart of funk, samba, reggae and jazz, and it's what makes people want to move. Rhythm is also physical: we naturally move in time to a beat, an ability shared by very few animals.
!? Tempos around 120 BPM are among the most common in dance music; it's close to a comfortable brisk walking pace.
> Rhythm organises time; tempo sets its speed, and syncopation gives it groove.

# Instrument families
A symphony orchestra has roughly 70–100 players, grouped into four families by how they make sound. The groups also sit in sections, which is why an orchestra looks so organised from the audience.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Strings", "b": "bowed or plucked strings"}, {"a": "Woodwind", "b": "air across a hole or reed"}, {"a": "Brass", "b": "buzzing lips in a mouthpiece"}, {"a": "Percussion", "b": "struck, shaken or scraped"}]}
### Some surprises
• The **flute** is a woodwind even when made of metal, because the sound comes from air split across an edge, not buzzing lips
• The **saxophone** is brass-bodied but uses a reed, so it counts as woodwind
• The **piano** has strings but they're struck by hammers, so it's sometimes listed with percussion or keyboards
### Where they sit
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Percussion at the back", "Brass behind woodwind", "Woodwind in the middle", "Strings at the front"]}
The strings are the biggest section, often 50–60 players, because each string instrument is relatively quiet. Brass is powerful, so a few players can fill a hall.
### A scientific system
Musicologists use a broader system from 1914 by Erich von Hornbostel and Curt Sachs, which works for instruments worldwide:

> Instruments are grouped by how they make vibrations: strings, air, lips or striking.

# Classical eras
"Classical music" loosely means Western art music from the last thousand years. Historians split it into eras, each with its own sound, instruments and social role. The dates are approximate and styles overlap.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1600", "l": "Baroque: Bach, Vivaldi"}, {"y": "1750", "l": "Classical: Haydn, Mozart"}, {"y": "1820", "l": "Romantic: Chopin, Verdi"}, {"y": "1900", "l": "Modern: Stravinsky"}]}
### What changed
• **Baroque** (about 1600–1750): ornate interweaving lines (counterpoint), the harpsichord, opera's birth. Bach's death in 1750 is a handy end date.
• **Classical** (about 1750–1820): clear melodies, balanced phrases, the symphony and string quartet take shape.
• **Romantic** (about 1820–1900): big emotions, bigger orchestras, national styles, star performers like Liszt.
• **20th century onwards**: experiments with rhythm, dissonance and electronics; Stravinsky's *Rite of Spring* caused uproar at its 1913 Paris premiere.
### Beethoven, the bridge
Beethoven (1770–1827) started in the Classical style of Haydn and Mozart and pushed it into Romanticism, with longer, more personal works. He kept composing after going almost completely deaf, including the Ninth Symphony (1824), whose final theme is now the anthem of the European Union.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Classical era", "items": ["balance, clarity", "orchestra of ~40"]}, "r": {"t": "Romantic era", "items": ["emotion, drama", "orchestra of 80+"]}}
### Portuguese composers
Carlos Seixas (1704–1742) wrote brilliant Baroque keyboard sonatas in Lisbon. Later names include João Domingos Bomtempo, the pianist José Vianna da Motta and the 20th-century composer Fernando Lopes-Graça.
!! "Classical" means all old orchestral music => Strictly, the Classical era is just about 1750–1820, the time of Haydn and Mozart
> Each era reflects its age: Baroque ornament, Classical balance, Romantic emotion, modern experiment.

# Fado
Fado means "fate". It emerged in the working-class neighbourhoods of Lisbon, like Alfama and Mouraria, around the 1820s–1840s, among sailors, taverns and the poor. Its exact roots are debated: suggested influences include Afro-Brazilian dances, Portuguese folk song and the music of sailors.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1840s", "l": "Maria Severa, Lisbon"}, {"y": "1940s", "l": "Amália's rise"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Amália in Pantheon"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "UNESCO listing"}]}
### How it sounds
A classic fado has a singer, the **fadista**, with a **Portuguese guitar** (12 metal strings, bright and shimmering) and a classical guitar, called *viola* in Portugal, keeping the rhythm and bass. Songs are often slow, with dramatic pauses and ornamented phrasing. The mood is **saudade**: a deep longing for something absent or lost, which may never return.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Fadista: voice and story", "Portuguese guitar: melody", "Viola: rhythm and bass"]}
### Amália and after
Amália Rodrigues (1920–1999) took fado to concert halls from Paris to New York and recorded with poets' texts, raising its status. When she died, Portugal declared three days of national mourning, and she was moved to the National Pantheon in 2001. Today Mariza, Ana Moura, Carminho and Camané carry it on, often mixing in new sounds.
### Lisbon and Coimbra
Lisbon fado is the best known. Coimbra has its own student tradition, sung by men and linked to the university.
!? Under the Estado Novo, fado was sometimes promoted as a national symbol, which made some people after 1974 suspicious of it, until a revival from the 1990s.
> Fado turns saudade, a longing for what is gone, into a public art.

# Why music moves us
Music has no obvious survival value, yet every human culture makes it, and it can give us chills. Neuroscience has started to explain why. In a 2011 study, Valorie Salimpoor and colleagues in Montreal scanned people listening to music that gave them shivers. They found **dopamine**, a brain chemical linked to reward and motivation, released at two moments: during the build-up and at the peak itself.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Music sets up a pattern", "Brain predicts what's next", "Dopamine during build-up", "Peak: release and chills"]}
### Prediction is the key
The brain constantly guesses what comes next. Music plays with those guesses. A familiar progression creates expectations; a delay, an unexpected chord or a sudden silence builds tension; the resolution delivers a reward. Too predictable is boring, too random is confusing. The pleasure lives in between.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Expectation", "Tension or delay", "Resolution", "Reward"]}
### What triggers chills
• A sudden change in loudness, especially a quiet passage giving way to full sound
• A new voice or instrument entering
• An unexpected harmony that then resolves
• A singer's voice breaking or rising at an emotional moment
### More than chemistry
Music also works through memory (a song tied to a summer or a person), the body (rhythm makes us move and synchronise) and social bonds (singing together, in a choir or a stadium, makes people feel closer).
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why music moves us", "items": ["Prediction and reward", "Memory", "Body and rhythm", "Shared experience"]}
!? Not everyone gets musical chills: some people experience little pleasure from music at all, a condition researchers call specific musical anhedonia.
> Music moves us by playing with our predictions: tension, delay and release.

# The oldest stories
The *Epic of Gilgamesh* comes from Mesopotamia, in today's Iraq. Gilgamesh was probably a real king of the city of Uruk around 2700 BCE. Poems about him were written in Sumerian by about 2100 BCE, and around 1200 BCE a scholar named Sîn-lēqi-unninni shaped the best-known version: twelve clay tablets in the Akkadian language, written in cuneiform, wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.2700BC", "l": "King Gilgamesh of Uruk"}, {"y": "c.2100BC", "l": "Sumerian poems"}, {"y": "c.1200BC", "l": "Twelve-tablet version"}, {"y": "1872", "l": "Flood story decoded"}]}
### The story
Gilgamesh is a proud, restless king. The gods create a wild man, Enkidu, as his equal. They become close friends and kill monsters together, but Enkidu is punished with death. Grief-stricken and terrified of dying, Gilgamesh travels to the end of the world to find Utnapishtim, the one man granted eternal life. He wins a plant that restores youth, but a snake steals it while he bathes. He returns home and finds meaning in the great walls of his city.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Proud king", "Friendship with Enkidu", "Loss and fear of death", "Search for immortality", "Acceptance"]}
### A sensational rediscovery
The tablets lay buried for over 2,000 years. Many were dug up in the 1850s from the library of King Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. In 1872 George Smith, working at the British Museum, read the eleventh tablet and found a flood story strikingly similar to Noah's ark. It caused a sensation in Victorian Britain.
!? New pieces keep turning up: in 2015 a museum in Sulaymaniyah, Iraq, identified a tablet that added about 20 lines to the fifth chapter.
> Humanity's oldest great story is about what we all face: love, loss and the fact that we will die.

# Homer
The *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* are the founding works of Western literature. Ancient Greeks credited both to a poet called Homer, but we know almost nothing certain about him. Most scholars date the poems to roughly the 8th–7th centuries BCE, growing out of a much older tradition of sung storytelling.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Iliad", "items": ["war at Troy", "Achilles' anger", "about 15,700 lines"]}, "r": {"t": "Odyssey", "items": ["journey home", "Odysseus' cunning", "about 12,100 lines"]}}
### Not the whole war
The *Iliad* covers only a few weeks in the tenth year of the Trojan War, centred on Achilles' rage and its terrible cost. The famous wooden horse barely appears in it; it's recounted in the *Odyssey* and by later poets like Virgil. The *Odyssey* follows Odysseus through monsters, temptations and shipwreck, then shows him reclaiming his home in disguise.
### How we know they were oral
In the 1930s the American scholar Milman Parry and his student Albert Lord recorded singers in Yugoslavia who performed epics of thousands of lines from memory. They found the singers relied on stock phrases that fit the rhythm, just as Homer does with phrases like "rosy-fingered dawn" and "swift-footed Achilles".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bards sing and improvise", "Stock phrases fit the metre", "Stories pass down generations", "Written down, c. 700s BC"]}
• The metre, **dactylic hexameter**, has six beats per line
• Repeated epithets gave singers time to think of what came next
• Listeners enjoyed the familiar phrases, like a chorus
!? A legend says Odysseus (Ulysses in Latin) founded Lisbon, and some claim the old name Olisipo comes from him. It's a myth, but Pessoa used it in his poetry.
> Homer's epics are the written trace of a living, oral art.

# The hero's journey
In 1949 the American scholar Joseph Campbell published *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*. Comparing myths from many cultures, he argued that hero stories share a deep pattern, which he called the **monomyth**. Campbell described 17 stages; most people now use simpler versions.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Ordinary world", "Call to adventure", "Trials and allies", "Ordeal and reward", "Return changed"]}
### The core pattern
• **Departure**: the hero is called away, often refuses at first, then meets a mentor
• **Initiation**: they cross into a strange world, face tests, and hit a moment of near-death
• **Return**: they come back with something, such as wisdom, an object or a cure, that changes their community
### From myth to Hollywood
George Lucas said Campbell's book helped him shape *Star Wars* (1977): Luke leaves his farm, meets the mentor Obi-Wan, enters a strange world and returns a hero. In 1985 the Disney story consultant Christopher Vogler wrote a short memo turning Campbell into a 12-step guide for screenwriters. It spread through the industry and influenced films like *The Lion King*.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Call to adventure", "b": "Luke finds Leia's message"}, {"a": "Mentor", "b": "Obi-Wan Kenobi"}, {"a": "Ordeal", "b": "the Death Star"}, {"a": "Return", "b": "hero of the rebellion"}]}
### Limits
Critics say the pattern is so general it fits almost anything, that Campbell chose examples to suit his theory, and that it centres a lone (often male) hero. Many great stories are about communities, quiet change or failure.
!! Every good story follows the hero's journey => It's one useful pattern among many; plenty of classics ignore it
> The hero's journey is a map of change: leave, be tested, come back different.

# Shakespeare
William Shakespeare was born in Stratford-upon-Avon in 1564 and worked in London as an actor, playwright and part-owner of a theatre company. He wrote or co-wrote around 39 plays: comedies, histories, tragedies and late romances. Seven years after his death, two fellow actors gathered 36 plays into the **First Folio** (1623). Without it, about 18 plays, including *Macbeth* and *The Tempest*, might have been lost.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1564", "l": "Born, Stratford"}, {"y": "1599", "l": "Globe Theatre opens"}, {"y": "1616", "l": "Dies, aged 52"}, {"y": "1623", "l": "First Folio"}]}
### What made him different
• **Language**: he bent words into new uses, turning nouns into verbs and inventing compounds
• **Character**: figures like Hamlet seem to think aloud, changing as we watch
• **Range**: kings and gravediggers, farce and despair, often in the same play
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Shakespeare's plays", "items": ["Comedies", "Histories", "Tragedies", "Romances"]}
### Words and phrases
The Oxford English Dictionary records many words first in Shakespeare, such as "eyeball", "lonely" and "swagger". Some he surely coined; others he may simply be the first surviving written source for. Phrases like "wild-goose chase", "break the ice" and "heart of gold" either began with him or became popular through him.
### Shakespeare and Cervantes
Shakespeare and Miguel de Cervantes, author of *Don Quixote*, both died in April 1616, dates that are often given as the same day. That's why UNESCO chose 23 April as World Book Day, though Spain and England then used different calendars, so they actually died about ten days apart.
!! Shakespeare invented thousands of words => Estimates have shrunk as older texts were searched; many "firsts" were already in use
> Shakespeare's power lies in characters who think and change, written in a language he stretched to fit them.

# Os Lusíadas
Luís Vaz de Camões (about 1524–1580) wrote Portugal's national epic, published in 1572. Its title means "The Lusiads", the sons of Lusus, the mythical ancestor of the Portuguese. Modelled on Homer and Virgil's *Aeneid*, it uses Vasco da Gama's voyage to India (1497–1499) as a frame to tell the whole history of Portugal.

### Structure
Each stanza has eight lines of ten syllables, rhyming ABABABCC, a form called **ottava rima** borrowed from Italian epics. The famous opening, "As armas e os barões assinalados", announces the subject: the arms and the famous men.
### Famous episodes

The Old Man of Restelo, who curses the lust for fame as the ships leave Belém, shows Camões was no simple cheerleader. The epic celebrates the voyages but also counts their cost.
### A life of adventure
Camões lost an eye fighting in North Africa, was jailed after a street fight in Lisbon, and spent about 17 years in Asia. Tradition says he was shipwrecked near the Mekong delta and swam ashore holding his manuscript above the water. He died poor in Lisbon in 1580, the year Portugal lost its independence to Spain.

# The novel
A novel is a long fictional story in prose, usually focused on individual characters and their inner lives. Long prose fiction existed earlier: Japan's *Tale of Genji*, written by the court lady Murasaki Shikibu around 1000 CE, is sometimes called the first novel. But the European novel took shape with Miguel de Cervantes' *Don Quixote* (Part 1 in 1605, Part 2 in 1615).
### Why Don Quixote counts as "modern"
An ageing gentleman reads so many chivalry romances that he decides to become a knight. Cervantes mocks the old romances while creating rounded, changing characters, comedy, and a playful awareness of being a book. In Part 2, characters have even read Part 1.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.1000", "l": "Tale of Genji"}, {"y": "1605", "l": "Don Quixote"}, {"y": "1719", "l": "Robinson Crusoe"}, {"y": "1888", "l": "Os Maias"}]}
### Why it grew
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Rise of the novel", "items": ["Printing cheaper", "More readers", "Middle-class leisure", "Serial publication"]}
Literacy spread, books became cheaper and circulating libraries lent them out. In the 19th century, many novels came out in weekly or monthly instalments, like a TV series. Dickens' readers waited eagerly for each part.
### The great 19th century
• **Jane Austen**: sharp social comedy, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813)
• **Charles Dickens**: London's poor and rich, *Great Expectations*
• **Leo Tolstoy**: history and families, *War and Peace* (1869)
• **Eça de Queirós**: Portuguese realism and satire, *Os Maias* (1888), set among Lisbon's elite
!? The word "novel" comes from the Italian *novella*, "a little new thing", a short tale. In Portuguese, *romance* keeps the older link to medieval romances.
> The novel lets us live inside other minds for hundreds of pages.

# Saramago
José Saramago (1922–2010) was born in Azinhaga, a village in the Ribatejo, to a poor rural family. He worked as a car mechanic, civil servant and journalist, and only found his great voice late: his breakthrough novels came when he was about 60. In 1998 he became the first, and so far only, writer in Portuguese to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1982", "l": "Memorial do Convento"}, {"y": "1991", "l": "Gospel novel row"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "Blindness"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "Nobel Prize"}]}
### The style
Saramago wrote very long sentences and paragraphs. Dialogue has no quotation marks: speakers are separated only by commas, with a capital letter marking each new voice. It feels like listening to a storyteller. At first it's demanding; after a few pages, many readers find it hypnotic.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Usual dialogue", "items": ["quotation marks", "new line per speaker"]}, "r": {"t": "Saramago", "items": ["commas only", "capital letter = new voice"]}}
### "What if" stories
Many of his novels start from one impossible premise and follow it seriously:
• *Blindness* (1995): a whole city goes blind, and society collapses
• *The Stone Raft* (1986): the Iberian Peninsula breaks off from Europe and drifts into the Atlantic
• *Death with Interruptions* (2005): people in one country stop dying
### Controversy
His novel *The Gospel According to Jesus Christ* (1991) angered the Catholic Church. In 1992 the Portuguese government removed it from a shortlist for a European literary prize. In protest, Saramago moved to Lanzarote in the Canary Islands, where he lived until his death.
!? The José Saramago Foundation is in Lisbon's Casa dos Bicos, and his ashes lie beneath an olive tree in front of it, brought from his home village.
> Saramago took one impossible idea and followed it, step by step, to show us how people really behave.

# Genres
Genres are families of stories that share expectations. When you pick up a crime novel, you expect a mystery and, usually, a solution. Genres help readers choose, help publishers sell, and give writers a set of conventions to follow or break.
### Ancient roots
The Greek philosopher Aristotle, in his *Poetics* (about 335 BCE), analysed tragedy, comedy and epic.

### Modern genres and their origins

• **Science fiction** asks "what if?" about science and society; Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* is often called the first
• **Crime** grew from Edgar Allan Poe's detective Dupin to Sherlock Holmes and Agatha Christie
• **Fantasy** builds whole worlds with their own rules, history and magic
• **Romance** is one of the best-selling genres of all
### Mixing and breaking

# Show, don't tell
"Show, don't tell" is the most repeated advice in writing classes. **Telling** names a feeling or fact directly. **Showing** gives the reader actions, details and dialogue, and lets them work out the feeling for themselves. Because readers do the work, they feel more involved.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "She was nervous.", "r": "She checked her phone a third time."}, {"w": "The house was old.", "r": "Paint flaked off the swollen door."}]}
### Why it works
When you read concrete details, your mind builds a scene and infers emotions, as it does with real people. That inference creates engagement. Abstract labels like "sad" or "beautiful" give the mind nothing to picture.
### The tools of showing
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Action: what characters do", "Dialogue: what they say (or don't)", "Senses: sound, smell, texture", "Specific objects and details"]}
### Origins
The advice is often attributed to the Russian writer Anton Chekhov. In an 1886 letter to his brother he urged him to suggest a moonlit night through small, precise details rather than describe it in general terms. The neat line usually quoted is a later paraphrase. Ernest Hemingway's "iceberg theory" pushed the idea further: leave most of the meaning below the surface.
### When telling is right
Telling is useful for speed. A novel can't show every moment, so writers summarise to skip time ("Three years passed") and save showing for the scenes that matter.
!! Good writing never tells => Writers balance both; telling moves the story on, showing makes key moments land
> Give the reader the evidence, and let them feel the conclusion.

# Why fiction matters
When you read fiction, you spend hours inside the thoughts of people unlike you. Many psychologists suspect this works like a flight simulator for social life: practice at imagining what others think and feel. That skill is called **theory of mind**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Enter a character's mind", "Imagine their feelings", "Practise perspective-taking", "Better at reading people?"]}
### What the studies say
The evidence is real but mixed, and worth stating honestly:
• **Long-term readers**: studies led by Keith Oatley and Raymond Mar in Toronto found that people who read more fiction tend to score better on tests of social understanding, but this is a correlation, and empathic people may simply choose fiction
• **Quick effect**: a 2013 study in *Science* by Kidd and Castano reported that reading a short piece of literary fiction boosted theory-of-mind scores immediately
• **Replication**: larger follow-up studies, including one in 2016, failed to reproduce that short-term effect

### Other benefits
Fiction may also help with "narrative transportation", being absorbed in a story, which can shift attitudes. Novels like *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) changed public debate on slavery. Reading also builds vocabulary and concentration, which matters at any age.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Fiction may build", "items": ["Empathy", "Vocabulary", "Focus", "Wider perspectives"]}
!? "Narrative transportation" is a real research term: the more absorbed people are in a story, the more their beliefs tend to shift towards the story's view.
> Fiction lets you rehearse being someone else, and that practice likely leaves a trace.

# The illusion of motion
A film is a strip of still photographs, called frames. Show them fast enough and the brain fills in the movement between them. Since the arrival of sound films in the late 1920s, the standard has been **24 frames per second**: fast enough for smooth motion, slow enough to save expensive film stock, and steady enough to keep the soundtrack in sync.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Camera takes still frames", "Projector shows them fast", "Brain fills the gaps", "We see smooth motion"]}
ex: A 2-hour film | 120 minutes × 60 seconds × 24 frames | = 172,800 separate images
### Why the brain is fooled
The old explanation was "persistence of vision", the idea that images linger on the retina. Researchers now think the main cause is how the brain processes motion: when a shape appears in slightly different places in quick succession, motion-detecting areas of the brain read it as a single moving object. This is called **apparent motion** or the beta movement.
### Hiding the flicker
At 24 frames per second you'd see flicker, so film projectors flash each frame two or three times, giving 48 or 72 flashes per second. The image is steady even though the motion still updates 24 times a second.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "frames per second", "items": [{"l": "Silent films", "v": 18.0, "t": "16–20"}, {"l": "Cinema", "v": 24.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "European TV", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "The Hobbit (HFR)", "v": 48.0}]}
European television used 25 frames per second because the electricity grid runs at 50 Hz. When Peter Jackson shot *The Hobbit* (2012) at 48 fps, many viewers found it looked too real, like video, showing how much 24 fps shapes the "film look".
!! Persistence of vision explains movies => It explains why we don't see darkness between frames; motion perception happens in the brain
> Cinema is a trick: still images, shown quickly, become motion inside your head.

# The birth of cinema
Many inventors raced to make moving pictures in the 1880s and 1890s. Thomas Edison's company created the **Kinetoscope** (1893), a cabinet where one person at a time peered through a hole. On 28 December 1895, Auguste and Louis Lumière held a public, paying screening in the basement of the Grand Café in Paris with their **Cinématographe**, a light, hand-cranked camera that also worked as a projector. That night is usually treated as cinema's birthday.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1893", "l": "Edison's Kinetoscope"}, {"y": "1895", "l": "Lumière screening"}, {"y": "1896", "l": "First Portuguese film"}, {"y": "1902", "l": "A Trip to the Moon"}]}
### What audiences saw
About ten short films, each under a minute: workers leaving a factory, a baby being fed, a gardener sprayed by a hose. The legend that viewers fled in terror from *The Arrival of a Train* is probably exaggerated, but the films truly astonished people.
### Méliès, the magician
Georges Méliès was a stage magician. He discovered that stopping the camera, changing something and restarting it could make people vanish. He built a glass studio and made hundreds of fantasy films. *A Trip to the Moon* (1902), about 14 minutes long, has the famous image of a rocket landing in the Moon's eye.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lumière", "items": ["real life, outdoors", "document the world"]}, "r": {"t": "Méliès", "items": ["studio sets, tricks", "invent new worlds"]}}
### Portugal joins in
In 1896 the Porto photographer Aurélio da Paz dos Reis filmed workers leaving the Confiança factory, Portugal's first film, inspired directly by the Lumières.
!? Louis Lumière is often said to have called cinema "an invention without a future", but there's no solid source for the remark.
> Cinema was born twice: once as a window on reality, once as a magic box.

# Silent era to sound
"Silent" films were never silent. Cinemas had a pianist, organist or full orchestra, and some films came with special scores. In Japan, live narrators called *benshi* explained and voiced the action. Actors told stories through faces, gestures and title cards.
### The Jazz Singer
Warner Bros. released *The Jazz Singer* in October 1927, using the **Vitaphone** system, which played sound from a disc synchronised with the projector. It's mostly a silent film with songs, plus a few short moments of spoken dialogue. Audiences were thrilled, and within about three years nearly all Hollywood films had sound.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1927", "l": "The Jazz Singer"}, {"y": "1929", "l": "Most studios convert"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "A Severa (Portugal)"}, {"y": "1936", "l": "Modern Times"}]}
### What sound changed
• **Cameras**: noisy cameras were put in soundproof booths, so early talkies often looked static
• **Actors**: voices now mattered; some stars with strong accents or thin voices lost their careers
• **Language**: silent films crossed borders by swapping title cards; talkies needed dubbing or subtitles
• **Film speed** was fixed at 24 fps so the sound would stay steady
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Silent films", "items": ["acting by gesture", "live music", "easy to export"]}, "r": {"t": "Talkies", "items": ["dialogue drives story", "recorded sound", "language barrier"]}}
### Chaplin holds out
Charlie Chaplin feared his Little Tramp would lose his universal appeal if he spoke. *City Lights* (1931) and *Modern Times* (1936) used music and sound effects but almost no dialogue. His first full talkie was *The Great Dictator* (1940).
!? Portugal's first sound feature, *A Severa* (1931) by Leitão de Barros, told the story of the legendary 19th-century fado singer Maria Severa.
> Sound gave cinema voices, and took away, for a while, its universal language.

# The language of shots
Every shot is a choice about distance, angle and movement, and each choice shapes how you feel. Filmmakers have a shared vocabulary for this, developed since the 1910s.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Extreme wide: tiny in landscape", "Wide: whole body and setting", "Medium: waist up, conversation", "Close-up: face and emotion", "Extreme close-up: eyes, detail"]}
### Angles carry meaning
• **Low angle** (looking up): the character seems powerful or threatening
• **High angle** (looking down): they look small, weak or trapped
• **Eye level**: neutral, equal
• **Dutch angle** (tilted): something is wrong or unstable
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Low angle", "b": "power, threat"}, {"a": "High angle", "b": "weakness, isolation"}, {"a": "Tilted frame", "b": "unease, madness"}, {"a": "Point of view", "b": "see through their eyes"}]}
### Conversations and the 180° rule
In a dialogue, filmmakers often alternate **over-the-shoulder** shots. To keep the viewer oriented, the camera stays on one side of an imaginary line between the two speakers: the **180-degree rule**. Cross it and the characters suddenly seem to face the same way, which confuses the eye. Some directors break it on purpose to signal conflict.
### Movement
A slow push towards a face signals a realisation. A pull-back can reveal that a character is alone. A handheld camera feels urgent and documentary-like; a smooth Steadicam glides like a ghost.
!? Sergio Leone's westerns cut from vast landscapes to extreme close-ups of eyes, using the jump in scale to build almost unbearable tension.
> Distance sets how close you feel; angle sets who has the power.

# Editing
Editing decides which shots appear, in what order and for how long. When done well, you barely notice it. Yet it controls rhythm, emotion and even meaning.
### The Kuleshov effect
Around 1918–1921, the Soviet filmmaker Lev Kuleshov joined a shot of the actor Ivan Mozzhukhin's neutral face with different shots: a bowl of soup, a child in a coffin, a woman on a sofa. Viewers reportedly praised the actor's subtle hunger, grief and desire, although the face was the same each time.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Neutral face", "+ bowl of soup", "Viewer sees hunger"]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Face + soup", "b": "hunger"}, {"a": "Face + coffin", "b": "grief"}, {"a": "Face + woman", "b": "desire"}]}
The original footage is lost and the anecdote may be polished, but modern experiments have found a real, if smaller, effect: context changes how we read faces.
### Two traditions
• **Continuity editing**, the Hollywood style, hides the cuts: cutting on movement and matching eyelines so time and space feel seamless
• **Montage**, championed by Sergei Eisenstein in *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), crashes images together to create ideas and shock, as in the Odessa Steps massacre
### Faster and faster
Film scholars have measured average shot length in Hollywood films falling from roughly 8–10 seconds in the 1930s–40s to around 3–4 seconds in many 2000s action films.
!? Stanley Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968) has a famous "match cut" from a bone thrown in the air to a spacecraft, jumping millions of years in a single splice.
> Meaning in film lives between shots: the cut tells you how to read what you see.

# Three-act structure
The idea that a story has a beginning, middle and end goes back to Aristotle. In 1979 the screenwriting teacher Syd Field turned it into a practical formula in his book *Screenplay*, and it became Hollywood's standard. A screenplay page lasts about one minute on screen, so writers plan acts in pages.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of a 120-minute film (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Act 1: set-up", "v": 25.0, "t": "~30 min"}, {"l": "Act 2: conflict", "v": 50.0, "t": "~60 min", "hi": true}, {"l": "Act 3: resolution", "v": 25.0, "t": "~30 min"}]}
### Key turning points
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Inciting incident", "Plot point 1: no return", "Midpoint: stakes rise", "Plot point 2: lowest point", "Climax"]}
• **Act 1** introduces the world, the hero and what they want; the inciting incident disrupts normal life
• **Act 2** is the longest: obstacles grow, and a midpoint often changes the game
• **Act 3** forces a final confrontation and shows the new normal
ex: *Jaws* | Act 1: a shark attack, the beach stays open | Act 2: more attacks, the hunt begins | Act 3: the final fight at sea
### Other shapes
Plays were often divided into five acts, as Gustav Freytag described in 1863. Chinese, Korean and Japanese storytelling uses a four-part shape, **kishōtenketsu** in Japanese, where the third part introduces a twist rather than a conflict. Many arthouse films resist neat structure altogether.
!! Three acts is a strict formula => It's a loose description of rising tension and release; good films bend it constantly
> Three acts simply means: make us care, make it hard, then make it matter.

# Sound and music
Film sound has three main layers: dialogue, sound effects and music. Sound designers also distinguish **diegetic** sound, which the characters can hear (a radio, a band in the scene), from **non-diegetic** sound, such as the score, which only the audience hears.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Diegetic", "items": ["in the story world", "a radio, footsteps"]}, "r": {"t": "Non-diegetic", "items": ["only for the audience", "the score, narration"]}}
### Two notes from Jaws
For *Jaws* (1975), John Williams wrote a theme built on just two alternating low notes, a semitone apart, speeding up as the shark approaches. Because the mechanical shark kept breaking, Spielberg showed it rarely, letting the music stand in for it. Your imagination did the rest.
### Themes that tell stories
Composers often borrow the idea of the **leitmotif** from the opera composer Richard Wagner: a short musical theme tied to a character or idea. *Star Wars* (1977) has themes for Luke, Leia and Darth Vader, so the music can tell you who matters before they appear.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Leitmotif: a theme per character", "Stingers: a sudden note for a shock", "Silence: tension through absence", "Foley: everyday sounds, re-recorded"]}
### The invisible craft
**Foley** artists, named after Jack Foley of Universal Studios, re-create footsteps, rustling clothes and punches in a studio, often with odd objects: celery for breaking bones, cornflour for footsteps in snow. Ben Burtt made the *Star Wars* lightsaber hum from an old projector motor and a TV set's interference.
!? Many viewers think the shower scene in *Psycho* (1960) is terrifying because of what they see, but Hitchcock reportedly planned it without music; Bernard Herrmann's shrieking strings changed his mind.
> Sound reaches your emotions before your eyes have worked out what is happening.

# Animation
Animation creates movement one frame at a time. Instead of filming continuous action, animators make each image, then film or render them in sequence. The illusion of life depends on small changes between frames, plus principles like "squash and stretch", which make things feel weighty and alive.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Animation methods", "items": ["Hand-drawn (cel)", "Stop-motion", "Computer (CGI)", "Cut-out and others"]}
### Milestones
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1937", "l": "Snow White (Disney)"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "Toy Story"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Spirited Away"}, {"y": "2023", "l": "Ice Merchants nominated"}]}
Disney's *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* (1937) was the first American feature-length cel-animated film; some earlier features existed in Argentina and Germany. Pixar's *Toy Story* (1995) was the first feature made entirely by computer. Its frames took hours each to render on a farm of over 100 machines.
### Counting frames
ex: Hand-drawn films often use "twos" | 24 fps ÷ 2 = 12 new drawings per second | 80-minute film ≈ 58,000 drawings
### Stop-motion
In stop-motion, real puppets or clay figures are moved a tiny bit between each photo. Aardman's Wallace and Gromit and Laika's *Coraline* (2009) work this way. A good day's work might produce only a few seconds of finished film.
### Portuguese animation
Portugal has a strong tradition of short animated films. The Cinanima festival in Espinho has run since 1977. Regina Pessoa's *Tragic Story with Happy Ending* (2005) won many international prizes, and João Gonzalez's *Ice Merchants* (2022) won at Cannes and became the first Portuguese film nominated for an Oscar.

# Portuguese cinema
Portuguese cinema is small in budget but large in reputation, especially among critics and festivals. It is known for long takes, literary dialogue, poetry and a patient look at people on society's edges.

# Watch like a filmmaker
Most of what makes a film work is invisible on first viewing. Training your attention on a few elements turns any film into a lesson in technique. Try one at a time; noticing everything at once is exhausting.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Shot length: count the cuts", "Music: when does it begin?", "Camera: where, and how high?", "Light and colour: what mood?", "Sound: what's missing?"]}
### A simple exercise
ex: Pick a 1-minute dialogue scene | Count the cuts (say 15) | Average shot = 60 ÷ 15 = 4 seconds
Then compare it with a scene from a film by Manoel de Oliveira or Pedro Costa, where a single shot can last minutes. Long takes ask you to look; fast cutting tells you where to look.
### Big decisions
When a character makes a key choice, watch the camera. Does it move closer? Go silent? Hold on the face after the dialogue ends? Directors often save their closest shot and quietest moment for the turning point.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice a strong feeling", "Pause or rewind", "Ask: what caused it?", "Shot, cut, sound or light?"]}
### Watch it twice
On a first viewing you follow the story. On the second you can watch the craft: how a scene plants a clue, how colour shifts as a character changes, how sound bridges two scenes. Turning the sound off for a few minutes shows how much the music was doing.

# Classical orders
An "order" is more than a column. It's a complete system: base, shaft, capital (the top), and the beam structure above, the entablature, all in set proportions. The Greeks developed three orders; the Romans added two more, the plain Tuscan and the Composite, which mixes Ionic scrolls with Corinthian leaves.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Doric", "b": "plain cushion capital"}, {"a": "Ionic", "b": "scroll-shaped volutes"}, {"a": "Corinthian", "b": "acanthus leaves"}, {"a": "Tuscan, Composite", "b": "Roman additions"}]}
### Proportion and character
Each order has its own slenderness, measured as column height to diameter. The Roman architect Vitruvius, writing around 30–15 BCE, even gave them personalities: Doric was manly and sturdy, Ionic matronly, Corinthian like a slender young girl.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "column height ÷ diameter (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Doric", "v": 6.0, "t": "~5–7"}, {"l": "Ionic", "v": 9.0, "t": "~9"}, {"l": "Corinthian", "v": 10.0, "t": "~10", "hi": true}]}
### Famous examples
• **Doric**: the Parthenon in Athens (447–432 BCE)
• **Ionic**: the Erechtheion beside it, with its porch of statue-columns
• **Corinthian**: Rome's Pantheon portico, and the Roman Temple of Évora in Portugal, from the 1st century CE
### A language that never died
Renaissance architects revived the orders, and they spread across the world in banks, parliaments, museums and churches. When a building wants to look serious and permanent, it often reaches for columns.
!? Vitruvius tells a story that the Corinthian capital was inspired by a basket left on a girl's grave, around which an acanthus plant grew.
> The classical orders are a visual grammar that architects have reused for 2,500 years.

# The arch
Stone is very strong when squeezed (compression) but weak when pulled or bent (tension). A flat stone beam over a gap cracks easily, so Greek temples needed columns close together. An **arch** solves this: its wedge-shaped stones, called voussoirs, press against each other, so every stone is in compression.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Load presses down", "Wedges push on each other", "Force turns sideways", "Thrust carried to ground"]}
### The catch: thrust
An arch pushes outwards at its feet. Without something to resist that sideways **thrust**, such as thick walls, neighbouring arches or buttresses, it spreads and collapses. Romans placed arches side by side in aqueducts and bridges so the thrusts cancelled out.
### Roman concrete
The Romans mixed lime with volcanic ash called **pozzolana**, which hardens even underwater. With it they could pour vaults and domes. The Pantheon, completed around 125 CE under Emperor Hadrian, has a dome 43.3 m wide with a round opening, the oculus, about 8–9 m across at the top.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "43.3 m", "l": "Pantheon dome width"}, {"v": "~1,900", "l": "years standing"}, {"v": "0", "l": "steel reinforcement"}]}
### How the dome stays up
The builders made the concrete lighter as it rose: heavy basalt at the base, then brick, then light pumice near the top. The walls are about 6 m thick at the bottom, thinning towards the oculus, and sunken square panels (coffers) remove more weight.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Top: light pumice", "Middle: brick and tufa", "Base: heavy stone, ~6 m"]}
!? Lisbon's Águas Livres Aqueduct (completed in the 18th century) has a stone arch about 65 m high over the Alcântara valley, and it survived the 1755 earthquake.
> An arch turns a crushing weight into sideways pushes, and stone is happy to be pushed.

# Gothic
Gothic architecture began around 1140 when Abbot Suger rebuilt the choir of the Abbey of Saint-Denis, near Paris. He wanted a church flooded with coloured light, which he saw as a reflection of the divine. Over the next century, cathedrals at Chartres, Paris, Reims and Amiens pushed the style higher and lighter.
### Three inventions working together
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Gothic structure", "items": ["Pointed arch", "Ribbed vault", "Flying buttress", "Big windows"]}
• **Pointed arch**: pushes less sideways than a round arch and can be made any height, so bays of different widths still line up
• **Ribbed vault**: stone ribs gather the weight of the ceiling into a few points
• **Flying buttress**: an external arm of stone that catches the outward thrust and carries it down, away from the wall
With the weight channelled into a stone skeleton, the walls in between no longer held anything up, so they could become windows.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Romanesque", "items": ["round arches", "thick walls", "small windows"]}, "r": {"t": "Gothic", "items": ["pointed arches", "stone skeleton", "walls of glass"]}}
### Pushing the limits
Builders competed for height. The choir vault at Beauvais rose to about 48 m, but part of it collapsed in 1284 and had to be rebuilt with more supports.
### Gothic in Portugal
The Monastery of Alcobaça, begun in 1178, is an austere early Gothic church holding the tombs of King Pedro I and Inês de Castro. The Monastery of Batalha was built after the victory at Aljubarrota (1385) and mixes Portuguese and English influences.
!! "Gothic" was what its builders called it => Renaissance critics later used the word as an insult, linking it to the "barbarian" Goths
> Gothic builders turned walls into a stone skeleton, freeing the spaces between for light.

# Manueline style
The Manueline style flourished during and after the reign of King Manuel I (1495–1521), at the peak of Portugal's maritime expansion. Wealth from the spice trade with India paid for spectacular buildings. The name itself is later: it was coined in the 19th century.
### Symbols of the sea and the crown
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Armillary sphere", "b": "Manuel's emblem, navigation"}, {"a": "Cross of Christ", "b": "Order that funded voyages"}, {"a": "Twisted ropes", "b": "ships and the sea"}, {"a": "Coral, seaweed", "b": "the ocean world"}]}
The structure is mostly late Gothic, with ribbed vaults and pointed arches. What's new is the decoration, which turns stone into knotted rope, shells, exotic plants and nautical instruments.
### Masterpieces
• **Jerónimos Monastery**, Lisbon, begun in 1501–02 by Diogo de Boitaca and continued by João de Castilho; its church columns spread out into the vault like palm trees
• **Belém Tower** (about 1514–1520) by Francisco de Arruda, guarding the Tagus estuary
• **Convent of Christ, Tomar**: its window, attributed to Diogo de Arruda, is a riot of ropes, roots and coral
• **Batalha's Unfinished Chapels**: a monumental Manueline doorway open to the sky

!? Belém Tower has a carved rhinoceros head, thought to record the live rhino sent to Manuel I from India in 1515, the same animal Albrecht Dürer made famous in a woodcut.
> The Manueline style carved the Age of Discovery into stone: ropes, spheres and the sea.

# Azulejos
The word *azulejo* probably comes from the Arabic *al-zulaij*, "small polished stone". It has nothing to do with *azul* (blue), despite appearances. Portugal first imported tiles from Seville in the 15th century, in Moorish geometric patterns. By the 16th century Portuguese workshops were making their own, using Italian and Flemish techniques.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1500s", "l": "Moorish tiles, Seville"}, {"y": "1500s", "l": "Lisbon workshops start"}, {"y": "c.1700", "l": "Blue-and-white boom"}, {"y": "1800s", "l": "Tiled house façades"}]}
### How a tile is made
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Shape and fire clay", "Coat in white tin glaze", "Paint with oxides", "Fire again, ~1,000 °C"]}
Blue comes from **cobalt oxide**, which turns a deep blue in the kiln. Chinese porcelain and Dutch Delftware made blue-and-white fashionable in the late 17th century, and Portuguese painters created huge figurative panels showing saints, battles, hunting scenes and city views.
### Tiles everywhere
• **Churches and palaces**: whole walls telling stories, like a comic strip
• **House façades**: from the 19th century, tiles protected walls from rain and kept them cool

Thefts of antique tiles became a serious problem. The SOS Azulejo project, launched in 2007, documents stolen tiles, and since 2013 Lisbon has banned demolishing tiled façades without permission.

> Azulejos turn walls into storybooks and are one of Portugal's most distinctive arts.

# Pombaline Baixa
On the morning of 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day, a huge earthquake struck Lisbon, probably around magnitude 8.5–9. A tsunami followed about 40 minutes later, and fires burned for days. Estimates of the dead in Lisbon range widely, often given as 30,000–50,000. Much of the city centre was destroyed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earthquake, 1 Nov 1755", "Tsunami and fires", "Pombal takes charge", "Grid plan rebuilt"]}
### A planned city
The king's chief minister, later the **Marquis of Pombal**, put engineers Manuel da Maia, Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel in charge. They swept away the medieval maze between Rossio and the riverside square, now Praça do Comércio, and laid out a rational grid of wide streets with uniform buildings. Parts were standardised and prefabricated to speed construction.
### The gaiola
Inside the masonry walls sits a 3D timber frame, the **gaiola** ("cage"), braced with diagonal crosses. When the ground shakes, the wood flexes and holds the structure together while the masonry absorbs and dissipates energy.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Timber cage", "b": "flexes instead of cracking"}, {"a": "Diagonal crosses", "b": "resist sideways shaking"}, {"a": "Firewalls", "b": "stop fire spreading"}, {"a": "Standard parts", "b": "faster rebuilding"}]}
Tradition says the design was tested by having soldiers march around a model to simulate shaking.
### Birth of seismology
Pombal also sent a questionnaire to every parish: when did the shaking start, how long did it last, did the sea rise? These answers make the 1755 quake one of the first scientifically studied earthquakes.
!? Many Pombaline buildings have since been weakened by renovations that cut through the timber frame to open up shops or add floors.
> Lisbon's Baixa was rebuilt as one of the world's first earthquake-conscious city plans.

# Form follows function
In 1896 the American architect Louis Sullivan wrote that "form ever follows function", arguing that a building's shape should express its purpose. His tall Chicago office blocks were early skyscrapers. In the early 20th century, the phrase became a slogan of **modernism**.

### The modernist creed
• **Adolf Loos** argued around 1910 that ornament was a waste of labour and money
• The **Bauhaus** school in Germany (1919–1933) united art, craft and industry
• **Le Corbusier**, in *Towards an Architecture* (1923), praised ships, planes and cars as models of design
• **Mies van der Rohe** made glass and steel boxes of extreme simplicity
### Le Corbusier's five points
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Pilotis: raise it on columns", "Free plan: no load-bearing walls", "Free façade", "Long horizontal windows", "Roof garden"]}
Reinforced concrete made these possible: if columns carry the load, walls and windows can go anywhere.
### The backlash
Critics said modernism produced cold, repetitive buildings, especially in mass housing. In 1966 Robert Venturi answered Mies' "less is more" with "less is a bore", launching postmodernism's return to colour, history and wit.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Modernism", "items": ["function first", "no ornament"]}, "r": {"t": "Postmodernism", "items": ["meaning and wit", "history returns"]}}
!? In Lisbon, the Gulbenkian Foundation's headquarters and museum (1969) is one of Portugal's finest modernist buildings, with gardens designed as part of the architecture.

# Good design
Dieter Rams, born in 1932, was head of design at the German electronics company Braun from 1961 to 1995. His radios, record players, shavers and calculators became models of clean, functional design. Around 1980 he summed up his approach in **ten principles**.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Innovative, useful, aesthetic", "Understandable, unobtrusive", "Honest, long-lasting", "Thorough, eco-friendly", "As little design as possible"]}
### What the principles mean
• **Useful**: a product exists to be used, and design should serve that
• **Understandable**: ideally, an object explains itself without a manual
• **Honest**: it shouldn't pretend to be more valuable or powerful than it is
• **Long-lasting**: avoid fashion that will look dated next year
• **As little design as possible**: his motto was "Weniger, aber besser", "less, but better"
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Add features to impress", "r": "Remove all but the essential"}, {"w": "Follow this year's trend", "r": "Design to last decades"}]}
### His influence
Rams' Braun ET 66 calculator (1987) is often compared to the calculator app of early iPhones, and Apple's designers have openly admired his work. His 606 shelving system, designed in 1960 for Vitsœ, is still made today.
### Design and sustainability
Rams increasingly stressed that good design should reduce waste. Products that last, can be repaired and don't go out of style are better for the planet than cheap, disposable ones. The EU's right-to-repair rules move in the same direction.
!? A 1956 Braun radio-record player Rams worked on, the SK 4, was nicknamed "Snow White's coffin" because of its clear plastic lid.
> Good design is mostly about what you take away.

# Golden ratio myths
The golden ratio, written φ (phi), is about 1.618. Divide a line so that the whole is to the longer part as the longer part is to the shorter part, and you get φ. It has real mathematical charms: it is linked to the Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…), whose successive ratios approach 1.618.
= φ = (1 + √5) ÷ 2 ≈ 1.618
ex: 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6 | 13 ÷ 8 = 1.625 | 21 ÷ 13 ≈ 1.615 | getting closer to 1.618
### Where it's real
• **Plants**: sunflower seed heads and pine cones often show Fibonacci numbers of spirals, a result of efficient packing
• **Geometry**: the regular pentagon and five-pointed star are full of golden ratios
• **Deliberate use**: Le Corbusier built his Modulor proportion system (1948) on it, and some modern artists used it on purpose
### Where it's a myth
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "The Parthenon is golden", "r": "Only if you choose the points"}, {"w": "Nautilus shells are golden", "r": "Their spiral ratio is ~1.33"}]}
With any complex shape, you can find some pair of measurements close to 1.6 if you look hard enough. The Parthenon's ratio depends on which edges you measure. There's no evidence the Greeks designed it with φ in mind, or that Leonardo planned the Mona Lisa around it.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.0, "hi": 2.0, "unit": "long ÷ short side", "items": [{"l": "A4 paper", "v": 1.414}, {"l": "Golden ratio", "v": 1.618, "hi": true}, {"l": "16:9 screen", "v": 1.778}]}
### Do we prefer it?
In the 1870s Gustav Fechner reported that people preferred golden rectangles. Later studies have been inconsistent: preferences vary, and many people pick rectangles from about 1.5 to 1.8.

# Leonardo da Vinci
Leonardo was born in 1452 near the Tuscan town of Vinci, the illegitimate son of a notary. As a teenager he trained in Florence in the workshop of the sculptor and painter Andrea del Verrocchio. Legend says that when Verrocchio saw the angel Leonardo painted in his *Baptism of Christ*, he gave up painting.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1452", "l": "Born near Vinci"}, {"y": "1482", "l": "Moves to Milan"}, {"y": "c.1503", "l": "Begins Mona Lisa"}, {"y": "1519", "l": "Dies in France"}]}
### More engineer than painter?
When he wrote to the Duke of Milan around 1482 offering his services, Leonardo listed bridges, weapons, siege machines and drainage, and mentioned painting only near the end. He designed flying machines, an armoured vehicle and canal systems, though few were built.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Leonardo's interests", "items": ["Painting", "Anatomy", "Engineering", "Nature and water"]}
### The notebooks
Around 7,000 pages of his notes survive, probably a fraction of what he wrote. He wrote in mirror script, right to left, probably because he was left-handed. The pages mix shopping lists with studies of birds' flight, water currents, plants and the human body.
• He dissected around 30 human bodies and drew the heart, muscles and a foetus in the womb with startling accuracy
• His *Vitruvian Man* (around 1490) shows the ideal proportions of the human body
• Bill Gates bought one notebook, the *Codex Leicester*, in 1994 for about $30 million
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "15–20", "l": "paintings surely his"}, {"v": "~7,000", "l": "notebook pages survive"}, {"v": "~30", "l": "bodies dissected"}]}
### Why so few paintings?
Leonardo was slow, perfectionist and easily drawn to new projects. Many commissions were left unfinished. He spent his last years in Amboise, in France, as a guest of King Francis I, and died there in 1519.
!? The *Salvator Mundi*, sold in 2017 for $450 million, the highest price ever paid for a painting, is attributed to Leonardo, but many experts doubt how much of it he painted.
> Leonardo saw no wall between art and science: to paint the world, he had to understand it.

# The Mona Lisa
The sitter is almost certainly Lisa Gherardini, wife of the Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo, which is why the painting is called *La Gioconda* in Italian and Portuguese. Leonardo began it around 1503 but never delivered it. He kept refining it for years and took it to France, where it entered the French royal collection.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.1503", "l": "Leonardo begins"}, {"y": "1797", "l": "Enters the Louvre"}, {"y": "1911", "l": "Stolen"}, {"y": "1913", "l": "Found in Florence"}]}
### Small and on wood
It is painted in oil on a poplar panel only about 77 × 53 cm, smaller than many visitors expect. It sits behind protective glass, which was added after attacks in the 1950s.
### The theft that made a legend
On 21 August 1911, Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian handyman who had worked at the Louvre, walked out with the painting hidden under his clothes. The empty wall made front pages worldwide, and crowds came to stare at the gap. The poet Guillaume Apollinaire was arrested and Picasso questioned. Two years later Peruggia tried to sell it to a dealer in Florence and was caught.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Already admired", "Stolen in 1911", "Global headlines", "Mass-media icon"]}
### Why the smile shifts
The neuroscientist Margaret Livingstone suggested in 2000 that the smile is clearest in the blurry, low-detail information our peripheral vision picks up. Look straight at the mouth and the smile fades; look at the eyes and it seems to return.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why so famous?", "items": ["Sfumato technique", "Ambiguous smile", "1911 theft", "Endless reproductions"]}
!! The Mona Lisa was always the world's most famous painting => It was admired by experts, but became a global celebrity largely after the 1911 theft
> The Mona Lisa's fame is part genius, part history, and part a crime story.

# Sfumato
*Sfumato* comes from the Italian *fumo*, smoke. Instead of drawing outlines and filling them in, Leonardo let light fade gradually into shadow, so edges seem to melt. It's especially visible around the corners of the Mona Lisa's eyes and mouth, the areas that carry most emotional expression.
### How he did it
Leonardo used **oil paint**, which dries slowly, letting him blend for days. He built up dozens of extremely thin, translucent layers called **glazes**, each slightly darkening or warming the tone beneath.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Thin glaze, layer after layer", "Darker glaze in the shadows", "Light underpainting"]}
!? A 2010 X-ray study of Leonardo's faces at the Louvre found up to about 30 layers of glaze, together often thinner than 40 micrometres, less than half the width of a human hair.
### Why it feels alive
In real life we don't see hard lines around faces. Light curves around forms, and our eyes register soft transitions. By avoiding outlines, Leonardo made faces look more like living flesh and left expressions slightly open to interpretation.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hard outline", "items": ["clear, graphic edges", "fixed expression"]}, "r": {"t": "Sfumato", "items": ["soft, smoky edges", "shifting expression"]}}
### Aerial perspective
Leonardo also noticed that distant mountains look paler and bluer because of the air between us and them. He painted backgrounds this way, adding depth without lines. You can see it in the hazy landscape behind the Mona Lisa.
### The four modes
Renaissance art writers later described four ways to model light: sfumato (Leonardo), chiaroscuro (strong contrast), cangiante (changing colour instead of darkening, as Michelangelo did) and unione (soft but more colourful, like Raphael).
> Sfumato replaces lines with light, so forms seem to breathe.

# The Last Supper
Leonardo painted *The Last Supper* around 1495–1498 on a wall of the dining hall (refectory) of the convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, for Duke Ludovico Sforza. It is about 4.6 m high and 8.8 m wide. The monks ate their meals looking up at Christ's last meal.
### The dramatic moment
Leonardo chose the instant after Jesus says that one of them will betray him. The apostles react in waves of shock, grouped in threes. Judas leans back into shadow, clutching a money bag, and knocks over a salt cellar. All the lines of the room converge on Christ's head, framed by the window behind.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "True fresco", "items": ["paint on wet plaster", "fast, durable", "binds with the wall"]}, "r": {"t": "Leonardo's method", "items": ["paint on dry wall", "slow, blendable", "flakes off"]}}
### A doomed experiment
True **fresco** means painting into wet lime plaster, so the pigment becomes part of the wall. But it must be done quickly, in daily sections. Leonardo wanted to work slowly and blend like oil painting, so he painted on dry plaster with an experimental tempera and oil mix. Within about 20 years, paint was flaking.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.1498", "l": "Completed"}, {"y": "1652", "l": "Door cut through it"}, {"y": "1943", "l": "Bomb hits refectory"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "21-year restoration ends"}]}
### Survival against the odds
A doorway was cut through the bottom in 1652, removing Jesus' feet. Napoleon's troops used the room as a stable. In 1943 an Allied bomb destroyed much of the refectory, but the wall, protected by sandbags, survived. A restoration from 1978 to 1999 removed centuries of overpainting.
!? To protect what remains, visitors are let in only in small groups for about 15 minutes, and tickets often sell out weeks ahead.
> Leonardo's Last Supper is a masterpiece of drama, and a warning about experimenting on walls.

# Michelangelo
Michelangelo Buonarroti was born in 1475 in Caprese, Tuscany, and lived until 1564, reaching 88, a remarkable age then. He trained briefly as a painter, but he always called himself a sculptor, sometimes signing letters "Michelangelo, sculptor". He believed the figure already existed inside the stone and his job was to free it.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1499", "l": "Pietà, aged 24"}, {"y": "1504", "l": "David completed"}, {"y": "1512", "l": "Sistine ceiling done"}, {"y": "1564", "l": "Dies, aged 88"}]}
### David
In 1501 Florence gave the 26-year-old a huge block of marble that had been partly worked and abandoned decades earlier. He finished *David* in 1504. It stands about 5.17 m tall. Unlike earlier versions showing David after killing Goliath, Michelangelo showed him before the fight, tense and alert.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "5.17 m", "l": "David's height"}, {"v": "~3 years", "l": "to carve it"}, {"v": "26", "l": "his age at the start"}]}
### Clever proportions
David's head and hands are slightly too big for his body. Michelangelo probably intended the statue to be seen from below, perhaps high on the cathedral. In the end it was placed in the main square as a symbol of the Florentine Republic standing up to powerful enemies. Since 1873 the original has been indoors at the Accademia; a copy stands outside.
### Many talents
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Michelangelo", "items": ["Sculptor: David, Pietà", "Painter: Sistine Chapel", "Architect: St Peter's dome", "Poet: about 300 poems"]}
!? The Rome *Pietà*, made when he was about 24, is the only work Michelangelo signed; legend says he carved his name across Mary's sash after hearing visitors credit it to another sculptor.
> Michelangelo saw sculpture as freeing a figure from stone, and carried that intensity into everything he made.

# The Sistine ceiling
In 1508 Pope Julius II asked Michelangelo to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in the Vatican. Michelangelo protested that he was a sculptor. He worked from 1508 to 1512, about four years, covering a vault of roughly 500 m² about 20 m above the floor.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~4 years", "l": "1508 to 1512"}, {"v": "~500 m²", "l": "of painted vault"}, {"v": "300+", "l": "figures"}]}
### Standing, not lying
Michelangelo designed his own scaffolding, a wooden platform anchored into the walls, so the chapel could stay in use below. He painted standing up, head tilted back and arm raised for hours. He wrote a comic poem about it, describing his beard pointing at the sky and paint dripping on his face, with a sketch of himself painting upright.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "He painted lying on his back", "r": "He stood, head bent back"}]}
### What it shows
• Nine central scenes from Genesis, from the Creation to the story of Noah
• The **Creation of Adam**, with the nearly touching fingers, is one of the most reproduced images in the world
• Prophets and sibyls sit around the edges, with nude youths (ignudi) between them
### The fresco process
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fresh plaster for one day", "Transfer cartoon outline", "Paint into wet plaster", "Pigment sets in the wall"]}
Each day's section is called a *giornata*. Experts can count them, which shows how fast he worked.
!? Cleaning in 1980–1994 removed centuries of soot and old varnish, revealing bright oranges, greens and pinks. Some critics said too much was removed; most now accept the colours are his.
> The Sistine ceiling was painted standing up, in about four years, by a man who insisted he was a sculptor.

# Raphael
Raphael (Raffaello Sanzio) was born in Urbino in 1483, the son of a court painter. He trained with Perugino, then moved to Florence, where he studied Leonardo and Michelangelo closely. In 1508, aged about 25, he was called to Rome to decorate rooms in the papal apartments, now known as the Raphael Rooms.

### The School of Athens
Painted around 1509–1511, this fresco shows ancient Greek thinkers in a grand classical hall. At the vanishing point stand Plato and Aristotle.

The gestures sum up their philosophies: Plato looks to a world of perfect ideas, Aristotle to what we can observe and measure.
### Hidden portraits
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Plato", "b": "possibly Leonardo"}, {"a": "Heraclitus", "b": "possibly Michelangelo"}, {"a": "Euclid", "b": "possibly Bramante"}, {"a": "Young man, right", "b": "Raphael himself"}]}

### A short, brilliant life
Raphael ran a workshop of dozens of assistants and was also an architect. He died in 1520 on his 37th birthday and was buried, at his request, in the Pantheon in Rome.
!? The two bored little angels at the bottom of Raphael's *Sistine Madonna* (1512) have become one of the most reproduced images in the world, on postcards, mugs and umbrellas.

# Patrons
Renaissance artists didn't usually paint what they liked and then look for buyers. Most work was **commissioned**: a patron ordered it, specified it, and paid. Patrons gained prestige, religious merit and political advantage.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Who paid for art?", "items": ["Banking families", "Popes and Church", "Guilds", "Rulers"]}
### Key patrons
• **The Medici**, Florence's banking dynasty: Cosimo and Lorenzo the Magnificent supported artists such as Botticelli and the young Michelangelo
• **Popes**: Julius II commissioned the Sistine ceiling and Raphael's rooms; Leo X was himself a Medici
• **Guilds**: Florence's trade associations each paid for a statue in the niches of the church of Orsanmichele, competing to impress
### Contracts
Contracts set the subject, size, deadline and price, and often the materials. The most expensive pigment, **ultramarine** blue, was made from lapis lazuli mined in Afghanistan and could cost more than gold, so patrons specified how much and of what quality. A 1485 contract with Domenico Ghirlandaio required ultramarine of a set price per ounce.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Patron orders work", "Contract: size, colours", "Artist and workshop paint", "Patron gains prestige"]}
### Changing status
At first artists were seen as craftsmen paid for materials and labour. By the 1500s stars like Michelangelo could argue with popes and set their own terms.
!? A modern Portuguese parallel: the oil magnate Calouste Gulbenkian left his collection and fortune to a foundation in Lisbon, which opened its museum in 1969 and still funds the arts.
> Renaissance masterpieces were paid for, specified and often shaped by patrons.

# Northern Renaissance
In Flanders (today Belgium), the Netherlands and Germany, a parallel Renaissance developed in the 1400s and 1500s. Where Italians focused on classical ideals, anatomy and perspective, Northern artists were obsessed with detail and the textures of the real world: fur, metal, glass, skin.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Italy", "items": ["ideal bodies", "perspective, harmony"]}, "r": {"t": "The North", "items": ["minute detail", "texture, reflections"]}}
### Jan van Eyck and oil paint
Jan van Eyck (about 1390–1441) worked in Bruges. He didn't invent oil paint, but he perfected it, using thin, glowing layers that let light pass through and bounce back. His *Ghent Altarpiece* (completed 1432, with his brother Hubert) and *Arnolfini Portrait* (1434), with its tiny convex mirror reflecting the whole room, still amaze viewers.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Van Eyck", "b": "oil glazes, detail"}, {"a": "Bosch", "b": "strange, moral fantasies"}, {"a": "Dürer", "b": "prints and self-portraits"}, {"a": "Bruegel", "b": "peasant life, landscapes"}]}
### Albrecht Dürer
Dürer (1471–1528), from Nuremberg, travelled to Italy twice and brought its ideas north. He made his fortune from **prints**, woodcuts and engravings that could be reproduced hundreds of times and sold across Europe. His 1500 self-portrait presents the artist almost like Christ, a bold claim for an artist's status.
### Portuguese connections
Portugal traded heavily with Flanders, and many Flemish paintings came to Portuguese churches. In 1428–29 Van Eyck himself travelled to Portugal to paint Princess Isabel, daughter of King João I, for her marriage to the Duke of Burgundy.
!? Dürer's famous 1515 woodcut of a rhinoceros was based on a sketch of an animal sent from India to King Manuel I in Lisbon. Dürer never saw it, which is why it looks like it wears armour.
> The Northern Renaissance showed that perfect detail could be as powerful as perfect proportion.

# Rivalries
Around 1503–1504, the government of Florence commissioned two battle scenes for the great council hall of the Palazzo Vecchio. Leonardo, then in his fifties, was to paint the *Battle of Anghiari*. Michelangelo, nearly 30 and fresh from *David*, got the *Battle of Cascina* on another wall of the same hall. Florence effectively staged a contest between its two greatest artists.

### Why neither finished
• **Leonardo** tried an experimental technique, and the paint reportedly ran and failed to dry properly. He abandoned the work and left for Milan.
• **Michelangelo** finished only the full-size drawing (the cartoon) before Pope Julius II called him to Rome in 1505.
Both designs were copied by young artists and influenced a generation. A drawing by Rubens records the central group of Leonardo's horsemen. The original cartoons are lost.

### Personal tension
Early sources describe Michelangelo mocking Leonardo in public for never completing his giant bronze horse in Milan. Leonardo also sat on the committee deciding where to put *David*, and seems to have suggested a less prominent spot.
### Rivalry as a motor
Rivalry was part of Renaissance culture. In 1401 Ghiberti beat Brunelleschi in a competition to design bronze doors for Florence's Baptistery. Michelangelo later suspected Raphael and the architect Bramante of plotting against him in Rome.

# Carving and modelling
Almost all sculpture is made in one of two opposite ways. **Carving** is subtractive: you start with a block and take away everything that isn't the figure. **Modelling** is additive: you build the form up from soft material, adding and pushing until it's right.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Carving", "items": ["remove material", "stone, wood, ivory", "mistakes are final"]}, "r": {"t": "Modelling", "items": ["add material", "clay, wax, plaster", "easy to correct"]}}
### Carving
Carvers work from rough to fine. They block out the main masses with a point chisel and mallet, refine with toothed claw chisels and flat chisels, then smooth with rasps, files and abrasives. Michelangelo carved his *David* (1501–04), over 5 m tall, from a block of Carrara marble that another sculptor had abandoned decades earlier. He liked to say the figure was already inside the stone, waiting to be freed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Block out with point", "Shape with claw chisel", "Refine with flat chisel", "Smooth and polish"]}
Portugal has its own famous stone: the marble of Estremoz, Borba and Vila Viçosa in the Alentejo, used for centuries in churches, palaces and sculpture.
### Modelling
Clay is cheap and forgiving, so most sculptors make their first ideas in it. Fired clay becomes **terracotta**, a finished work in itself. More often, a clay or wax model is a step towards a cast in bronze, or towards a marble copy made with measuring tools that transfer points from the model to the block.
### A third way
In the 20th century, artists added **assemblage** and construction: joining found objects, sheet metal or wood. Picasso's cardboard and metal *Guitar* (1912–14) is an early example.
> Carving reveals a form hidden in a block; modelling grows a form from nothing.
!? Barcelos, in Minho, is famous for its painted clay figures; the potter Rosa Ramalho (1888–1977) turned this folk tradition into recognised art.

# Lost-wax casting
Lost-wax casting (French *cire perdue*) is more than 5,000 years old and is still used in foundries today. It lets a sculptor turn a soft model into durable metal, and it can capture fingerprints and fine textures.
### The process
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Model the figure in wax", "Coat it in a clay mould", "Heat: wax melts out", "Pour in molten bronze", "Break mould, finish metal"]}
For anything larger than a small statuette, the wax is only a thin skin, a few millimetres thick, over a heat-resistant core. Metal pins hold the core in place. Wax channels are added as pathways for the metal to flow in and for air to escape. After pouring and cooling, the mould is broken away, the channels are cut off, and the surface is chased (refined with tools) and given a **patina**, a coloured layer made with chemicals and heat.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 88/12", "l": "copper/tin in bronze"}, {"v": "≈ 1,100 °C", "l": "pouring temperature"}]}
### Why hollow?
• Less metal, so far cheaper and lighter
• Even, thin walls cool without cracking
• Large figures can be cast in sections and joined
### Famous examples
The tiny *Dancing Girl* of Mohenjo-daro in the Indus Valley dates from around 2300 BC. The Greek *Riace bronzes*, two warriors from about 450 BC, were found by a diver off southern Italy in 1972. The kingdom of Benin, in today's Nigeria, produced superb brass plaques and heads from around the 15th century.
Modern foundries often use the **indirect** method: a rubber mould of the original lets them make several wax copies, so an edition of casts can be produced while the original survives.
> Lost-wax casting swaps a fragile wax form for metal, detail for detail.
!? Most ancient Greek bronzes were later melted down for their metal, so we know many famous ones only through Roman marble copies.

# Ancient Greece
Over about 200 years, Greek sculptors transformed the human figure from a rigid pattern into something that seems to breathe. It is one of the fastest changes in the history of art.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.600 BC", "l": "Stiff kouros statues"}, {"y": "c.480 BC", "l": "Kritios Boy"}, {"y": "c.440 BC", "l": "Doryphoros"}, {"y": "c.190 BC", "l": "Victory of Samothrace"}]}
### Archaic: stiff and frontal
Early **kouroi** (young men) stand straight, fists clenched, left foot forward, much like Egyptian statues. Their faces wear the "archaic smile", a slight upturn of the lips. They were grave markers and offerings to the gods.
### Classical: contrapposto
Around 480 BC a new pose appears in a statue known as the *Kritios Boy*. The weight rests on one leg; the other relaxes. Then the whole body responds.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Weight on one leg", "Hip on that side rises", "Shoulders tilt the other way", "Body forms a gentle S"]}
The Italian word **contrapposto** means "counterpoise". Polykleitos' *Doryphoros* (Spear-bearer), around 440 BC, became the model. Polykleitos wrote a treatise, the *Canon*, on ideal proportions, now lost; Roman copies suggest the head is about one-seventh of the body's height.
### Hellenistic: drama and emotion
After Alexander the Great, sculpture became more theatrical: twisting bodies, windswept drapery, old age and suffering. The *Winged Victory of Samothrace* in the Louvre and the *Laocoön* group in the Vatican show this intensity.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Archaic", "items": ["rigid, frontal", "symmetrical"]}, "r": {"t": "Classical", "items": ["relaxed, natural", "weight shifts"]}}
> Contrapposto made marble seem to carry real weight, and so seem alive.
!? Michelangelo's *David* uses the same contrapposto pose invented in Greece some 2,000 years earlier.

# Painted statues
The gleaming white marble we associate with Greece and Rome is largely an accident of time. Ancient statues and temples were painted with strong colours: skin tones, red lips, dark-rimmed eyes, and robes covered with patterns. Some had inlaid eyes of glass or stone and bronze eyelashes.
### How we know
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["UV light shows ghost patterns", "Infrared finds Egyptian blue", "X-ray spots metal pigments", "Raking light shows outlines"]}
Pigments that have mostly vanished still leave traces. Under ultraviolet light, areas once painted weather differently and show faint outlines of borders and designs. **Egyptian blue**, an ancient synthetic pigment, glows under infrared imaging even when invisible to the eye. Chemical analysis identifies minerals such as cinnabar (red) and malachite (green).
### Why the white myth?
• Paint wore off over 2,000 years of weather and burial
• Renaissance collectors and restorers sometimes cleaned off what remained
• The German scholar Winckelmann (1764) praised white marble as ideal beauty
• Neoclassical sculptors like Canova then made white marble the standard
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "What we imagine", "items": ["pure white", "calm and cool"]}, "r": {"t": "What was made", "items": ["bright colours", "patterned robes"]}}
Since 2003 the travelling exhibition *Gods in Color*, led by archaeologists Vinzenz Brinkmann and Ulrike Koch-Brinkmann, has shown painted reconstructions. Scholars still debate how bright and how subtle the originals were, since only traces survive.
### Colour never left
In Portugal and Spain, Baroque wooden saints were richly painted and gilded. In the **estofado** technique, gold leaf was covered with paint and then scratched through to imitate rich brocade.
> Ancient sculpture was colourful; our image of white antiquity is partly a modern invention.
!! Classical statues were meant to be pure white => Most were painted; white marble became an ideal only much later

# Bernini
Gian Lorenzo Bernini (1598–1680) dominated art in Baroque Rome as sculptor, architect and stage designer. Popes competed for his time. His aim was to make stone seem to move, feel and even breathe.
### The *Rape of Proserpina*
He carved it in 1621–22, aged about 23, for Cardinal Scipione Borghese; it is still in the Galleria Borghese in Rome. It shows the myth of Pluto, god of the underworld, seizing Proserpina. ("Rape" here comes from the Latin *rapere*, to seize or abduct.) Her hand pushes his face away; tears seem to run down her cheek; his fingers sink into her thigh as if into soft flesh.
### How he did it
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Drills", "b": "deep curls of hair"}, {"a": "Rasps and files", "b": "skin's soft texture"}, {"a": "Polishing", "b": "glowing flesh"}, {"a": "Rough finish", "b": "contrast for cloth, fur"}]}
Bernini used different surface finishes side by side, so skin, hair, stone and fur catch the light in different ways. He also designed works to be seen as you move around them, revealing new moments of the story from each angle.
### Movement and drama
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Michelangelo's David", "items": ["calm, before action", "still pose"]}, "r": {"t": "Bernini's David", "items": ["mid-throw", "twisting body"]}}
His *Apollo and Daphne* (1622–25) catches the instant a nymph turns into a laurel tree, with leaves sprouting from her fingers. *The Ecstasy of Saint Teresa* (1647–52) combines sculpture, architecture and hidden light like a theatre set.
### Shaping Rome

# Rodin
Auguste Rodin (1840–1917) is often called the father of modern sculpture. He was rejected three times by the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and worked for years as a craftsman before fame arrived in his late thirties.
### The *Gates of Hell*
In 1880 the French state commissioned bronze doors for a planned Museum of Decorative Arts in Paris. Rodin chose Dante's *Inferno* as the theme. The museum was never built, but he worked on the doors until his death, filling them with about 180 writhing figures. The bronze version was cast only after he died.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1880", "l": "Gates commissioned"}, {"y": "c.1904", "l": "Thinker enlarged"}, {"y": "1906", "l": "Placed at Panthéon"}, {"y": "1917", "l": "Rodin dies"}]}
### From poet to thinker
The seated figure above the doors was first called *The Poet*: Dante contemplating his poem and the damned below. Shown on its own and enlarged to about 1.8 m, it became *The Thinker*, a universal image of the mind at work. His whole body is tense, even the toes gripping the rock.
Other figures also left the door and became famous on their own, including *The Kiss*, which shows Paolo and Francesca, lovers from Dante's poem.
### What made him modern
• He left fingermarks and rough surfaces, showing how the work was made
• He showed fragments, like a torso or a hand, as finished works
• He sought inner emotion rather than smooth ideal beauty

French law now limits a sculpture's edition to 12 casts counted as originals; there are over 20 large casts of *The Thinker* worldwide, many made posthumously.
> *The Thinker* was born as Dante gazing into hell and became a symbol of thought itself.

# Modern sculpture
In the 20th century, sculptors stopped trying to copy the body. They asked what sculpture itself could be: a pure shape, an empty space, a moving object, or an everyday thing in a new context.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Brâncuși", "b": "pure, polished essences"}, {"a": "Giacometti", "b": "thin, isolated figures"}, {"a": "Henry Moore", "b": "holes and reclining forms"}, {"a": "Calder", "b": "mobiles that move"}]}
### Simplification
Constantin Brâncuși (1876–1957), a Romanian in Paris, reduced a bird to a single gleaming curve in *Bird in Space* (from 1923). He wanted to capture the essence of flight, not feathers.
### Space and voids
Henry Moore (1898–1986) and Barbara Hepworth pierced their forms with holes, so empty space became part of the sculpture. Moore's reclining figures echo hills and caves. Alberto Giacometti (1901–66) made thin, eroded figures after the Second World War that many see as images of loneliness.
### New materials and ideas
• Welded iron and steel: Julio González, David Smith
• Movement: Alexander Calder's mobiles from the 1930s
• Readymades: Marcel Duchamp presented ordinary objects as art
• Plastic, glass, light and sound later joined the list
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1917", "l": "Duchamp's Fountain"}, {"y": "1923", "l": "Bird in Space begins"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "Calder's first mobiles"}, {"y": "1960", "l": "Walking Man I"}]}
### What changed
Sculpture moved off the pedestal. It could sit on the floor, hang from the ceiling or fill a room, and the viewer's movement around it became part of the experience.
> Modern sculptors made empty space, motion and pure form as important as the solid figure.

Public sculpture is art you don't choose to see: it sits in squares, parks and roundabouts. It tells a community's story, marks events and creates landmarks, and it can spark fierce arguments about whose story is told.

# Land art
In the late 1960s, some American and British artists left the gallery altogether. They wanted art that couldn't be bought, hung on a wall or moved, made from and in the landscape itself. This became **land art** or earthworks.
### Spiral Jetty
In April 1970, Robert Smithson used bulldozers and trucks to push basalt rock and earth into the Great Salt Lake at Rozel Point, Utah. The result is a coil running anticlockwise into reddish water, about 460 m long and 4.5 m wide. The pink tint comes from salt-loving microbes.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1970", "l": "Jetty is built"}, {"y": "1972", "l": "Lake rises, covers it"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "Reappears in drought"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "Often on dry land"}]}
For almost 30 years it lay under water. When lake levels fell, it reappeared crusted in white salt crystals. Smithson welcomed this kind of change; he was fascinated by **entropy**, the slow drift of everything towards disorder.
### Other approaches
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Richard Long", "b": "a line made by walking"}, {"a": "Walter De Maria", "b": "400 steel poles, desert"}, {"a": "Christo & Jeanne-Claude", "b": "wrapped buildings"}, {"a": "Andy Goldsworthy", "b": "leaves, ice, stone"}]}
Some works are huge and permanent; others last only hours, recorded in photographs. Christo and Jeanne-Claude wrapped the Reichstag in Berlin in 1995, and their wrapped Arc de Triomphe was realised in 2021 after Christo's death.

# The camera obscura
"Camera obscura" is Latin for "dark room". Make a room completely dark, open a small hole in one wall, and an upside-down, full-colour image of the outside world appears on the opposite wall.
### Why the image flips
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Light goes in straight lines", "Rays cross at the hole", "Top of scene hits bottom", "Image is upside down"]}
Light from the top of a tree travels down through the hole and lands low on the far wall; light from the bottom lands high. A smaller hole gives a sharper but dimmer image. From the 16th century, putting a **lens** in the hole made the image both bright and sharp; mirrors could turn it the right way up.
### A long history
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.400 BC", "l": "Mozi in China"}, {"y": "c.1020", "l": "Ibn al-Haytham"}, {"y": "1558", "l": "Della Porta"}, {"y": "1600s", "l": "Portable boxes"}]}
The Chinese thinker Mozi described the effect around the 5th–4th century BC, and Aristotle noticed crescent-shaped images of a partial eclipse under trees. The Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham studied it carefully in his *Book of Optics*. Giambattista della Porta's book of 1558 popularised it in Europe.
### A tool for artists
Portable boxes with lenses and a mirror let artists trace landscapes and buildings. The Venetian painter Canaletto is widely believed to have used one. Whether Vermeer or earlier painters did is still debated: the artist David Hockney argued they relied on optics, while many historians are unconvinced.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Camera obscura", "items": ["projects an image", "image vanishes"]}, "r": {"t": "Photograph", "items": ["projects an image", "chemistry fixes it"]}}
Your eye works the same way: the pupil is the hole, the lens focuses, and the retina receives an upside-down image that the brain turns round.
> The camera existed for centuries; photography began only when someone found a way to keep the image.
!? You can visit working camera obscuras in Portugal, including at Lisbon's Castelo de São Jorge and in a converted water tower in Tavira.

# The first photographs
For centuries people could project images but not keep them. In the 1820s the French inventor Nicéphore Niépce succeeded. His *View from the Window at Le Gras* (about 1826–27) is the oldest surviving camera photograph: blurry rooftops seen from his country house near Chalon-sur-Saône.
### How Niépce did it
He coated a pewter plate with **bitumen of Judea**, a natural asphalt that hardens in light. After an exposure of many hours, possibly days, he washed away the unhardened parts, leaving a faint image. The sun lit both sides of the courtyard during the exposure.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.1827", "l": "Niépce: Le Gras"}, {"y": "1835", "l": "Talbot's negative"}, {"y": "1839", "l": "Daguerreotype public"}, {"y": "1841", "l": "Talbot's calotype"}]}
### Daguerre and Talbot
Louis Daguerre, Niépce's partner, developed a much faster method. The **daguerreotype**, announced in 1839, used a silver-plated copper sheet made light-sensitive with iodine and developed with mercury vapour. The French state bought the rights and gave the process to the world. Each daguerreotype was a unique, extremely sharp image on a mirror-like surface.
In England, William Henry Fox Talbot invented a paper **negative** from which many positive prints could be made. His calotype of 1841 was less sharp, but the negative–positive idea shaped photography until digital cameras.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Daguerreotype", "items": ["unique image", "very sharp"]}, "r": {"t": "Calotype", "items": ["paper negative", "many prints"]}}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "exposure time, minutes (rough)", "items": [{"l": "Niépce, c.1827", "v": 480.0, "t": "8 h+", "hi": true}, {"l": "Daguerre, 1839", "v": 10.0, "t": "≈10"}, {"l": "Portrait, 1841", "v": 1.0, "t": "<1"}]}
> Photography was born when chemistry learned to freeze the image a camera obscura could only show.
!? In Brazil, the French-born Hercule Florence used the word "photographie" for his own light-sensitive experiments in the 1830s, years before it caught on in Europe.

# Exposure triangle
A photo is made by letting the right amount of light reach the sensor. Three controls set it, and each also changes the look of the picture.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Aperture", "b": "light + depth of field"}, {"a": "Shutter speed", "b": "light + motion blur"}, {"a": "ISO", "b": "sensitivity + noise"}]}
### Aperture
The aperture is the opening inside the lens. It is written as an **f-number**: the focal length divided by the opening's diameter.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "N = f ÷ D", "items": [{"s": "N", "m": "f-number"}, {"s": "f", "m": "focal length, mm"}, {"s": "D", "m": "aperture diameter, mm"}]}
A small number like f/1.8 means a wide opening: lots of light and a shallow zone of sharpness, with a blurry background. A large number like f/16 means a narrow opening: less light, and most of the scene sharp.
### Shutter speed and ISO
Shutter speed is how long the sensor is exposed: 1/1000 s freezes a footballer; 1 s turns a waterfall into silk. ISO sets how strongly the signal is amplified: higher ISO helps in the dark but adds grainy **noise**.
### Stops
Photographers measure light in **stops**. One stop doubles or halves the light. The standard f-numbers (2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16) are each one stop apart, because the area of the opening halves each time.
ex: Start: f/4, 1/250 s, ISO 200 | Change to f/5.6: one stop less light | Fix it: 1/125 s, or ISO 400
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.4, "hi": 16.0, "unit": "f-number", "items": [{"l": "Blurry bg", "v": 1.8}, {"l": "Portrait", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Landscape", "v": 11.0}]}
> Every exposure is a trade: more light from one control means choosing what to give up with the others.
!? The old "sunny 16" rule says that in bright sun, at f/16, the right shutter speed is roughly 1 divided by the ISO.

# Rule of thirds
Imagine two horizontal and two vertical lines dividing the frame into nine equal parts. The rule of thirds suggests placing important elements along those lines, or where they cross, rather than in the exact centre.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Horizon on a third line", "Eyes on the top third", "Subject on an intersection", "Space ahead of the gaze"]}
### Why it often works
A centred subject can feel static, like a target. Off-centre placement creates tension and leads the eye across the frame. It also leaves room for context: a landscape, a road, or empty space that gives a subject somewhere to look or move into, called **lead room**.
### Where it comes from
The name was coined by the English painter and engraver John Thomas Smith in 1797, describing balance in landscape painting. Painters had used similar ideas long before. Most phones and cameras can show a thirds grid on screen.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rule of thirds", "items": ["1/3 and 2/3", "simple grid"]}, "r": {"t": "Golden ratio", "items": ["≈ 0.38 and 0.62", "close, not equal"]}}
The rule is often confused with the **golden ratio**. The two give similar positions, but they are not the same, and claims that famous artworks follow the golden ratio are often exaggerated.
### When to break it
• **Symmetry**: reflections, doorways and buildings often look best centred
• **Direct portraits**: a centred face can feel confrontational and strong
• **Patterns**: repeating shapes can fill the frame evenly
ex: Seaside photo | Horizon in the middle: sea and sky compete | Horizon low: dramatic sky; high: rocks and waves
> The rule of thirds is a starting point for balance, not a law; break it when you have a reason.
!! The rule of thirds comes from the golden ratio => They're different: thirds divide at 33% and 67%, the golden ratio at about 38% and 62%

# Golden hour
The golden hour is the period shortly after sunrise and before sunset when the Sun is low in the sky. Photographers love it because it flatters almost everything: faces, buildings and landscapes.
### Why the light changes
When the Sun is low, its light passes through far more air than at midday. Air molecules scatter blue light more than red (**Rayleigh scattering**), so much of the blue is scattered out of the direct beam. What reaches you is warmer: gold, orange and pink.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sun low in the sky", "Light crosses more air", "Blue scatters away", "Warm light remains"]}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "colour temperature, K (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Candle", "v": 1900.0}, {"l": "Golden hour", "v": 3500.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Midday sun", "v": 5500.0}, {"l": "Shade, blue sky", "v": 7500.0}]}
### Why it flatters
• Soft: the low sun is less harsh, with gentler contrast
• Directional: side light reveals texture and shape
• Long shadows: they add depth and drama
• Warm: skin tones look healthy
### Blue hour
Just after sunset (and before sunrise), when the Sun is a few degrees below the horizon, the sky turns deep blue. City lights and the sky balance beautifully, ideal for photographing a lit-up Ponte 25 de Abril or Porto's riverside.
### How long does it last?
It depends on latitude and season. Near the equator the Sun drops steeply, so golden light is brief. In Portugal it lasts roughly an hour; further north in summer, the Sun slides along the horizon and the golden light can last for hours.
> Golden hour light is warm, soft and low, because the atmosphere filters the Sun when it's near the horizon.
!? At the horizon, sunlight crosses about 38 times as much air as when the Sun is directly overhead.

# Photojournalism
Photojournalism tells news stories through pictures. Since cameras became portable and fast, single images have sometimes changed how millions of people saw a war, a disaster or the planet itself.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1936", "l": "Capa: Falling Soldier"}, {"y": "1944", "l": "Capa on D-Day"}, {"y": "1968", "l": "Earthrise"}, {"y": "1972", "l": "'Napalm Girl'"}]}
### Robert Capa
Born Endre Friedmann in Budapest in 1913, Capa became famous for *The Falling Soldier* (1936), from the Spanish Civil War, though whether it was staged is still debated. On 8 June 1972, after a napalm attack in South Vietnam, a nine-year-old girl, Phan Thị Kim Phúc, ran down a road with other children. On 24 December 1968, astronaut Bill Anders on Apollo 8 photographed Earth rising over the Moon's horizon. The small, fragile blue planet became an emblem of the early environmental movement.

# Portrait tips
A good portrait shows a person, not just a face. Technique helps, but what matters most is the connection between photographer and sitter.
### Eyes first
Viewers look at the eyes first, so they must be sharp. With a shallow depth of field, focus on the nearer eye. Look for **catchlights**, small reflections of the light source, which make eyes look alive.
### Light from the side
Soft side light, such as a window on a cloudy day, shapes the face gently. A classic setup is **Rembrandt lighting**: the light at about 45° leaves a small triangle of light on the shadowed cheek. Harsh overhead noon sun creates dark eye sockets; move into open shade instead.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Window light", "b": "soft, flattering"}, {"a": "Noon sun", "b": "harsh, dark eye sockets"}, {"a": "Open shade", "b": "even, gentle"}, {"a": "Golden hour", "b": "warm glow"}]}
### Distance and lens
Shooting very close makes the nose look bigger, because it is much nearer to the camera than the ears. Stepping back and zooming in flattens the face more flatteringly; many portrait photographers like 85 mm lenses (full-frame) for this reason.
ex: Nose 50 cm away, ears about 60 cm | Nose appears 60 ÷ 50 = 1.2× larger | At 2 m: 2.1 ÷ 2.0 = only 1.05×
### Angle and activity
Shoot at eye level for a natural, equal feeling; from below, people look powerful; from above, smaller. Give people something to do, like cooking, talking or working, and they forget the camera.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Say 'cheese' and freeze", "r": "Chat, then catch the laugh"}]}
> In portraits, sharp eyes, soft light and a relaxed person matter more than expensive gear.
!? For his famous 1941 portrait of Winston Churchill, Yousuf Karsh plucked the cigar from Churchill's mouth and caught his scowl.

# Smartphone revolution
A phone's camera sensor is tiny, often a few millimetres across, compared with about 36 × 24 mm in a full-frame camera. Small sensors catch less light, so they struggle with noise and contrast. Phones overcome this with software: **computational photography**.
### What happens when you tap
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Camera already buffering", "Captures a burst", "Aligns the frames", "Merges and tone-maps", "Saves one photo"]}
Your phone is recording frames before you press the button. It picks the sharpest ones, aligns them to correct for hand shake, and merges them. Random noise averages out, while real detail adds up.
ex: Merge 9 frames | Noise falls by √9 = 3× | Like a sensor with much more light
### Main tricks
• **HDR**: blend dark and bright exposures so both sky and shadows show detail
• **Night mode**: stack many frames over a second or more
• **Portrait mode**: estimate depth, then blur the background artificially
• **AI processing**: recognise faces, skies or food and adjust each
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1975", "l": "First digital camera"}, {"y": "2000", "l": "Early camera phone"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "iPhone launches"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "Night modes arrive"}]}
### Consequences
Industry estimates suggest well over a trillion photos are now taken every year, most on phones. Compact cameras have largely disappeared, and Kodak, whose engineer Steven Sasson built the first digital camera in 1975, filed for bankruptcy in 2012. Critics note that a photo is now partly the phone's interpretation of the scene, not just a record of light.
> Modern phones make up for tiny lenses with clever maths, merging many frames into one.
!? Sasson's 1975 prototype took about 23 seconds to record a 0.01-megapixel black-and-white image onto cassette tape.

# Photography as art
When photography appeared in 1839, many critics saw it as a mechanical copying process, not art. A machine did the work, they said, so where was the artist? In 1859 the poet Charles Baudelaire argued photography should be a humble servant of the arts and sciences, not an art itself.
### How photographers answered
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1890s", "l": "Pictorialism"}, {"y": "1902", "l": "Photo-Secession, NY"}, {"y": "1940", "l": "MoMA photo dept"}, {"y": "1952", "l": "Decisive Moment"}]}
At first they imitated painting. **Pictorialists** used soft focus, textured paper and hand-worked prints. Then Alfred Stieglitz in New York championed "straight" photography, sharp and unmanipulated, valued for what only a camera can do. In 1940 New York's Museum of Modern Art opened a department of photography, a major sign of acceptance.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pictorialism", "items": ["soft, painterly", "imitates painting"]}, "r": {"t": "Straight photo", "items": ["sharp, direct", "camera's own art"]}}
### Henri Cartier-Bresson
The Frenchman (1908–2004) co-founded Magnum in 1947. His 1952 book, known in English as *The Decisive Moment*, described capturing the instant when form and meaning come together in a fleeting scene, usually with a small Leica and no flash.
### Annie Leibovitz
Born in 1949, she became famous for portraits for *Rolling Stone* and *Vanity Fair*, combining celebrity, staging and intimacy. Her portrait of John Lennon and Yoko Ono was taken hours before Lennon was killed in December 1980.
### Today
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "$12.4m", "l": "Man Ray photo, 2022"}, {"v": "1940", "l": "MoMA photo department"}]}
Photographs now hang in major museums and sell for millions. In Portugal, the Centro Português de Fotografia occupies Porto's former Relação prison.
> Photography became art when photographers stopped imitating painting and used what only cameras can do.
!? The record price for a photograph, $12.4 million in 2022, went to Man Ray's 1924 image of a woman's back painted with violin sound-holes.

# Truth and manipulation
People tend to trust photographs as proof: "the camera never lies". But photos have been staged, retouched and combined almost since the beginning, and digital tools and AI have made convincing fakes easy.
### Before computers
• A famous portrait of Abraham Lincoln is his head placed on another politician's body
• The *Cottingley Fairies* (1917), paper cut-outs photographed by two girls, fooled many, including Arthur Conan Doyle
• Under Stalin, officials who fell from favour were scratched, airbrushed or painted out of photographs
The secret-police chief Nikolai Yezhov, for example, was removed from a photo of him walking beside Stalin after he was arrested and executed in 1940.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1917", "l": "Cottingley Fairies"}, {"y": "1930s", "l": "Stalin's edits"}, {"y": "1990", "l": "Photoshop launched"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "AI images go public"}]}
### The AI era
Image generators can now create realistic photos of events that never happened. Deepfake video can put words in anyone's mouth. Even real photos can be accused of being fake, which some call the **liar's dividend**.
### Checking provenance
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Who took it, and when?", "Reverse image search", "Look for Content Credentials", "Check trusted news sources"]}

# Portuguese guitar
The Portuguese guitar descends from the European cittern, a pear-shaped instrument with metal strings. In the 18th century the so-called "English guitar", popular with Porto's British merchant community, became fashionable, and Portuguese makers adapted it. By the late 19th century it had become the voice of fado.
### Why it shimmers
• **12 steel strings in 6 pairs** (courses): each pair is plucked together, so notes ring with a slight chorus
• The lower pairs are tuned an octave apart, adding a bright overtone
• It's played with the thumb and index finger, often with picks called **unhas** ("nails"), in a fast technique called dedilho
• A fan of screw tuners on the head replaces the usual wooden pegs

### The fado line-up
In a fado house you'll usually see three musicians: the singer, the Portuguese guitar weaving a counter-melody, and the **viola** (a classical guitar) keeping the chords. A bass guitar is often added.

# Coimbra fado
Coimbra has one of Europe's oldest universities: founded in 1290 and settled in Coimbra for good in 1537. Its students developed their own song tradition in the 19th century, and Coimbra fado is still tied to student life, sung by men in the academic dress of black cape and suit (capa e batina).

### The serenade
The classic setting is a night serenade: a singer and guitarists under a window, or on the steps of the Old Cathedral (Sé Velha). The songs are often addressed to a sweetheart, to the city, or to the student years that will soon end.

# Calçada portuguesa
Portuguese pavement is made of small, roughly cubic stones, about 4–6 cm across, set by hand into sand and tapped down with a hammer. White limestone and black basalt are arranged into patterns, from simple waves to ships, stars and coats of arms.

Portuguese workers took the craft to Brazil. Copacabana's beach promenade in Rio copied Rossio's waves, but turned them to run parallel to the sea. # Filigree
Filigree comes from the Latin *filum* (thread) and *granum* (grain). It's an ancient technique, found in jewellery from Mesopotamia, Etruria and Iron Age Iberia. In Portugal it became a living folk art, concentrated in the north.

June is Portugal's month of saints. The festivals honour **Santo António** (13 June), **São João** (24 June) and **São Pedro** (29 June). They fall around the summer solstice, and many customs, like bonfires and fresh herbs, probably go back to older midsummer celebrations.

# Barcelos rooster
The story is set in medieval times. A pilgrim from Galicia, on his way to Santiago de Compostela, stopped in Barcelos just as a theft had shocked the town. As a stranger, he was arrested and sentenced to hang.

# Literature beyond Camões
Camões gave Portugal its epic in 1572, but the country's literature kept renewing itself. Four writers show its range: a realist novelist, a modernist poet of many selves, a poet of the sea and light, and a Nobel novelist.

# Cante alentejano
Cante is the traditional group singing of the Alentejo, the vast plain of southern Portugal. It's sung without instruments, slowly, by groups of about ten to thirty singers standing in rows. UNESCO added it to its list of intangible heritage in November 2014.
### How a song unfolds
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ponto: solo opening", "Alto: high voice joins", "Choir answers", "Alto soars above"]}
The **ponto** starts the song alone. The **alto** then takes over the line, singing higher, and the whole choir enters in harmony. The alto's voice floats above the group, decorating the melody.
### Songs of work and land
Cante grew among farm workers, miners and villages. Songs talk about the land, the wheat fields, the heat, love, longing and hard labour. Singers often link arms and sway with a slow step as they sing, as if walking together.
• Men's groups dominated for most of the 20th century; women's and mixed groups have grown since
• Its origins are debated: church chant, Arab music and older folk singing have all been suggested
• Towns like Serpa, Beja and Cuba hold regular festivals

== ar-poetry

# What makes poetry
Poetry is older than writing. Homer's epics were recited from memory long before they were written down, around the 8th century BCE. Rhythm, rhyme and repetition helped singers remember thousands of lines, and they still make poems stick in our heads.
### The tools of compression
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "A poet's toolkit", "items": ["Rhythm and metre", "Sound and rhyme", "Images", "Line breaks"]}
• **Sound**: rhyme, alliteration (repeated first consonants) and assonance (repeated vowels)
• **Imagery**: pictures for the senses, rather than abstract statements
• **Line breaks**: the poet, not the page width, decides where a line ends
### The line break
In prose, the line ends when the paper runs out. In poetry, every break is a choice. A pause at the end of a line can create suspense, a double meaning or a breath. When a sentence runs over into the next line, it's called **enjambment**, and it pulls the reader forward.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Prose", "items": ["lines end at margin", "explains", "read with the eye"]}, "r": {"t": "Poetry", "items": ["lines end by choice", "suggests", "heard in the ear"]}}
### Why read it aloud?
A poem's rhythm only fully exists when spoken. Many poems that look difficult on the page become clear once you hear where the stresses fall and where the voice pauses.
!? Samuel Taylor Coleridge defined poetry as "the best words in the best order", compared with prose as "words in their best order".
> Poetry packs the most meaning and feeling into the fewest words, using sound as well as sense.

# Metre
English verse counts **stresses**. A **foot** is a small unit of stressed (DUM) and unstressed (da) syllables, and a line is measured in feet.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Iamb", "b": "da-DUM: a-BOVE"}, {"a": "Trochee", "b": "DUM-da: GAR-den"}, {"a": "Anapaest", "b": "da-da-DUM: un-der-STAND"}, {"a": "Dactyl", "b": "DUM-da-da: BEAU-ti-ful"}]}
### Iambic pentameter
"Pentameter" means five feet, so an iambic pentameter line has ten syllables with five stresses. It's close to the natural rhythm of English speech, which is why Shakespeare used it for most of his plays and sonnets.
ex: Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? | shall-I / com-PARE / thee-TO / a-SUM / mer's-DAY | 10 syllables, 5 stresses: iambic pentameter
### Portuguese counts differently
Portuguese verse counts syllables up to the **last stressed syllable** of the line. The two great metres are:
• **Redondilha maior**: 7 syllables, the metre of folk verses, like the little quatrains on Santo António basil pots
• **Decassílabo**: 10 syllables, usually stressed on the 6th and 10th, the metre of Camões's sonnets and *Os Lusíadas*
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "English verse", "items": ["counts stresses", "feet: iambs etc."]}, "r": {"t": "Portuguese verse", "items": ["counts syllables", "to the last stress"]}}
### Breaking the pattern
Good poets don't follow metre like a metronome. A sudden change, such as an extra stress or a missing syllable, draws attention to a word, just as a drummer's off-beat does.
> Metre is the heartbeat of a poem; the interesting moments are when it skips.

# The sonnet
"Sonnet" comes from the Italian *sonetto*, "little sound" or "little song". It was invented in the 1230s at the Sicilian court of Emperor Frederick II, probably by the poet-notary Giacomo da Lentini.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1230s", "l": "Invented in Sicily"}, {"y": "1300s", "l": "Petrarch's Laura"}, {"y": "1595", "l": "Camões's Rimas"}, {"y": "1609", "l": "Shakespeare's Sonnets"}]}
### Two main shapes
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Petrarchan", "items": ["8 + 6 lines", "turn after line 8"]}, "r": {"t": "Shakespearean", "items": ["4 + 4 + 4 + 2", "turn at the couplet"]}}
The key moment is the **volta**, the turn, where the poem shifts direction: a question gets an answer, a problem a twist. In Petrarch's form it comes after line 8; in Shakespeare's, the final rhyming couplet often delivers the punchline.
### Petrarch, Shakespeare, Camões
• **Petrarch** (1304–1374) wrote more than 300 sonnets to Laura, setting the model of longing, unreachable love
• **Shakespeare** published 154 sonnets in 1609, many addressed to a young man and a "dark lady"
• **Sá de Miranda** brought the sonnet to Portugal in the 1520s, after a trip to Italy
• **Camões** perfected it in Portuguese; his *Rimas* appeared in 1595, after his death, and the exact number of his sonnets is still debated
!? Camões's famous sonnet opening "Amor é fogo que arde sem se ver" ("love is a fire that burns unseen") defines love entirely through contradictions, a classic Petrarchan trick.
> Fourteen lines are just enough to set up a problem and turn it on its head.

# Haiku
Haiku began as the **hokku**, the opening verse of *renga*, long chains of linked verse written by groups of poets. The opening had to set the scene and the season. In the 17th century Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) made the opening verse an art on its own. The word "haiku" was only coined by Masaoka Shiki in the 1890s.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Line 1: 5 sound units", "Line 2: 7 sound units", "Line 3: 5 sound units"]}
### Not quite syllables
Japanese counts **on**, short sound units, which are not the same as English syllables. An English syllable usually holds more sound, so many English-language haiku poets write fewer than 17 syllables to keep the lightness.
### Two hidden rules
• **Kigo**: a season word, such as frog (spring), cicada (summer) or snow (winter)
• **Kireji**: a "cutting word" that splits the poem into two parts that spark off each other
### The old pond
Bashō's poem of 1686, *furuike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto*, sets an old, still pond against the sudden sound of a frog jumping in. The *ya* at the end of the first line is the cut. The poem doesn't explain anything; it lets silence and sound meet.

> A haiku is a snapshot: two images placed side by side, and a spark between them.

# Metaphor and simile
Both compare two things, but they work differently. A **simile** keeps the two apart with "like" or "as": Robert Burns's "my love is like a red, red rose" (1794). A **metaphor** fuses them: Shakespeare's "All the world's a stage".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simile", "items": ["X is LIKE Y", "you see the comparison"]}, "r": {"t": "Metaphor", "items": ["X IS Y", "the comparison disappears"]}}
### Metaphors we live by
In 1980 the linguist George Lakoff and philosopher Mark Johnson argued that metaphor isn't just decoration; it structures everyday thought. We talk about arguments like wars ("attack a position", "win the debate") and time like money ("spend", "save", "waste").
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Argument is war", "b": "defend, attack, win"}, {"a": "Time is money", "b": "spend, save, waste"}, {"a": "Life is a journey", "b": "crossroads, dead end"}, {"a": "Ideas are food", "b": "half-baked, digest"}]}
### Dead metaphors
Many everyday words are metaphors so old we no longer notice them: the *leg* of a table, the *foot* of a mountain, a *bottleneck*. In Portuguese, the *braço* (arm) of a river works the same way. Poets try to make fresh ones that make us see again.
### Other figures
• **Personification**: giving human traits to things ("the wind howled")
• **Metonymy**: naming something by what's linked to it ("the Crown" for the monarchy)

> A good metaphor doesn't decorate an idea; it changes the way you think about it.

# Free verse
Free verse (from the French *vers libre*) drops regular metre and fixed rhyme. Instead, it builds rhythm from repetition, sentence shape and line length. It took off in France in the 1880s, with poets such as Rimbaud and Jules Laforgue.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1855", "l": "Whitman's Leaves"}, {"y": "1886", "l": "Vers libre in France"}, {"y": "1915", "l": "Orpheu, Lisbon"}, {"y": "1917", "l": "Eliot on vers libre"}]}
### Walt Whitman
In 1855 Whitman self-published *Leaves of Grass*. His long, rolling lines borrowed from the Bible's psalms and from speeches, listing people, places and jobs across America. He kept revising the book for the rest of his life.
### Álvaro de Campos
Campos was one of Pessoa's heteronyms: an imaginary naval engineer educated in Glasgow. His "Triumphal Ode" and "Maritime Ode" appeared in the magazine *Orpheu* in 1915, the start of Portuguese modernism. They're enormous, noisy poems that celebrate machines, ports and speed, with lines that shout, repeat and break apart.
### Free, but not formless
• **Anaphora**: repeating the same opening words line after line, as Whitman does
• **Cadence**: rhythm that follows breath and speech instead of a fixed count
• **Line breaks**: still carefully chosen to create pauses and emphasis
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Free verse has no rules", "r": "It swaps metre for breath, repetition"}]}
!? T. S. Eliot wrote in 1917 that "no vers is libre for the man who wants to do a good job": even free verse demands control.
> Free verse trades the fixed beat for the rhythm of breath and speech.

# Pessoa's Mensagem
*Mensagem* ("Message") came out in December 1934, a year before Pessoa's death. It's a short book of 44 poems. Pessoa had published poems in English, but this was his only book in Portuguese during his lifetime. It won a prize from the regime's propaganda office, though only in a second category because it was considered too short.
### Three parts
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Brasão: founders, kings", "Mar Português: the voyages", "O Encoberto: the hidden king"]}
• **Brasão** (Coat of Arms): figures from Ulysses, the mythical founder of Lisbon, to the early kings
• **Mar Português** (Portuguese Sea): Henry the Navigator, Bartolomeu Dias, the sailors and the monster at the Cape
• **O Encoberto** (The Hidden One): a waiting for national rebirth
### The myth of Sebastian
King Sebastião vanished at the battle of Alcácer Quibir in Morocco in 1578. His body was never clearly identified, and the legend grew that he would return on a foggy morning to save Portugal. This **Sebastianism**, together with Padre António Vieira's idea of a **Fifth Empire**, runs through the final part of *Mensagem*.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Founders and kings", "Sea voyages", "Loss at Alcácer Quibir", "Dream of a return"]}
### History as myth
Pessoa isn't writing history. He turns kings and navigators into symbols, and the sea into a test of the soul. The famous poem about the salt sea imagines the ocean's saltiness as made of the tears of Portuguese mothers, wives and children, and asks whether the price was worth it, answering that everything is worth it if the soul is not small.
!? The Fifth Empire Pessoa dreamed of was not a territory but a cultural and spiritual empire, built with language and ideas.
> *Mensagem* turns Portugal's history of voyages and losses into a myth of a nation still waiting to fulfil itself.

# Florbela Espanca
Florbela Espanca was born on 8 December 1894 in Vila Viçosa, in the Alentejo. She was one of the first women to enrol in law at the University of Lisbon, in 1917. She died in Matosinhos on her 36th birthday, in 1930.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1894", "l": "Born, Vila Viçosa"}, {"y": "1919", "l": "Livro de Mágoas"}, {"y": "1923", "l": "Soror Saudade"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "Charneca em Flor"}]}
### Her books
• *Livro de Mágoas* ("Book of Sorrows", 1919)
• *Livro de Soror Saudade* ("Book of Sister Longing", 1923)
• *Charneca em Flor* ("Heath in Bloom"), published in 1931, a few weeks after her death
### What makes her different
She mostly wrote **sonnets**, the form of Camões and Antero de Quental, but filled them with a very personal, often female voice: desire, loneliness, pride and longing to be more than the world allowed. Her poems swing between exaltation and despair, and she often speaks about being a poet herself as a kind of fate.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Florbela's themes", "items": ["Passionate love", "Longing (saudade)", "Being a poet", "Freedom as a woman"]}
### Scandal, then love
She divorced twice and married three times, which shocked the Portugal of the 1920s, and critics largely ignored her. After her death, readers made her one of the country's most loved poets. Her sonnets have been set to music by many artists, and a feature film about her life, *Florbela*, came out in 2012.
!? Her birth name was Flor Bela de Alma da Conceição; "Espanca" was her father's surname.
> Florbela took the old, disciplined sonnet and made it burn with an intensely personal voice.

# Spoken word
For most of history, poetry was performed, not read silently. Homer's rhapsodes recited epics, West African **griots** still keep family histories in song, and medieval troubadours sang their verses.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~800 BCE", "l": "Homeric bards"}, {"y": "1200s", "l": "Galician cantigas"}, {"y": "1970s", "l": "Hip hop in the Bronx"}, {"y": "1984", "l": "Slam in Chicago"}]}
### Portugal's singing poets
Between about 1200 and 1350, Galician-Portuguese poets wrote **cantigas** to be sung: love songs, songs in a girl's voice longing for her friend, and mocking satires. King Dinis was one of the best. In the Minho, the tradition of **cantares ao desafio** survives: two singers improvise rhyming verses against each other, trading jokes and insults to music.
### Rap and slam

• Rappers use internal rhymes, multisyllable rhymes and complex rhythms, much like formal poets
• **Poetry slam** began around 1984–86 in Chicago, where Marc Smith had poets compete in front of a scoring audience
• Portuguese rap took off in the 1990s, with artists like General D and later Sam the Kid and Valete

# Reading a poem
A poem is meant to be slow food. You don't need special training: a few simple habits reveal most of what's going on.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Read aloud, twice", "Note what surprises you", "Follow images and breaks", "Ask what changed"]}
### Questions to ask
• **Who is speaking, and to whom?** The "I" of a poem is not always the poet
• **What images stay with you?** Ask what feelings they carry
• **Where do the lines break?** What word sits at the end, in the spotlight?
• **Where's the turn?** Many poems change direction near the end
### A worked example
Take Camões's sonnet that begins "Amor é fogo que arde sem se ver" (published 1595).
ex: Read aloud: steady 10-syllable lines | Notice: every line defines love as a contradiction, a fire unseen, a wound that doesn't hurt | Turn: the last lines ask how something so contrary can bring people together | Result: love is shown, not explained

> Read a poem twice aloud and ask what changed by the end: that's most of the work.

# Greek theatre
Athenian theatre grew out of the **City Dionysia**, a spring festival for Dionysus, god of wine and fertility. Plays were not everyday entertainment: they were staged as competitions, paid for by wealthy citizens as a civic duty, and watched by a large share of the city's men.
Tradition credits a performer called Thespis, around 534 BCE, with stepping out of the singing chorus to speak as a character. That single move created the actor, which is why actors are still called "thespians" today.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 534 BCE", "l": "Thespis, first actor"}, {"y": "472 BCE", "l": "Aeschylus' Persians"}, {"y": "c. 429 BCE", "l": "Sophocles' Oedipus"}, {"y": "c. 340 BCE", "l": "Epidaurus theatre"}]}
### How the theatre worked
Theatres were cut into hillsides so the slope did the work of a grandstand. The Theatre of Dionysus in Athens probably held well over 10,000 people; Epidaurus seats about 13,000 and is famous for acoustics so good that a coin dropped on stage can be heard high up.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Theatron: tiered seats on hill", "Orchestra: circle for the chorus", "Skene: hut and backdrop"]}
• Two or three speaking actors played every role, switching masks
• A chorus of 12–15 sang, danced and commented on the action
• Masks made faces readable from far away and let men play women
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "13,000", "l": "seats at Epidaurus"}, {"v": "3", "l": "speaking actors, max"}]}
!? Portugal has its own ancient stage: the Roman theatre of Lisbon, built under Augustus around 2,000 years ago, was rediscovered after the 1755 earthquake and is now a museum.
> The first theatre was a religious and civic event, and almost every stage since still borrows its shape.

# Tragedy and comedy
Athenian playwrights entered competitions. A tragic poet presented three tragedies plus a rowdy "satyr play", while comic poets competed separately. Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides are the three tragedians whose work survives; Aristophanes is the great comic voice.
### What tragedy did
A tragic hero is usually high-born and admirable, yet makes a terrible error. In *Oedipus the King*, Oedipus hunts for a king's murderer and discovers it was himself. The philosopher Aristotle argued that watching such a fall stirs pity and fear, then releases them, a process he called **catharsis**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Admirable hero", "Error of judgement", "Reversal of fortune", "Recognition", "Catharsis for audience"]}
### What comedy did
Old Comedy was loud, rude and political. Aristophanes mocked the powerful general Cleon by name and in *The Clouds* (423 BCE) turned Socrates into a fraud dangling in a basket. Free speech on stage was part of democracy's self-image.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tragedy", "items": ["myths and heroes", "suffering, downfall", "pity and fear"]}, "r": {"t": "Comedy", "items": ["living politicians", "fantasy plots", "laughter, mockery"]}}
The two masks, one weeping and one laughing, are linked to the Muses Melpomene (tragedy) and Thalia (comedy).
!! A tragic hero is ruined by a "fatal flaw" in their character => Aristotle's word, hamartia, means closer to "error" or "mistake"; the downfall often comes from ignorance, not vice
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hamartia", "b": "the hero's error"}, {"a": "Peripeteia", "b": "sudden reversal of fortune"}, {"a": "Catharsis", "b": "emotional release"}]}
> Tragedy asked how good people can fall; comedy asked why the powerful deserve a kick.

# Gil Vicente
Gil Vicente wrote for the court of kings Manuel I and João III, at the height of the Portuguese Discoveries. His first piece, the *Monólogo do Vaqueiro* (1502), was performed in the queen's chamber to celebrate the birth of the future João III. Vicente himself reportedly played the cowherd.
He wrote around 40–50 plays, in Portuguese, Castilian or a mix of both, ranging from religious "autos" to earthy farces.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1502", "l": "Monólogo do Vaqueiro"}, {"y": "1517", "l": "Barca do Inferno"}, {"y": "1523", "l": "Inês Pereira farce"}, {"y": "c. 1536", "l": "Death of Vicente"}]}
### The boats of the dead
In *Auto da Barca do Inferno* (1517) souls arrive at a river bank. The Devil's boat goes to hell and the Angel's boat to paradise. Each passenger tries to talk their way on board, and their excuses reveal their sins.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Devil's boat", "items": ["nobleman, moneylender", "friar, corrupt judge", "shoemaker who cheated"]}, "r": {"t": "Angel's boat", "items": ["Joane the fool", "four crusader knights"]}}

• The moneylender wants to bring his money
• The friar arrives with his mistress
Only the simple fool and knights who died fighting for their faith are saved. Vicente's target is hypocrisy, not religion: wealth, rank and even church office buy nothing at the river.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Satire of every social class", "Allegory: Devil and Angel", "Mix of verse, humour, faith"]}
!? *Barca do Inferno* is still part of the Portuguese school curriculum, so most Portuguese adults met it as teenagers.
> Vicente put the whole of Portuguese society on a jetty and let each character condemn themselves.

# Shakespeare's Globe
The Globe was built in 1599 on London's south bank, in Southwark, by Shakespeare's company, the Lord Chamberlain's Men. They reused the oak timbers of an older playhouse, carried across the Thames after a lease dispute.
### A day at the Globe
Plays ran in the afternoon, by daylight. A flag on the roof announced a performance. The cheapest spot was the open yard, where "groundlings" stood for a penny; a seat in the covered galleries cost more, and a cushion more again.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Galleries: seats, 2–3 pence", "Stage thrust into the crowd", "Yard: groundlings, 1 penny"]}
The stage pushed out into the yard, so actors were surrounded on three sides. There was almost no scenery; words painted the scene, which is why Shakespeare's characters so often describe where they are.
Women's roles went to boys whose voices had not yet broken. Women first acted legally on the English stage in 1660.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1599", "l": "Globe built"}, {"y": "1613", "l": "Burns down"}, {"y": "1642", "l": "Theatres closed"}, {"y": "1997", "l": "Replica opens"}]}
### Fire and rebirth
In 1613 a stage cannon set the thatched roof alight during *Henry VIII*. The Globe was rebuilt, then closed when Puritans shut the theatres in 1642. The present replica, championed by American actor Sam Wanamaker, opened in 1997 close to the original site.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~3,000", "l": "audience, original Globe"}, {"v": "1 penny", "l": "to stand in the yard"}]}
!! Shakespeare's audience was refined and silent => Crowds ate, drank, heckled and answered back; the plays were popular entertainment
> The Globe was a noisy, crowded, open-air space where words had to do the work of scenery.

# Commedia dell'arte
Commedia dell'arte means "comedy of the profession": it was played by paid, travelling companies rather than amateurs. The earliest known contract for a troupe was signed in Padua in 1545, and the form thrived for about two centuries.
### No script, but a plan
Troupes worked from a **scenario**, a page outlining entrances and plot twists. Actors improvised the dialogue and slotted in rehearsed comic routines called **lazzi**, such as a servant trying to catch a fly. Each actor often played the same character for life.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Scenario pinned backstage", "Actors improvise lines", "Insert lazzi gags", "Adapt to the crowd"]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Arlecchino", "b": "hungry, nimble servant"}, {"a": "Pantalone", "b": "miserly old merchant"}, {"a": "Il Dottore", "b": "pompous know-all"}, {"a": "Innamorati", "b": "young lovers, no masks"}]}
Masks marked the type at a glance, so audiences anywhere in Europe could follow the action, even in another language. The young lovers performed unmasked, playing it straight against the chaos.
!? Unlike most theatre of the time, commedia companies included women. Isabella Andreini, a star of the late 1500s, was famous across Italy and France.
### Its long shadow
Molière shared a Paris theatre with an Italian troupe and borrowed its types: the miser, the quack doctor, the clever servant. Pulcinella became England's Punch, and Harlequin became a pantomime staple.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Commedia dell'arte", "items": ["Molière's comedies", "Punch and Judy", "Pantomime", "Sitcom stock types"]}
A modern sitcom works in a similar way: fixed characters with known quirks, dropped into new situations each week.
> Give an audience characters they recognise instantly, and you can improvise almost anything around them.

# Opera
Opera was born from an experiment. In late-1500s Florence, a group of scholars and musicians known as the **Camerata** tried to recreate how they believed Greek tragedy had been sung. The result was a new art form in which the whole drama is set to music.
Claudio Monteverdi's *L'Orfeo* (Mantua, 1607) is the earliest opera still regularly performed. In 1637 Venice opened the first public opera house, turning a court luxury into a ticketed show.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1600", "l": "Peri's Euridice"}, {"y": "1607", "l": "Monteverdi's Orfeo"}, {"y": "1637", "l": "First public house"}, {"y": "1793", "l": "São Carlos, Lisbon"}]}
### How an opera works
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Libretto", "b": "the text, 'little book'"}, {"a": "Recitative", "b": "speech-like singing, plot"}, {"a": "Aria", "b": "solo song, emotion"}, {"a": "Chorus", "b": "crowd scenes in song"}]}
Singers project over a full orchestra without microphones, using trained breath support and resonance. The great repertoire includes Mozart, Verdi, Wagner and Puccini.
### Opera in Lisbon
Lisbon's grand Ópera do Tejo opened in 1755 and was destroyed by the earthquake that November, only months later. Its successor, the **Teatro Nacional de São Carlos**, opened in 1793. Modelled on Naples' Teatro San Carlo, it was funded by Lisbon merchants and named after Princess Carlota Joaquina.

!! Opera was always an elite art => In 19th-century Italy, opera tunes were popular music, whistled in the street and played by barrel organs
> Opera began as an attempt to revive Greek drama and became the most lavish stage spectacle Europe ever built.

# Realism
For most of history, serious drama was about kings, gods and heroes, often in verse. In the late 1800s a group of playwrights asked a new question: what happens behind the doors of an ordinary middle-class home?
### Ibsen: the slammed door
Norway's Henrik Ibsen wrote *A Doll's House* (1879). Nora, a wife treated like a pretty toy, finally walks out on her husband and children. The sound of the door closing behind her caused a scandal across Europe. Some theatres demanded a happier ending.
### Chekhov: the drama underneath
Russia's Anton Chekhov wrote plays where people chat, drink tea and miss their chances. The real drama lies in what is left unsaid. *The Seagull* flopped in 1896, then triumphed at the Moscow Art Theatre in 1898.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1879", "l": "A Doll's House"}, {"y": "1898", "l": "Seagull revived"}, {"y": "1904", "l": "The Cherry Orchard"}]}
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before", "items": ["kings and gods", "verse speeches", "painted backdrops"]}, "r": {"t": "Realism", "items": ["ordinary families", "everyday speech", "real furniture"]}}
### New acting for new plays
Director Konstantin Stanislavski developed a method in which actors build a character's inner life and motives. His ideas, filtered through American "Method" teachers, shaped film acting from Marlon Brando onwards.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Box set: a room, three walls", "Everyday dialogue", "Social problems on stage", "Psychological acting"]}
!? Chekhov gave writers a famous rule: if a gun hangs on the wall in Act One, it should be fired by the end. Every detail on a realist stage should earn its place.
> Realism moved the battlefield of drama from palaces to the living room and into characters' minds.

# Breaking the fourth wall
In a typical modern stage set the audience looks into a room through an open side. The actors behave as if that side were a solid wall. The French writer Denis Diderot described the idea in 1758, and it became the rule of realist theatre.
### Brecht's alternative
German playwright Bertolt Brecht (1898–1956) thought illusion was dangerous. If spectators simply cried along with a character, they would go home unchanged. He wanted them to watch like a sports crowd: alert, judging and arguing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Illusion", "items": ["hide the machinery", "feel with the hero", "forget the theatre"]}, "r": {"t": "Brecht", "items": ["show the lights", "judge the choices", "think about society"]}}
He called his tool the **Verfremdungseffekt**, usually translated as the "estrangement" or "distancing" effect.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Signs announcing each scene", "Songs that interrupt the plot", "Actors speaking to the crowd", "Visible lights and stagehands"]}
In *Mother Courage and Her Children* (1941), a mother profits from war while it takes her children. Brecht wanted audiences to see the economics behind the tragedy, not just to weep.
### Not so new
Shakespeare's soliloquies and asides talk straight to the audience, and pantomime crowds shout "he's behind you!". Film and TV use the trick too: Ferris Bueller, *House of Cards* and *Fleabag* all wink at the camera.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 1600", "l": "Shakespeare's asides"}, {"y": "1758", "l": "Diderot's 'wall'"}, {"y": "1941", "l": "Mother Courage"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Fleabag on TV"}]}
> The fourth wall is a convention, and breaking it reminds the audience that they are watching and should think.

# Musicals
A musical tells its story through spoken scenes, songs and dance. The key idea is that a song moves the plot forward or reveals character, rather than stopping the story for a pleasant interlude.

# Theatre of the Absurd
After the Second World War, the Holocaust and the atomic bomb, many writers felt the old stories with clear meaning no longer fitted the world. The critic Martin Esslin grouped them under the label "Theatre of the Absurd" in 1961, borrowing from Albert Camus' idea that life has no built-in meaning.
### Waiting for Godot
Samuel Beckett, an Irishman living in Paris, wrote *En attendant Godot* in French; it premiered in 1953. Two tramps, Vladimir and Estragon, wait by a bare tree. They argue, eat a carrot, and meet a cruel master and his slave. A boy says Godot will come tomorrow. Act Two repeats almost the same pattern.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Wait by the tree", "Pass the time", "Boy: not today", "Decide to leave", "Do not move"]}
One critic joked it was a play in which nothing happens, twice. Yet audiences recognised their own lives: hope, routine, friendship and waiting.

### Common features
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Circular plots, no resolution", "Language that breaks down", "Dark, clown-like humour", "Bare, symbolic stages"]}
!? In 1957 *Godot* was performed at San Quentin prison in California. The inmates understood it immediately: they too knew what waiting for nothing felt like.
Beckett won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1969. He always refused to say who or what Godot was.
> Absurdist plays don't explain the meaning of life; they show people laughing and suffering while they wait for one.

# Serif and sans serif
The serif probably goes back to Roman stone carving. Letters on monuments such as Trajan's Column (113 CE) were painted with a flat brush before being chiselled, and the brush left little flared ends. Carvers sharpened them into neat finishing strokes.
"Sans" is French for "without". Sans-serif type was rare until the early 1800s, when British printers used it for loud posters. It was nicknamed "grotesque" because it looked so stark.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Serif", "items": ["Garamond, Times", "books, newspapers", "tradition, authority"]}, "r": {"t": "Sans serif", "items": ["Helvetica, Arial", "signs, apps", "modern, neutral"]}}
### The families inside each family
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Typefaces", "items": ["Old style: Garamond", "Didone: Bodoni", "Slab: Rockwell", "Geometric sans: Futura"]}
Within each group, shapes differ a lot. Futura (1927) is built from circles and triangles. Humanist sans serifs like Gill Sans keep the rhythm of handwriting. Slab serifs have heavy, blocky ends, ideal for posters and typewriters.
### Screens changed the game
Early computer screens had coarse pixels, so fine serifs turned into blurry blobs. Sans serifs dominated interfaces. Today's high-resolution phones render serifs crisply, and serif fonts are returning in apps and websites.
!! Serif fonts are proven to be easier to read => Research comparing the two is mixed; size, spacing and familiarity matter far more than serifs
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Typeface", "b": "the design, e.g. Garamond"}, {"a": "Font", "b": "one style: Garamond Bold 12"}, {"a": "Serif", "b": "small finishing stroke"}]}
> Serifs carry the feel of chiselled stone and printed books; sans serifs carry the feel of signs, machines and screens.

# Gutenberg
Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith from Mainz, did not invent printing. He combined several technologies into a system that could produce books quickly and cheaply.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Metal type cast in a hand mould", "Oil-based ink that sticks", "Screw press, like a wine press", "Paper, cheaper than vellum"]}
The hand mould was the heart of it: it let workers cast thousands of identical letters fast. A page was assembled letter by letter, inked, printed many times, then the type was broken up and reused.
### The Gutenberg Bible
Finished around 1455, it had 42 lines per column and was so carefully made that buyers compared it to fine manuscripts. About 180 copies were printed; around 49 survive, in whole or in part.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cast letters", "Set the page", "Ink and press", "Reuse the type"]}
### An information explosion
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~250", "l": "European towns with presses by 1500"}, {"v": "millions", "l": "books printed by 1500"}]}
Before print, a scribe might copy a book in months. After it, identical copies made it possible to compare, correct and cite. Luther's pamphlets in the 1510s–1520s spread the Reformation; scientists could share precise diagrams.
!? The oldest surviving book printed in Portugal is a Hebrew Pentateuch, printed in Faro in 1487 by the Jewish printer Samuel Gacon.
!! Gutenberg invented movable type => Bi Sheng made clay movable type in China around 1040, and Korea printed with metal type in 1377; Gutenberg's system was what scaled up
> Gutenberg's real invention was a system for making identical copies, and identical copies made modern knowledge possible.

# Hierarchy
Visual hierarchy is the order in which the eye takes in a design. Readers don't read a page; they scan it first and decide whether it's worth their time. Hierarchy decides what they see in those first seconds.
### The tools designers use
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Size", "b": "bigger is read first"}, {"a": "Weight and colour", "b": "bold or bright stands out"}, {"a": "Position", "b": "top-left gets seen early"}, {"a": "Space", "b": "isolated items gain power"}]}
A concert poster is a simple test. First the band's name, then date and venue, then ticket price and small print. If all four lines were the same size, you would have to read everything to find anything.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["1. Band name: huge", "2. Date and venue: medium", "3. Tickets, small print"]}
### How eyes move
In Western languages, eyes tend to start top-left. Eye-tracking studies of web pages often show an **F-pattern**: readers scan across the top, then down the left edge, catching the starts of lines. Simple posters tend to be read in a Z shape.
### Three levels are usually enough
Most good layouts use about three clear levels: primary, secondary and supporting. Add too many sizes and the levels blur into each other.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Make everything bold", "r": "Bold only what matters"}, {"w": "Six font sizes", "r": "Three clear levels"}]}
!? Designers use the "squint test": half-close your eyes and look at the layout. Whatever still stands out is what the reader will notice first.
> If everything is important, nothing is; hierarchy means choosing what wins.

# White space
White space, also called negative space, is any area without text or images. It need not be white: it is simply room. Designers treat it as an active material, like silence in music.
### Two scales of space
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Micro space", "items": ["between letters", "between lines", "inside buttons"]}, "r": {"t": "Macro space", "items": ["margins", "between sections", "around images"]}}
Micro space affects reading comfort. Body text usually needs line spacing of about 1.4–1.6 times the font size; cramped lines make the eye slip to the wrong row.
Macro space affects mood and grouping. Items close together look related; a gap says "new topic". This grouping rule comes from Gestalt psychology, which studied how the brain organises what it sees.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Add space around it", "Element stands alone", "Eye goes there first", "Feels important"]}
### Why space looks expensive
A discount flyer crams every corner because each centimetre has to sell. A luxury brand leaves pages almost empty, signalling it can afford not to shout. Apple's packaging and minimalist fashion advertising use the same trick.
!? The FedEx logo hides an arrow in the white space between the "E" and the "x". Many people use it daily for years before they notice it.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Fill the empty space", "r": "Let key items breathe"}]}
!! White space is wasted space => It improves comprehension and focus, and guides where the eye lands
> Space is not the absence of design; it's what lets the design be seen.

# The grid
A grid is a set of invisible guidelines that divides a page into columns and rows. Designers snap text and images to it, so a page looks ordered even when its content varies.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Columns", "b": "vertical strips for content"}, {"a": "Gutters", "b": "gaps between columns"}, {"a": "Margins", "b": "space around the edge"}, {"a": "Baseline grid", "b": "lines text sits on"}]}
### An old idea
Medieval scribes ruled their pages before writing, and the Gutenberg Bible used two neat columns. In 1981 the Swiss designer Josef Müller-Brockmann published *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, still a standard reference.
### Why 12 columns?
Many websites use a 12-column grid because 12 divides evenly in so many ways.
ex: 12 columns | 2 halves of 6, 3 thirds of 4 | 4 quarters of 3, 6 sixths of 2 | One grid, many layouts
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "ways to split evenly", "items": [{"l": "10 columns", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "12 columns", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "16 columns", "v": 3.0}]}
On phones, the same grid collapses: 12 columns may become one, and content stacks. This is called responsive design.
### Benefits
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Consistent alignment", "Faster layout decisions", "Pages feel part of one family", "Teams can work in parallel"]}
!! A grid makes designs rigid and boring => Once a grid is in place, deliberately breaking it, e.g. with an image bleeding off the page, gets attention
> A grid is the hidden skeleton that makes a page feel calm, even when the content is busy.

# Logos
A logo is a company's signature. It has to be recognised in a split second, on a billboard, a shirt button or a 16-pixel browser tab, often in a single colour.

# Bauhaus
The Bauhaus was founded in Weimar in 1919 by architect Walter Gropius. Its aim was to end the divide between "fine" art and craft and to design well-made objects for mass production.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1919", "l": "Founded in Weimar"}, {"y": "1925", "l": "Moves to Dessau"}, {"y": "1932", "l": "Moves to Berlin"}, {"y": "1933", "l": "Closes under Nazis"}]}
### How it taught
Students began with a foundation course on colour, shape and materials, then trained in workshops: metal, weaving, furniture, printing. Teachers included painters Wassily Kandinsky and Paul Klee, and László Moholy-Nagy.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Building: the ultimate goal", "Workshops: wood, metal, textile", "Foundation course: form, colour"]}
### Objects you still see
• Marcel Breuer's tubular steel chairs, inspired by bicycle handlebars
• Marianne Brandt's geometric metal teapots and lamps
• Herbert Bayer's lowercase-only alphabet, with no capitals at all
The Nazis saw the school as un-German and politically suspect. In 1933, under pressure, it closed. Its teachers scattered to the USA and elsewhere, spreading its ideas.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Bauhaus diaspora", "items": ["New Bauhaus, Chicago", "Black Mountain College", "Tel Aviv White City", "Flat-pack furniture ideas"]}
!! "Form follows function" was the Bauhaus motto => The phrase comes from the American architect Louis Sullivan in 1896; the Bauhaus adopted the spirit, not the slogan
!? Tel Aviv has about 4,000 buildings in Bauhaus-influenced style, many by architects who trained in Germany; the "White City" became a UNESCO site in 2003.
> The Bauhaus lasted only 14 years, yet its idea that good design should be simple, useful and mass-produced shapes the objects around us.

# Swiss style
The **International Typographic Style**, usually called Swiss style, grew in Zurich and Basel in the 1950s. Its designers believed communication should be clear and objective, with the designer's personal taste kept out of the way.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Strict mathematical grids", "Sans-serif type", "Text flush left, ragged right", "Photos, not illustrations"]}
Key figures included Josef Müller-Brockmann, known for concert posters built from pure geometry, and Armin Hofmann and Emil Ruder, who taught in Basel.
### The birth of Helvetica
In 1957 Max Miedinger and Eduard Hoffmann at the Haas foundry near Basel released **Neue Haas Grotesk**. It was renamed Helvetica, from the Latin name for Switzerland, in 1960 to sell better abroad. The same year saw Adrian Frutiger's Univers, a rival with a numbered family of weights.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1957", "l": "Neue Haas Grotesk"}, {"y": "1960", "l": "Renamed Helvetica"}, {"y": "1981", "l": "Grid Systems book"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "Helvetica film"}]}
### Everywhere and nowhere
Helvetica spread through company logos, airline branding and transport signage, including the New York subway. Its neutrality made it a corporate favourite; critics called it bland.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Swiss style", "items": ["asymmetric, open", "objective tone", "sans serif"]}, "r": {"t": "Earlier posters", "items": ["centred, ornate", "emotional, painted", "decorative lettering"]}}
!? The 2007 documentary *Helvetica* by Gary Hustwit made a typeface a film star and helped turn type into a popular topic.
> Swiss style treated design as clear communication, and its grids and sans serifs became the default look of the modern world.

# Readability
**Legibility** is whether you can tell letters apart; **readability** is whether you can read a whole page comfortably. Body text is where readability matters most, because readers spend minutes or hours on it.
### Line length
Very long lines make the eye struggle to find the start of the next line. Very short lines break up phrases and tire the eye with constant jumps. Typographer Robert Bringhurst suggests around 66 characters as ideal for a single column.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 20.0, "hi": 120.0, "unit": "characters per line", "items": [{"l": "Too short", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Comfortable", "v": 66.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Too long", "v": 110.0}]}
ex: Column 600 px wide | Average character at 16 px ≈ 8–9 px | About 70 characters per line
### Size, spacing and contrast
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Text size", "b": "about 16 px on screens"}, {"a": "Line spacing", "b": "about 1.4–1.6 × size"}, {"a": "Contrast", "b": "at least 4.5 : 1 (WCAG)"}]}
The 4.5:1 contrast ratio comes from the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, which European accessibility law draws on. Light grey text on white often fails it, especially for older readers.
### Alignment
Left-aligned text keeps even spacing between words. Justified text, flush at both edges, can create gappy "rivers" of white unless hyphenation is good.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Pale grey, tiny text", "r": "Dark text, 16 px or more"}, {"w": "Full-width lines", "r": "Column of ~66 characters"}]}
!! Special fonts cure dyslexia => Studies have not shown that dyslexia fonts beat a clear ordinary font with good size and spacing
> Comfortable reading comes from a moderate line length, generous spacing and strong contrast, not from one magic font.

# Design is problem-solving
Designers start with a question, not a style: who needs to do what, and what gets in their way? The answer might be a poster, a form or a sign, and it might look very plain.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Understand users", "Define the problem", "Sketch ideas", "Prototype", "Test and refine"]}
### Classic solutions
In 1931 engineering draughtsman **Harry Beck** redrew the London Underground map like an electrical circuit: straight lines, even spacing, ignoring true distances. Passengers only needed order and connections. Transit maps worldwide, including Lisbon's and Porto's metro maps, follow his approach.
In the 1960s, **Jock Kinneir and Margaret Calvert** designed Britain's road signs: a new typeface readable at speed, with colours and shapes coding meaning.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tube map, 1933", "b": "clarity over geography"}, {"a": "UK road signs", "b": "readable at speed"}, {"a": "Simple forms", "b": "fewer errors, less effort"}]}
### When design fails
In the 2000 US presidential election, Palm Beach County in Florida used a "butterfly ballot". Candidates' names sat on both sides of a central column of punch holes. Many voters appear to have punched the wrong hole, in an election decided by a few hundred votes.
### Test with real people
Designers are poor judges of their own work because they already know how it works. Usability testing means watching a few real users try a task.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "5", "l": "users in a quick test"}, {"v": "most", "l": "big problems found"}]}
!? Usability expert Jakob Nielsen argued that testing with about five users reveals most major problems, and repeated small tests beat one big one.
> Design succeeds when people can do what they came to do without having to think about the design.

# The colour wheel
Isaac Newton drew the first colour circle in his book *Opticks* (1704). He bent the rainbow's spectrum round until red met violet, showing that colours form a loop rather than a line.
Painters built practical wheels from the pigments they mixed every day. In the traditional version, three primaries mix into three secondaries, and mixing neighbours again gives six tertiaries, such as red-orange or blue-green.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Primaries: red, yellow, blue", "Secondaries: orange, green, purple", "Tertiaries: in between"]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Red ↔ green", "b": "complementary pair"}, {"a": "Blue ↔ orange", "b": "complementary pair"}, {"a": "Yellow ↔ purple", "b": "complementary pair"}]}
### What complementaries do
Placed side by side, complementary colours make each other look more intense. Mixed together as paint, they cancel into greys and browns. Painters use this to tone down a colour that is too loud without using black: a touch of red calms a harsh green.
### A wheel for the Bauhaus
Johannes Itten, who taught the foundation course at the Bauhaus, published a 12-part wheel in 1961 that art students still use. Goethe, in 1810, had already studied how colours feel, not just how they mix.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1704", "l": "Newton's circle"}, {"y": "1810", "l": "Goethe's theory"}, {"y": "1961", "l": "Itten's 12-part wheel"}]}
!! Red, yellow and blue are the true primaries that make every colour => No three pigments mix every colour; printers get a wider range from cyan, magenta and yellow
> The colour wheel is a map of relationships: neighbours blend, opposites clash and intensify.

# Light vs paint
Your eye has three kinds of colour-sensing cells, called cones, tuned roughly to red, green and blue light. Every colour you see is your brain comparing how strongly each type fires. That is why three primaries are enough to fake almost any colour.
### Adding light
A screen starts black and adds light. Each pixel has tiny red, green and blue parts. Full red plus full green looks yellow; all three at full strength look white.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Additive (light)", "items": ["starts black", "red, green, blue", "all = white"]}, "r": {"t": "Subtractive (ink)", "items": ["starts white", "cyan, magenta, yellow", "all = near black"]}}
### Taking light away
Ink and paint work the other way. A pigment absorbs some wavelengths and reflects the rest. Each layer of ink subtracts more light from the white paper.
ex: Cyan ink absorbs red | Yellow ink absorbs blue | Together only green is reflected | Cyan + yellow = green
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "CMYK", "items": [{"s": "C", "m": "cyan: absorbs red"}, {"s": "M", "m": "magenta: absorbs green"}, {"s": "Y", "m": "yellow: absorbs blue"}, {"s": "K", "m": "key: black ink"}]}
### Why printers add black
Real cyan, magenta and yellow inks together give a muddy dark brown, not a true black. A separate black ink is sharper for text and cheaper than laying down three colours. The K stands for "key", the plate that carried the detail.
!? Look at a screen through a magnifying glass or a drop of water and you can see the separate red, green and blue dots.
> Screens build colour by adding light; printers build colour by subtracting it.

# Hue, saturation, value
Ordinary words like "dark blue" or "bright red" mix up three separate properties. Separating them gives artists and designers precise control. The American painter Albert Munsell built a colour system on these three dimensions in 1905.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hue", "b": "which colour: red, blue…"}, {"a": "Saturation", "b": "vivid or greyish"}, {"a": "Value", "b": "how light or dark"}]}
### Value does the heavy lifting
Our vision is far more sensitive to differences in lightness than to differences in hue. A picture with the right values but odd colours still reads perfectly; a picture with lovely colours but flat values looks muddy.
ex: Take a colour photo | Convert it to black and white | If shapes still read clearly | The values are working
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 100.0, "unit": "lightness (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Pure blue", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "Pure red", "v": 53.0}, {"l": "Pure yellow", "v": 97.0, "hi": true}]}
Pure hues are not equally light. Bright yellow is almost as light as white, while pure blue is fairly dark. That's why yellow text on white is nearly unreadable.
### Using saturation
Fully saturated colour everywhere is exhausting. Painters often keep most areas greyish and save full intensity for the focal point. Landscape painters also reduce saturation in the distance.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Change the hue to fix it", "r": "Check the value first"}]}
!? Many painters make a quick black-and-white study before starting a colour painting, solving the values first.
> Get the lights and darks right and the colours can do almost anything.

# Colour harmonies
A colour harmony is a recipe based on positions on the colour wheel. It doesn't guarantee beauty, but it gives a starting point that usually works.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Complementary", "b": "opposites: bold contrast"}, {"a": "Analogous", "b": "neighbours: calm, unified"}, {"a": "Triadic", "b": "3 evenly spaced: lively"}, {"a": "Monochromatic", "b": "one hue, many shades"}]}
### Seeing them in the world
Van Gogh painted *The Night Café* (1888) in clashing reds and greens to express what he called the terrible passions of humanity. Sunsets and autumn woods are natural analogous schemes of reds, oranges and yellows. Comic books often use triadic red, yellow and blue.
### Proportion matters more than the rule
Equal amounts of two strong colours fight. Interior designers use a rough 60–30–10 guideline: one dominant colour, one supporting, one accent.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of the space (guideline)", "items": [{"l": "Dominant", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Secondary", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Accent", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}]}
ex: Living room | 60% soft grey-blue walls and sofa | 30% warm wood | 10% orange cushions as the spark
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Equal amounts of each", "r": "One leads, one accents"}]}
!? A split-complementary scheme replaces one side of a complementary pair with its two neighbours, e.g. blue with yellow-orange and red-orange, giving contrast with less clash.
> Harmonies tell you which colours to pick; proportion tells you how much of each to use.

# Warm and cool
The warm/cool split is about association. Reds and oranges remind us of fire and sun; blues and greens of water, shade and ice. It's one of the most consistent colour impressions across cultures.
### Advancing and receding
Over long distances, air scatters blue light. Far-off mountains look bluer, paler and less sharp. Leonardo da Vinci described this **atmospheric perspective**, and painters copy it: cool, greyish colours for the background, warm and strong ones in front.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Warm", "items": ["come forward", "energetic, cosy", "fire, sun"]}, "r": {"t": "Cool", "items": ["recede", "calm, spacious", "sea, sky, shade"]}}
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Foreground: warm, saturated", "Middle: softer colours", "Distance: cool, pale blue"]}
### It's relative
Temperature depends on neighbours. A lemon yellow can look cool next to orange; a purple-red feels cool beside orange-red. Painters talk about a "warm blue" or a "cool red".
### A confusing twist with light bulbs
Lighting uses kelvin to describe colour temperature, and the scale runs the other way. A candle is about 1,900 K and looks orange; daylight is about 6,500 K and looks bluish.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1500.0, "hi": 7000.0, "unit": "kelvin", "items": [{"l": "Candle", "v": 1900.0}, {"l": "Warm bulb", "v": 2700.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Daylight", "v": 6500.0}]}
!? Restaurants and homes often use warm 2,700 K bulbs to feel cosy, while offices and hospitals favour cooler light for alertness.
> Warm colours advance and energise; cool colours recede and calm, but only relative to what's next to them.

# Colour and culture
The physics of colour is universal, but meanings are learned. The same red can mean luck, love, danger, communism or a football club depending on where you stand.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "White", "b": "Western weddings; mourning in China"}, {"a": "Red", "b": "Chinese luck; stop signs"}, {"a": "Black", "b": "European mourning"}, {"a": "Purple", "b": "Roman emperors' colour"}]}
### Meanings change over time
White wedding dresses became fashionable after Queen Victoria wore one in 1840; before that brides wore their best dress in any colour. Purple was royal because Tyrian purple, made from sea snails, was extremely costly. Once synthetic dyes arrived in the 1850s, anyone could wear it.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Antiquity", "l": "Purple for emperors"}, {"y": "1840", "l": "Victoria's white dress"}, {"y": "1856", "l": "First synthetic dye"}]}
### A Portuguese view
Black mourning was long a strong custom in Portugal, and older widows in villages often wore black for the rest of their lives. The national flag's green and red, adopted in 1911, signalled the new republic, breaking with the blue and white of the monarchy.
### Universal patterns
Some links are widespread: red for urgency, partly because it's the colour of blood and ripe fruit. That's why international standards use red for stop and danger, so a road sign means the same in Lisbon and Lagos.
!! Pink has always been for girls and blue for boys => In the early 1900s, some shops and magazines recommended pink for boys; the current rule settled only in the mid-20th century
> Colour meanings are a language, and like any language, they differ by place and drift over time.

# Expensive blue
Ultramarine comes from **lapis lazuli**, a deep blue stone mined for about 6,000 years at Sar-e-Sang in the Badakhshan mountains of Afghanistan. The name means "beyond the sea", because the stone had to be shipped to Europe from far away.
### Why it cost so much
Grinding lapis simply gives a dull greyish blue, because the stone also contains other minerals. The pure blue had to be extracted by a laborious process described by Cennino Cennini around 1400.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Grind the lapis", "Knead into wax dough", "Squeeze in lye", "Blue drains out", "Repeat: weaker grades"]}
The first extraction gave the richest blue; later ones gave paler "ultramarine ash". Painting contracts sometimes stated exactly how much ultramarine, at what price per ounce, the patron would pay for.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ultramarine", "items": ["lapis, Afghanistan", "intense, stable", "luxury price"]}, "r": {"t": "Azurite", "items": ["copper mineral", "greener blue", "much cheaper"]}}
### Used with care
Because of its cost and symbolism, it was reserved for the Virgin Mary's mantle and other holy subjects. Johannes Vermeer, unusually, used it lavishly, even in shadows, which may have contributed to his debts.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "6th–7th c.", "l": "Bamiyan murals"}, {"y": "c. 1400", "l": "Cennini's recipe"}, {"y": "1660s", "l": "Vermeer's blues"}, {"y": "1828", "l": "Synthetic version"}]}
!? In 1824 a French society offered a prize of 6,000 francs for a synthetic ultramarine. Jean-Baptiste Guimet won it in 1828, and the price of the blue collapsed.
> For centuries, using ultramarine was a statement of devotion and wealth, painted in a colour that cost a fortune.

# Colour blindness
Most colour vision deficiency is inherited. The genes for the red- and green-sensitive cones sit on the X chromosome. Men have one X, so one faulty copy is enough; women usually have a second, working copy. That's why it's far more common in men.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1 in 12", "l": "men (Northern European descent)"}, {"v": "1 in 200", "l": "women"}]}

Few people see in total greyscale. Most see plenty of colour but confuse certain pairs, such as red and green, or brown and green.
!? The chemist John Dalton described his own colour blindness in 1794, which is why in Portuguese it's called **daltonismo**.
### Designing for everyone

Traffic lights are a classic solution: position (top, middle, bottom) carries the meaning, so colour is a bonus.
### A Portuguese invention
Portuguese designer Miguel Neiva created **ColorADD**, a code of simple symbols for colours. It's used on school pencils, maps and hospital signs in Portugal and abroad.
> If information is carried by colour alone, about one man in twelve may miss it.

# Simultaneous contrast
Our eyes don't measure colours in isolation. The visual system constantly compares each area with its surroundings, exaggerating differences at edges. It is a useful trick for spotting objects, but it means colours shift with their neighbours.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Grey on black", "items": ["looks lighter", "seems to glow"]}, "r": {"t": "Grey on white", "items": ["looks darker", "seems heavier"]}}
### Discovered at a tapestry works
In the 1820s, the French chemist **Michel-Eugène Chevreul** was asked why some dyes at the Gobelins tapestry factory looked dull. The dyes were fine; their neighbours were changing them. His book on the law of simultaneous contrast (1839) influenced Delacroix, the Impressionists and Seurat.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1839", "l": "Chevreul's law"}, {"y": "1880s", "l": "Seurat's dots"}, {"y": "1963", "l": "Albers' book"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "'The dress' debate"}]}
### Colour shifts toward the opposite
A grey on a red background takes on a slightly greenish tint; on blue it looks faintly orange. The surrounding colour pushes the grey toward its complementary.
Josef Albers, a Bauhaus teacher, built a whole course on this. His book *Interaction of Color* (1963) shows the same colour looking like two different ones, and two different colours looking identical.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Grey on red looks greenish", "Grey on blue looks orangey", "Same paint, different look"]}
!? In 2015 a photo of a dress split the internet into "blue and black" and "white and gold". People's brains were assuming different lighting and correcting the colours differently.
> There is no such thing as a colour on its own; every colour is judged against its neighbours.

# Klein blue
Yves Klein (1928–1962), from Nice, wanted colour itself to be the artwork, with no drawing, no subject and no brushstroke to distract. He chose ultramarine as the purest expression of space and the immaterial.
### The problem with paint
Dry ultramarine pigment is dazzling, but mixing it with oil or other binders dulls it. Klein worked with a Paris colour merchant and a chemist to find a synthetic resin binder that held the powder in place without darkening it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pure pigment: dazzling", "Oil binder: dulls it", "Resin binder: keeps glow", "IKB"]}
### Registering, not patenting
In 1960 Klein recorded the formula with France's intellectual property office using a dated sealed envelope. It protected his process, not the colour as such, and anyone can buy ultramarine.
!! Yves Klein owned the colour blue => He registered a binder method in 1960; a colour itself can't be owned, though versions of "Klein blue" are sold commercially
### Art as provocation
In Milan in 1957 he showed eleven identical blue canvases, each with a different price. Buyers were paying for something invisible: the artist's "sensibility". In his *Anthropometries* (1960) models covered in IKB pressed their bodies onto canvas while musicians played.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1957", "l": "11 blue canvases"}, {"y": "1960", "l": "IKB registered"}, {"y": "1960", "l": "Anthropometries"}, {"y": "1962", "l": "Klein dies at 34"}]}
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Blue monochrome canvases", "Painted sponge reliefs", "Body prints, Anthropometries"]}
!? Klein also made gold and pink monochromes, completing a trio he linked to spiritual ideas, but the blue became his signature.
> Klein turned a single colour into the whole subject of a painting.

# Blues roots
The blues took shape in the Mississippi Delta and across the American South around 1900. It drew on African traditions carried through slavery: field hollers, work songs with call and response, and church spirituals.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Work songs, spirituals", "Delta blues", "Electric Chicago blues", "Rock and roll"]}
### The 12-bar form
Most blues follows a 12-bar pattern using just three chords, built on the 1st, 4th and 5th notes of the scale. Lyrics often state a line, repeat it, then answer it, an AAB pattern.
ex: Key of E: E–E–E–E | A–A–E–E | B–A–E–E | 12 bars, 3 chords
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "bars per chord, 12-bar blues", "items": [{"l": "I chord (E)", "v": 8.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "IV chord (A)", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "V chord (B)", "v": 2.0}]}
### Blue notes
Blues singers bend certain notes, especially the 3rd and 7th, a little flat. These "blue notes" fall between the keys of a piano, giving the sound its ache.

# Jazz
New Orleans was a port where French, Spanish, African, Caribbean and American cultures mixed. Brass bands played at parades and funerals, ragtime pianists played in bars, and the blues arrived from the countryside. Around 1900, jazz emerged from that mix.

# Why songs get stuck
Psychologists call an earworm **involuntary musical imagery**. Surveys suggest that around nine in ten people get one at least weekly, usually a chorus fragment that loops for minutes or hours.
### What makes a tune sticky
A 2016 study by music psychologists at Durham and Goldsmiths compared common earworms with similar songs that weren't. Sticky songs tended to share a few features.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Fairly fast, upbeat tempo", "Simple, familiar melody shape", "An unusual leap or repeat", "Heard often, recently"]}
### Why the loop runs
One theory is that the brain treats a song fragment as an unfinished task. It keeps replaying it, the way an interrupted job keeps nagging you. Stress, tiredness and idle moments make earworms more likely.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Hear a catchy hook", "Brain stores fragment", "Idle moment", "Fragment replays"]}
### How to get rid of one
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Chewing gum", "b": "keeps mouth busy, less inner singing"}, {"a": "Medium puzzle", "b": "anagrams or sudoku"}, {"a": "Play it to the end", "b": "'finishes' the task"}]}

# Why we dance
Dance is older than writing. Rock paintings and Egyptian tomb scenes show lines of people moving together, and anthropologists have never found a human society without some form of dance.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why people dance", "items": ["Celebration", "Worship and ritual", "Courtship", "Telling stories"]}
### Born to move to a beat
Babies bounce and sway to music before they can walk. Very few animals can lock their movements to a beat; a cockatoo called Snowball became famous in 2009 for doing it to pop songs. Researchers think beat-keeping links to our ability to learn complex sounds, such as speech.
### The chemistry of togetherness
Moving in sync with others seems to make people feel closer. In Oxford experiments, people who danced in synchrony could tolerate more pain afterwards, a sign that the body's natural painkillers, endorphins, had been released. They also reported feeling more bonded with their group.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Move in time together", "Endorphins released", "Pain feels weaker", "Group feels closer"]}
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Dancing alone", "items": ["exercise, joy", "self-expression"]}, "r": {"t": "Dancing in sync", "items": ["stronger bonding", "shared identity"]}}
### A Portuguese example
Every June, Lisbon's neighbourhoods compete in the **Marchas Populares** for the feast of St Anthony. Each bairro spends months rehearsing costumes, songs and choreography, and the parade down the Avenida da Liberdade is a show of local pride.
!? Armies, sports fans and religious processions all use synchronised movement; marching in step is a very old way of making individuals feel like one body.
> Dancing together is one of humanity's oldest technologies for turning a crowd into a community.

# Ballet
Ballet grew out of the lavish entertainments of Italian Renaissance courts. When Catherine de' Medici became queen of France, she brought these spectacles with her. The *Ballet Comique de la Reine* (1581) is often called the first ballet: over five hours of dance, music, poetry and scenery.

### The Sun King dances
Louis XIV trained daily and performed the rising sun in the *Ballet de la Nuit* (1653), which gave him his nickname. Dance was politics: courtiers who moved gracefully showed refinement and loyalty. In 1661 he founded the Académie Royale de Danse to set standards, and his ballet master Pierre Beauchamp codified the five positions of the feet.

# Modern dance
Around 1900, some dancers felt ballet's corsets, pointe shoes and fairy tales had nothing to do with real life. Modern dance set out to express inner feelings with the natural body.
### Isadora Duncan
Isadora Duncan (1877–1927), an American, danced barefoot in loose tunics inspired by ancient Greek vases. She moved from the solar plexus, using running, skipping and swaying. Audiences in Europe were stunned. She died in Nice when her long scarf caught in a car wheel.
### Martha Graham
Martha Graham (1894–1991) built a full technique that dancers still study. Its core is **contraction and release**: the torso hollows on an exhale, then expands on the inhale. Her movements were angular, grounded and intense, the opposite of ballet's lightness.

# The tango
Buenos Aires and Montevideo boomed in the late 1800s. Millions of immigrants, mostly young men from Italy and Spain, crowded into shared tenement houses alongside Afro-Argentine and Afro-Uruguayan communities and rural migrants.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Tango's roots", "items": ["Candombe (African)", "Habanera (Cuba)", "Milonga (local)", "European dances"]}
### The sound
The signature instrument is the **bandoneón**, a square button accordion invented in Germany for church music. In tango it gives a sighing, breathing sound. Early tango was played in bars and dance halls on the city fringes.
### The dance
Tango is danced in a close embrace and is improvised. The leader proposes each step through the chest and the follower interprets it in real time. Signature moves include walks, figure-of-eight "ochos" and quick leg flicks called "ganchos".

### Respectable via Paris
Around 1912–1913, tango became a craze in Paris and London. Once European high society embraced it, the Argentine elite who had despised it accepted it too.

# Portuguese folk dances
Almost every Portuguese region has its own dances, music and costumes. Many are kept alive by **ranchos folclóricos**, village folk groups that perform at festivals and romarias.

### The vira of the Minho
The vira is a lively dance in triple time. Couples form a circle, turning and changing direction. It's especially associated with the Romaria da Senhora d'Agonia in Viana do Castelo each August. Women wear embroidered red or blue costumes and gold filigree jewellery.

In the far northeast, teams of eight men in white skirts, embroidered shirts and hats dance with wooden sticks called *paulitos*. In the Algarve, the corridinho is a quick dance in two-beat time. Couples run and spin to accordion music, with stamping and changes of direction.

# Flamenco
Flamenco took shape in Andalusia in the late 1700s and 1800s. It mixes influences from the Gitanos (Spanish Roma, who arrived in the 1400s), Andalusian folk music, and older Moorish and Jewish traditions.

Many flamenco styles, such as the soleá and bulería, use a 12-beat cycle with accents on beats 3, 6, 8, 10 and 12. The dancers' heels and the clapping (*palmas*) are percussion instruments too.

In the mid-1800s, **cafés cantantes** in Seville and other cities hired professional flamenco artists, turning a family and community art into a stage art. In the 20th century, guitarist Paco de Lucía and singer Camarón de la Isla modernised it.

# Street dance
Street dances were invented outside dance schools, in parks, clubs and neighbourhoods, mostly by Black and Latino young people in US cities. They spread through battles, TV and later YouTube.

### Breaking
Breaking grew up with hip hop at Bronx parties, where dancers went wild during a record's drum break. Its dancers are b-boys and b-girls.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Freezes: hold a pose", "Power moves: windmills, flares", "Footwork on the floor", "Toprock: standing steps"]}
### The battle
Street dance is competitive. Two dancers or crews take turns, improvising to music they don't know in advance and responding to each other's moves.
### Olympic debut
Breaking first appeared at the Youth Olympics in Buenos Aires in 2018, then at the Paris 2024 Olympics. Judges scored technique, vocabulary, originality, execution and musicality. B-girl Ami of Japan and B-boy Phil Wizard of Canada won gold.

# Kizomba
Kizomba emerged in Angola in the 1980s. Angolan musicians blended local **semba** with **zouk**, a style from the French Caribbean made popular by the band Kassav'. The word means "party" in Kimbundu, one of Angola's languages.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Semba (Angola)", "+ zouk (Caribbean)", "Kizomba, 1980s", "Lisbon clubs, 1990s", "Europe-wide, 2000s"]}
### Why Lisbon?
After Angolan independence in 1975 and during the long civil war, many Angolans settled in Portugal. In the 1990s, Lisbon's African clubs became the meeting point for kizomba music and dancing. From there, teachers and festivals carried it to Spain, France and the rest of Europe.
### How it's danced
Kizomba is a close, smooth partner dance. The couple walks and shifts weight in a soft embrace, with the leader guiding through the torso rather than the hands. There are no flashy spins at its core; connection and musicality matter most.

### Variations

!? Lisbon hosts kizomba nights almost every day of the week, and dancers from across Europe travel there to learn from Angolan and Portuguese teachers.
> Kizomba travelled from Luanda to Lisbon and then across Europe, carried by music and migration.

# Dance and the brain
Dancing is a whole-brain workout. You hear rhythm, plan steps, remember sequences, keep your balance and watch a partner, all at once.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Auditory cortex", "b": "hears the beat"}, {"a": "Cerebellum", "b": "timing and balance"}, {"a": "Basal ganglia", "b": "rhythm and movement"}, {"a": "Prefrontal areas", "b": "memory, planning"}]}
### What the evidence suggests
A 2003 study of older adults in New York followed hundreds of people for years. Among leisure activities, frequent social dancing was linked with a lower risk of dementia. Such studies show associations, not proof, since active, social people may differ in other ways.
In a German trial published in 2017, older volunteers who learned new dance routines for 18 months showed changes in brain areas linked with memory and balance, more than a group doing standard fitness training.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Better balance, fewer falls", "Mood and social contact", "Possible memory benefits"]}
### Dance for Parkinson's
People with Parkinson's disease often find it easier to move to music. A strong beat can help start and steady steps. Programmes like Dance for PD, begun in New York in 2001, now run worldwide, including in Portugal.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Hear the beat", "Plan the step", "Move and balance", "Feel rewarded"]}
!! Only intense exercise benefits the brain => Gentle social dancing combines movement, learning and company, which together may matter as much as effort
> Dance trains body, memory and social connection at once, which may be why it is especially good for ageing well.

# Why myths matter
The Greek word *mythos* simply meant "story" or "speech". Myths are sacred or traditional stories that a community tells about gods, heroes and beginnings. They are not just "false stories"; they carry meaning.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "What myths do", "items": ["Explain nature", "Teach values", "Justify customs", "Give identity"]}
### Explaining the world
Before science, myths explained thunder, seasons and death. The Greeks said winter came because Demeter mourned while her daughter Persephone spent part of each year in the underworld.
### Giving a people an origin
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rome", "b": "Romulus and Remus"}, {"a": "Lisbon", "b": "founded by Ulysses (legend)"}, {"a": "Lusitania", "b": "named after Lusus (legend)"}]}
Portuguese tradition claimed Lisbon's old name, Olisipo, came from Ulysses, the wandering Greek hero. Camões repeated the legend in *Os Lusíadas* (1572), giving the young nation a link to Homer's world.
### The story pattern beneath many stories
In 1949 the American scholar Joseph Campbell argued that hero myths across the world share a pattern, which he called the **monomyth**.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Call to adventure", "Mentor and threshold", "Trials and ordeal", "Reward", "Return changed"]}
!! "Myth" just means a lie => In scholarship a myth is a meaningful traditional story; whether it is literally true is a separate question
> Myths are how communities explain where they came from and how to live.

# Greek gods
The Greek gods were the centre of a living religion, with temples, sacrifices and festivals. The Parthenon was built for Athena; the theatre of Athens honoured Dionysus.
### The family on the mountain
Mount Olympus, at about 2,918 metres the highest mountain in Greece, was imagined as the gods' home. The twelve Olympians were a quarrelsome family, led by Zeus, who had overthrown his father Cronus and the Titans.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Zeus", "b": "sky, thunder, king"}, {"a": "Hera", "b": "marriage, queen"}, {"a": "Athena", "b": "wisdom, crafts, war"}, {"a": "Poseidon", "b": "sea, earthquakes"}]}
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Olympians: Zeus and family", "Titans: Cronus, Rhea", "Primordial: Chaos, Gaia"]}
### Gods with human flaws
Greek gods were immortal and powerful, but not moral examples. Zeus was a serial philanderer; Hera punished his lovers; Apollo and Artemis killed the children of Niobe for boasting. Many stories warn against **hubris**, human pride that challenges the gods.
### Where we learn them
Our main sources are poets: Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* and Hesiod's *Theogony*, all from around 700 BCE, plus later tragedies and the Roman poet Ovid.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 700 BCE", "l": "Homer and Hesiod"}, {"y": "432 BCE", "l": "Parthenon finished"}, {"y": "8 CE", "l": "Ovid's Metamorphoses"}]}
!? Hades, ruler of the underworld, isn't usually counted among the twelve Olympians because he lived below ground, not on the mountain.
> The Greek gods were powerful, emotional and flawed, and their stories explored human behaviour on a cosmic scale.

# Words from myths
Greek and Roman myths are a hidden layer in many European languages. Portuguese shares most of them: *pânico*, *eco*, *narcisista*, *calcanhar de Aquiles*.

### The stories behind them
• **Echo** was cursed by Hera so she could only repeat others' last words; she faded until only her voice remained
• **Narcissus** fell in love with his own reflection in a pool and wasted away
• **Achilles** was dipped in the river Styx by his mother, who held him by the heel, the one place left vulnerable
• **Tantalus** was punished by standing in water under fruit trees, both always just out of reach

# Roman versions
As Rome absorbed Greek culture, it matched its own gods with Greek ones. Jupiter, an old Italic sky god, took on Zeus's stories; Minerva took Athena's. Some gods, like Apollo, kept their Greek name.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Zeus → Jupiter", "b": "king of the gods"}, {"a": "Ares → Mars", "b": "war"}, {"a": "Aphrodite → Venus", "b": "love and beauty"}, {"a": "Hermes → Mercury", "b": "messenger, trade"}]}
### Written in the sky
Planets were named by their character. Mercury moves fastest, like the messenger. Venus shines brightest, like the goddess of beauty. Mars glows red like blood. Jupiter, the largest, is the king.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "planet diameter, thousand km", "items": [{"l": "Mercury", "v": 4.9}, {"l": "Venus", "v": 12.1}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 6.8}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 139.8, "hi": true}]}
!? Uranus is the exception: it's named after the Greek sky god Ouranos, with no Roman equivalent. It was discovered in 1781, long after the others.
### Days and months
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["January: Janus, god of doorways", "March: Mars", "June: Juno", "Saturday: Saturn"]}
In French and Spanish, weekdays honour Roman gods: Tuesday is Mars' day (*martes*), Wednesday Mercury's, Thursday Jupiter's, Friday Venus's.
### Why Portuguese is different
Portuguese is unusual: its weekdays are numbered, *segunda-feira*, *terça-feira* and so on. The church in the region, notably St Martin of Braga in the 6th century, disliked naming days after pagan gods, and church Latin numbering won out.
> The Romans renamed the Greek gods, and those Roman names still sit on our planets, months and many weekdays.

# Norse myths
Norse myths come mainly from two Icelandic books written in the 1200s, after Iceland had become Christian: the *Poetic Edda*, a collection of older poems, and the *Prose Edda* by Snorri Sturluson, a handbook for poets.

Yggdrasil is a giant ash tree linking nine worlds, including Asgard (the gods), Midgard (humans) and Hel (the dead).

# Egyptian myths
Egyptian religion lasted around 3,000 years and had many versions. The fullest connected telling of the Osiris story actually comes from the Greek writer Plutarch, around 100 CE.
### Murder and resurrection
Osiris was a good king. His jealous brother Set tricked him into a chest, sealed it and threw it into the Nile; later he cut the body into pieces. Osiris's wife Isis searched Egypt, gathered the pieces and, with the jackal god Anubis, wrapped them into the first mummy. She conceived a son, Horus, who later defeated Set.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Set kills Osiris", "Isis gathers him", "First mummy made", "Osiris rules the dead", "Horus defeats Set"]}
The story mirrored nature: Osiris, often painted green, was linked to the Nile flood and new crops after the dry season.
### Weighing the heart
The *Book of the Dead* describes the judgement. The dead person's heart is placed on a scale against the feather of **Maat**, goddess of truth and order. Anubis checks the scale, and Thoth, the ibis-headed scribe, records the result.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Heart balances", "items": ["life was just", "enter the Field of Reeds"]}, "r": {"t": "Heart heavier", "items": ["life was sinful", "eaten by Ammit"]}}
The dead recited a list of sins they had not committed, such as "I have not stolen", a kind of moral checklist.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Living pharaoh", "b": "identified with Horus"}, {"a": "Dead pharaoh", "b": "identified with Osiris"}]}
!? A beautiful version of the weighing scene is on the Papyrus of Hunefer, made about 3,300 years ago and now in the British Museum.
> Egyptian myth turned death into a journey and a trial, with a good life as the passport to eternity.

# Creation stories
Every culture asks how the world began. The answers fall into a handful of recurring types.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "From chaos", "b": "Greek: Chaos, then Gaia"}, {"a": "From an egg", "b": "Chinese Pangu, Hindu texts"}, {"a": "From a giant's body", "b": "Norse Ymir"}, {"a": "From a word", "b": "Genesis: 'Let there be light'"}]}
### Building with a body
In Norse myth, Odin and his brothers kill the giant Ymir. His flesh becomes earth, his blood the sea, his skull the sky. In Chinese myth, Pangu grows inside a cosmic egg for 18,000 years, splits it into sky and earth, and his body becomes the mountains and rivers.
### Order out of chaos
The Babylonian *Enuma Elish* tells how the god Marduk defeats Tiamat, a goddess of the salt sea, and splits her body to form heaven and earth. Many myths share the idea that creation means imposing order on watery chaos.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Watery chaos", "A god or force acts", "Sky and earth separate", "Life and humans"]}
### Earth divers and world parents
In many Native American myths, an animal dives to the bottom of a primal ocean and brings up mud that grows into land. In Māori tradition, the sky father and earth mother are pushed apart by their children to let light in.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Shared questions", "items": ["What came first?", "Why is there order?", "Where do humans fit?"]}
!? The Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître, who proposed what became the Big Bang theory around 1931, described the early universe as a "primeval atom", sometimes called a cosmic egg.
> Creation myths differ in detail but ask the same questions: where order came from and where we fit.

# The flood
In 1872 George Smith, a self-taught assistant at the British Museum, deciphered a clay tablet from Nineveh. It told of a man warned by a god to build a boat before a great flood. Smith was reportedly so excited he began undressing in the reading room.
### The Mesopotamian flood
The story appears in the *Epic of Atrahasis* and on Tablet XI of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*. The hero, Utnapishtim, builds a boat, saves his family and animals, lands on a mountain and releases birds to find dry land.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Mesopotamia", "b": "Utnapishtim, warned by Ea"}, {"a": "Bible", "b": "Noah, warned by God"}, {"a": "Greece", "b": "Deucalion and Pyrrha"}, {"a": "India", "b": "Manu, warned by a fish"}]}
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gilgamesh", "items": ["many gods", "a dove, swallow, raven", "sacrifice after"]}, "r": {"t": "Noah", "items": ["one God", "a raven, then a dove", "sacrifice after"]}}
The parallels are close enough that most scholars think the biblical story drew on older Mesopotamian traditions.
### Greece and India
After the Greek flood, Deucalion and his wife Pyrrha throw stones over their shoulders, and the stones become new people. In India, the god Vishnu as a fish warns Manu and tows his boat to safety.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 1700 BCE", "l": "Atrahasis epic"}, {"y": "c. 1200 BCE", "l": "Gilgamesh standard"}, {"y": "1872", "l": "Smith reads tablet"}]}
### Real floods behind myths?
Mesopotamia lies between two rivers that flooded violently, and archaeologists have found thick flood layers at ancient cities. At the end of the last Ice Age, melting ice raised sea levels by over 100 metres, drowning coastlines. These may have fed flood memories, but no single event explains all the stories.
> Flood myths imagine the world washed clean and restarted, a fear and hope shared by cultures across the world.

# Clothes as language
Before anyone says a word, clothes have already spoken. The French thinker Roland Barthes, in *The Fashion System* (1967), treated clothing as a code with its own grammar: some pieces are "words", and the way we combine them makes "sentences".
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "What clothes signal", "items": ["Status and wealth", "Group belonging", "Role or job", "Mood and taste"]}
### Rules about who could wear what
For centuries, laws called **sumptuary laws** decided who could wear which fabrics and colours. In ancient Rome, the richest purple dye was reserved for the elite and, later, for emperors. Medieval and early modern Portugal, like most of Europe, had laws limiting silk, gold thread and lace to certain ranks.
### Uniforms and dress codes
• A uniform erases the individual and shows the role: doctor, soldier, pilot
• Dress codes such as "black tie" tell guests how formal an event is
• In Portugal, university students wear the **traje académico**, the black cape and suit, at ceremonies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Punk, 1970s", "b": "safety pins, rebellion"}, {"a": "Mods, 1960s", "b": "sharp suits, scooters"}, {"a": "Traje à vianesa", "b": "regional pride, festivals"}, {"a": "Business suit", "b": "seriousness, authority"}]}
### Reading the signals
The same item can mean opposite things in different contexts. A leather jacket on a 1950s biker meant danger; on a catwalk it means fashion. Codes shift as groups adopt and abandon them.
!? Researchers have long debated whether what we wear changes how we think, for example whether a lab coat makes people more careful; early results were striking, but later attempts to repeat them were mixed.
> Clothes are a language everyone speaks, even those who claim not to care about fashion.

# Silk Road
Silk comes from the cocoon of the caterpillar *Bombyx mori*, which feeds only on mulberry leaves. China was making silk at least 5,000 years ago. Each cocoon is a single continuous thread that can be close to a kilometre long.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Silkworm eats mulberry", "Spins a cocoon", "Cocoon boiled", "Thread unwound, spun"]}
### Many roads, not one
The name "Silk Road" was coined only in 1877, by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen. In reality it was a web of caravan routes and sea lanes linking China, Central Asia, Persia and the Mediterranean, carrying silk, spices, glass, horses, ideas and religions. Few traders travelled the whole way; goods passed from hand to hand.
### A guarded secret
For a long time silk-making was a Chinese secret. According to the Byzantine historian Procopius, around 552 CE two monks smuggled silkworm eggs to Constantinople hidden in hollow canes, letting the Byzantine Empire start its own industry.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~3000 BCE", "l": "Silk made in China"}, {"y": "~100 BCE", "l": "Routes link to the west"}, {"y": "~552 CE", "l": "Eggs smuggled west"}, {"y": "1877", "l": "Name coined"}]}
### Portugal and silk
In the 16th century, Portuguese ships from Macau carried Chinese silk to Japan and back to Europe, a highly profitable trade. In Freixo de Espada à Cinta, in Trás-os-Montes, families still raise silkworms and weave silk by hand.
!? In ancient Rome silk was so costly and so revealing that the Senate tried to stop men from wearing it in 14 CE.
> Silk was so valuable that a whole network of trade, ideas and empires came to bear its name.

# Industrial revolution
Before the 18th century, spinning and weaving happened at home, by hand. It could take several spinners to supply one weaver. A series of British inventions removed that bottleneck, starting with cotton.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1733", "l": "Flying shuttle"}, {"y": "~1764", "l": "Spinning jenny"}, {"y": "1769", "l": "Water frame"}, {"y": "1785", "l": "Power loom"}]}
### From cottage to factory
Richard Arkwright's water frame was too big for a cottage, so in 1771 he built a water-powered mill at Cromford in Derbyshire, often called the first modern factory. Steam engines later freed factories from rivers, and towns like Manchester boomed.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before", "items": ["hand spinning at home", "slow, costly cloth"]}, "r": {"t": "After", "items": ["machines in mills", "cheap cotton cloth"]}}
### The human cost
• Children worked long shifts among dangerous machines; a British law of 1833 banned children under 9 from textile mills
• Between 1811 and 1816, the **Luddites** smashed machines that threatened their skilled jobs
• Raw cotton came largely from plantations in the American South worked by enslaved people
### Fashion becomes affordable
Cheap printed cotton let ordinary people own more and brighter clothes. The sewing machine (1840s–50s) and standard sizes then made ready-made clothing possible.
!? The Jacquard loom of 1804 used punched cards to control patterns, an idea that later inspired early computing machines.
> Textile machines made cloth cheap and launched the modern factory, along with its harsh new working life.

# Haute couture
Charles Frederick Worth (1825–1895) was English. He moved to Paris in the 1840s and opened his own house in 1858 on the rue de la Paix. He dressed Empress Eugénie and Europe's elite, and changed the rules of fashion.
### What Worth invented
• Sewing a **label** with his name into garments, like an artist signing a painting
• Presenting collections each season, rather than just following a client's orders
• Showing clothes on live models; the first was his wife, Marie Vernet
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before Worth", "items": ["client chooses design", "dressmaker obeys"]}, "r": {"t": "After Worth", "items": ["designer creates", "client chooses from it"]}}
### A protected title
"Haute couture" is a legally protected term in France. Houses must be approved each year by the industry's federation in Paris. They have to make clothes to order for private clients, with fittings, in a Paris workshop with a minimum number of full-time staff, and show new collections twice a year, in January and July.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sketch and toile", "Client measured", "Several fittings", "Hand finishing"]}
### Why it costs so much
A couture evening dress can take hundreds or even thousands of hours of handwork: embroidery, pleating, beading. Few houses make a profit from couture itself; it works as a laboratory and advertisement for perfume, bags and ready-to-wear.
!? Ready-to-wear (prêt-à-porter) only became prestigious in the 1960s; Yves Saint Laurent opened his Rive Gauche boutique in 1966.
> Worth turned the dressmaker into a designer, and haute couture into fashion's laboratory.

# Chanel
Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel (1883–1971) grew up partly in an orphanage at Aubazine, where she learned to sew. She started with hats in Paris in 1910, then opened shops in the seaside resorts of Deauville and Biarritz, where wealthy women wanted clothes they could move in.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1910", "l": "Hat shop in Paris"}, {"y": "1921", "l": "Chanel No. 5"}, {"y": "1926", "l": "Little black dress"}, {"y": "1954", "l": "Comeback, tweed suit"}]}
### Comfort as luxury
• **Jersey**, a stretchy knit used for men's underwear, became elegant day dresses
• Waistlines dropped and corsets went out; she borrowed from menswear and sportswear
• In 1926 *Vogue* compared her simple **little black dress** to Ford's Model T: a design for everyone
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Belle Époque", "items": ["tight corset", "heavy layers", "decoration"]}, "r": {"t": "Chanel", "items": ["loose jersey", "short, simple", "comfort"]}}
### Not the only one
Chanel wasn't alone. Paul Poiret was already designing corset-free dresses around 1906, and the First World War pushed women into jobs needing practical clothing. Chanel's genius was turning the trend into a lifestyle and a brand.
### A darker side
During the German occupation of Paris she lived at the Ritz with a German officer, and archives opened later show links with German intelligence. Her reputation still carries this shadow.
!! Chanel single-handedly freed women from corsets => Poiret, wartime work and changing lifestyles all played a part
> Chanel made simplicity and comfort the new definition of chic.

# Denim
Denim is a sturdy cotton **twill**, woven so diagonal lines appear on the surface. The name probably comes from *serge de Nîmes*, a fabric from the French city; "jeans" likely comes from Genoa (Gênes in French), whose sailors wore tough cotton trousers.
### The patent
Jacob Davis, a tailor in Reno, Nevada, had the idea of putting copper rivets at the stress points of work trousers. He partnered with Levi Strauss, a Bavarian-born fabric merchant in San Francisco, and they received US patent 139,121 on 20 May 1873.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1873", "l": "Riveted patent"}, {"y": "1890", "l": "Lot number 501"}, {"y": "1953", "l": "Brando, The Wild One"}, {"y": "1955", "l": "James Dean's rebel"}]}
### Why jeans fade
The lengthwise threads (warp) are dyed with **indigo**, while the crosswise threads (weft) stay white. Indigo sits on the outside of the fibre, so it rubs away with wear, which is why jeans fade at the knees and why the inside looks pale.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Warp", "b": "blue, indigo-dyed"}, {"a": "Weft", "b": "white, undyed"}, {"a": "Twill weave", "b": "diagonal ribs"}, {"a": "Rivets", "b": "stop pockets tearing"}]}
### From miners to rebels
Miners, cowboys and farmers wore them first. Hollywood rebels of the 1950s turned them into a symbol of youth and defiance. In the Soviet bloc, Western jeans became precious black-market goods.
ex: One pair of jeans, whole life (Levi's 2015 study) | cotton growing, dyeing, washing at home | about 3,800 litres of water
> Jeans began as tough workwear and became the world's most democratic garment.

# Fast fashion
Fast fashion copies catwalk trends and puts them in shops within weeks. Spain's Inditex, founder of Zara in A Coruña in 1975, built the model on fast design and production close to home, including factories in northern Portugal. Online "ultra-fast" brands now add thousands of new items every day.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Spot a trend", "Design in days", "Make nearby, fast", "In shops in weeks"]}
### The numbers
Global clothing production roughly doubled between 2000 and 2014, while the number of times a garment is worn fell by more than a third. According to the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, less than 1% of material used for clothing is recycled into new clothing.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~2×", "l": "production, 2000–2014"}, {"v": "<1%", "l": "recycled into new clothes"}, {"v": "~16 kg", "l": "textile waste/EU person/yr"}]}
### Hidden costs
• Estimates of fashion's share of global greenhouse emissions range widely, from about 2% to 8%
• Synthetic fabrics come from oil and shed microfibres
• In 2013 the Rana Plaza factory collapse in Bangladesh killed more than 1,100 garment workers
### Europe responds
Since January 2025, EU countries must collect textiles separately from other waste, and new EU rules push producers to pay for collecting and recycling the clothes they sell.
!! Donated clothes always get reused locally => Much is exported, and a large share ends up in landfills or burned abroad
> Fast fashion made clothes cheap by making them disposable, and the bill arrives as waste and emissions.

# Portugal's textile industry
Textiles have deep roots in Portugal: wool in Covilhã and the Serra da Estrela, linen in the Minho, and from the 19th century cotton mills along the **Ave valley**, around Guimarães, Vila Nova de Famalicão and Santo Tirso.

When world quotas on textile trade ended in 2005, cheap Asian imports hit hard, and many factories closed. Survivors moved upmarket: faster delivery, small batches, technical fabrics and quality, rather than competing on price.

# Sustainable fashion
Most of a garment's environmental impact comes from making it: growing or producing fibres, dyeing and finishing. So the biggest lever is simply to make each garment last longer.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Wear what you own", "Repair and care", "Swap, borrow, buy used", "Buy fewer, better new"]}
### Longer life, smaller footprint
A UK study by the charity WRAP found that keeping clothes in use for about nine months longer would cut their carbon, water and waste footprints by roughly 20–30%.
ex: Cost per wear | €80 coat worn 100 times = €0.80 per wear | €20 top worn 4 times = €5 per wear
### Practical steps
• **Repair**: a needle, a button or a local seamstress (costureira) can add years
• **Second hand**: apps like Vinted and OLX, plus charity and vintage shops
• **Care**: wash cooler and less often, air-dry instead of tumble-dry
• **Rent or swap** for one-off events like weddings

### Watch out for greenwashing
Vague words like "eco" or "conscious" mean little. New EU rules are banning unproven environmental claims. Look for specific facts: fibre content, certifications or where it was made.
!! Recycling solves fashion's waste => Most fibre blends can't yet be recycled into new clothes; reducing and reusing matter more
> The most sustainable garment is usually the one already hanging in your wardrobe.

# Reading a label
In the EU, clothes must state their fibre composition by law. Care symbols aren't compulsory everywhere, but most garments use the same international set.

### Natural, semi-synthetic, synthetic

# Cooking made us human?
The idea comes mainly from the primatologist Richard Wrangham of Harvard, set out in his book *Catching Fire* (2009). He argues that cooking, not just eating meat, explains why our ancestors' brains grew and their guts shrank.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cook food", "Easier to chew, digest", "More energy per meal", "Fuel a bigger brain"]}
### The logic
• Heat breaks down tough fibres, swells starch and unfolds proteins, so the body extracts more energy
• The human brain is about 2% of body mass but uses about 20% of resting energy
• Compared with other apes, humans have small teeth, weak jaws and a short gut, which fit soft, cooked food
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Chimpanzee", "items": ["chews ~5–6 h a day", "large gut"]}, "r": {"t": "Human", "items": ["chews under 1 h", "small gut, big brain"]}}
### The debate
Wrangham links cooking to *Homo erectus*, about 1.8 million years ago, when brains got bigger and teeth smaller. The problem is evidence: the oldest fairly secure traces of controlled fire, such as in Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, are about 1 million years old, and regular fire use only becomes common around 400,000 years ago. Other researchers stress meat, tools or cooperation instead.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~1.8 Mya", "l": "Homo erectus appears"}, {"y": "~1 Mya", "l": "Early fire traces"}, {"y": "~400,000", "l": "Fire use widespread"}]}
!? Studies of modern people on strict raw-food diets find that many struggle to keep their weight up, suggesting how much energy cooking adds.
> Cooking may have been the first technology that changed our bodies, not just our lives.

# The Columbian exchange
The historian Alfred Crosby coined the term in 1972. After Columbus's voyage of 1492, plants, animals and diseases moved between continents that had been separate for millions of years. It reshaped diets everywhere.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Americas to Old World", "items": ["maize, potatoes", "tomatoes, chillies", "cocoa, turkey"]}, "r": {"t": "Old World to Americas", "items": ["wheat, rice", "sugar cane, coffee", "cattle, horses, pigs"]}}
### New staples
• The **potato** fed Europe's growing population, until the Irish famine of 1845–49 showed the danger of relying on one crop
• **Tomatoes** took a while to be trusted; today Italian cooking is unthinkable without them
• Portuguese traders took **cassava** and peanuts to Africa, where they became staples
### Maize in the Minho
Maize reached Portugal early in the 16th century and transformed farming in the north-west. It produced more food per field than older grains, supporting a denser rural population. It gave Portugal its **broa** (cornbread) and the stone granaries called **espigueiros**, like those at Soajo and Lindoso.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1492", "l": "Columbus crosses"}, {"y": "1500s", "l": "Maize in the Minho"}, {"y": "1845", "l": "Irish potato famine"}, {"y": "1972", "l": "Crosby names it"}]}
### The dark side
Old World diseases such as smallpox killed a huge share of the native population of the Americas; in some regions, estimates reach around 90%. Sugar plantations drove the Atlantic slave trade.
!! Tomatoes and potatoes are traditional European foods => Both came from the Americas and were only widely eaten in Europe from the 1700s
> Every plate today is a product of 1492: almost no cuisine on Earth was left untouched.

# Portuguese spice trade
In 1498 Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in India by sailing around Africa. Portugal soon controlled ports from Goa (1510) to Malacca (1511) and reached Japan in 1543. Pepper, cinnamon, cloves and nutmeg flowed to Lisbon, while Portuguese foods and crops flowed the other way.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1498", "l": "Da Gama in India"}, {"y": "1510", "l": "Goa taken"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "Arrival in Japan"}, {"y": "1557", "l": "Macau settled"}]}
### Food words that travelled
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Vinha d'alhos", "b": "Goan vindaloo"}, {"a": "Pão de Castela", "b": "Japanese castella"}, {"a": "Têmporas (fast days)", "b": "tempura, probably"}, {"a": "Pão", "b": "Japanese pan (bread)"}]}
• **Vindaloo**: the Portuguese meat marinade of wine and garlic became, in Goa, a dish with palm vinegar and plenty of chillies
• **Chillies** came from the Americas, carried by Portuguese ships to India and Asia; Indian food had no chillies before the 1500s
• **Tempura** probably comes from *têmporas*, Catholic fasting days when fried fish and vegetables replaced meat, think of **peixinhos da horta**
• **Castella** sponge cake in Nagasaki descends from Iberian sponge cakes like pão de ló
!! Japanese "arigatō" comes from "obrigado" => It's a native Japanese word, used long before the Portuguese arrived
!? Japan called the Portuguese **Nanban**, "southern barbarians", and the art showing their ships and baggy trousers is called Nanban art.
> Portuguese ships didn't just bring spices home; they reshuffled the flavours of half the world.

# Bacalhau
Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*) lives in cold northern seas: around Norway, Iceland and Newfoundland. Portuguese fishermen were sailing to the Grand Banks off Newfoundland in the 1500s. Cod was salted on board, then dried in the sun and wind, and could keep for months.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Catch cod, north", "Salt on board", "Dry in sun and wind", "Soak 24–48 h"]}
### Why so much cod?
• The Catholic calendar had many meatless days, and dried cod was cheap and kept anywhere
• It travelled well to inland villages far from the sea
• In the 20th century, the Estado Novo's **White Fleet** sent dozens of sailing ships to Newfoundland and Greenland each year
### The White Fleet
Fishermen spent months away, fishing alone from small boats called **dóris**. The hospital and support ship *Gil Eannes*, launched in 1955, now a museum ship in Viana do Castelo, cared for them at sea. Today most of Portugal's cod comes from Norway, for which Portugal is a key market.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Bacalhau à Brás: eggs, potato", "À Gomes de Sá: Porto classic", "Com natas: creamy oven bake", "Pastéis de bacalhau: fritters", "Consoada: boiled, with greens"]}
!? The Portuguese call cod their **fiel amigo**, "faithful friend"; the Maritime Museum in Ílhavo has an aquarium of live cod.
> Portugal fell in love with a fish it never caught at home, because salt let it travel and keep.

# Pastel de nata
Portugal's convents and monasteries produced a rich tradition of egg-yolk sweets, known as **doçaria conventual**. Large quantities of egg whites were used to starch religious habits and to clear wine, the story goes, leaving yolks to be turned into sweets with sugar from Madeira and Brazil.
### From monastery to bakery
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1500s", "l": "Jerónimos built"}, {"y": "1834", "l": "Monasteries closed"}, {"y": "1837", "l": "Belém bakery opens"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Gastronomy wonder"}]}
After the Liberal Wars, Portugal dissolved the religious orders in 1834. The monks of Jerónimos sold their recipe to a sugar refinery nearby, which opened the bakery Pastéis de Belém in 1837. Only the tarts made there may be called **pastéis de Belém**; the rest are pastéis de nata. The original recipe is still kept secret by a few master bakers.
### What makes a good one
• Crisp, laminated pastry, pressed into the tin by thumb
• Custard of yolks, milk, sugar and a little flour, often with lemon peel and cinnamon
• A very hot oven, around 250–300 °C or more, to blister the top in spots
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Blistered, caramelised top", "Creamy egg custard", "Thin, crisp, layered pastry"]}
### Going global
In Macau, the Englishman Andrew Stow opened a bakery in 1989 selling his version, which spread across Asia as the "egg tart". Today you can buy pastéis de nata from Paris to Sydney.
!? Other convent sweets include ovos moles from Aveiro, protected by an EU label since 2008, and toucinho do céu, "bacon from heaven", which traditionally contains no bacon.
> The pastel de nata is a monastery's leftover yolks turned into Portugal's most famous export.

# The Mediterranean diet
UNESCO first listed the Mediterranean diet in 2010 for Spain, Greece, Italy and Morocco, and extended it in 2013 to Cyprus, Croatia and Portugal. Portugal's representative community is **Tavira**, in the Algarve.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Shared meals, activity", "Vegetables, fruit, grains", "Olive oil, legumes, nuts", "Fish; some dairy, wine", "Little red meat, sweets"]}
### More than a diet
The word **diet** comes from the Greek *diaita*, "way of life". UNESCO's listing covers not just ingredients but farming, fishing, festivals and eating together. That's why it's heritage, not a menu.
### Where the science comes from
In the 1950s, the American physiologist Ancel Keys began the **Seven Countries Study**, noticing that heart disease was lower in southern Europe, such as Crete. Later, the Spanish **PREDIMED** trial found fewer heart attacks and strokes among older people at high risk who followed a Mediterranean diet with extra olive oil or nuts.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Traditional pattern", "items": ["seasonal produce", "home cooking", "shared meals"]}, "r": {"t": "Modern drift", "items": ["processed foods", "more meat, sugar", "eating alone"]}}
### Portugal's version
• Olive oil, much of it from the Alentejo, where Alqueva dam irrigation has expanded olive groves
• Soups with vegetables and beans, like caldo verde
• Grilled sardines, bread and seasonal fruit
!! The Mediterranean diet is what tourists eat in southern Europe => It's the traditional rural pattern; many people there now eat far less healthily
> The Mediterranean diet is about how people eat together, not just what's on the plate.

# Umami
In 1908 the chemist Kikunae Ikeda, at Tokyo Imperial University, wondered why his wife's kombu-seaweed broth (dashi) tasted so good. He isolated the cause: **glutamate**, an amino acid. He called the taste *umami*, roughly "delicious savouriness".
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Five basic tastes", "items": ["Sweet, sour", "Salty", "Bitter", "Umami"]}
### Three key molecules
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Glutamate (1908)", "b": "kombu, tomato, cheese"}, {"a": "Inosinate (1913)", "b": "bonito flakes, meat"}, {"a": "Guanylate (1957)", "b": "dried shiitake"}]}
Combining glutamate with the other two creates **synergy**: the umami taste becomes several times stronger than either alone. That's why dashi uses both kombu and bonito, and why cheese on a meat ragù works so well.
### Recognised late
Western science was slow to accept umami as a basic taste. It gained wide acceptance in the 1980s, and receptors for it were found on the tongue in the early 2000s.
### Umami in Portuguese food
Ripe tomatoes (glutamate rises as they ripen), aged cheeses, presunto, sardines, mushrooms and slow-cooked broths all carry umami.
• Ikeda patented a way to make **MSG** (monosodium glutamate); Ajinomoto began selling it in 1909
!! MSG is a dangerous additive => Controlled studies haven't found consistent harm at normal amounts; the "Chinese restaurant syndrome" story began with a 1968 letter
!? Human breast milk is rich in free glutamate, so umami may be one of the first tastes we know.
> Umami is the taste of protein-rich, savoury depth, and the reason a good broth feels satisfying.

# Fermentation
In fermentation, microbes such as yeasts, bacteria and moulds feed on sugars and turn them into acids, alcohol or gas. In the 1850s–60s Louis Pasteur showed that living microbes, not just chemistry, were responsible.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Microbes eat sugars", "Make acid, alcohol, gas", "pH drops", "Spoilers can't grow"]}
### Why it preserves
Acid and alcohol make food hostile to the microbes that cause decay and disease. Before refrigeration, fermentation was one of the main ways to keep food for months: cabbage became sauerkraut or kimchi, milk became cheese, grapes became wine.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Yeast", "b": "bread, wine, beer"}, {"a": "Lactic bacteria", "b": "yoghurt, kimchi, olives"}, {"a": "Moulds", "b": "soy sauce, blue cheese"}, {"a": "Acetic bacteria", "b": "vinegar"}]}
### Portuguese examples
• **Queijo Serra da Estrela**: sheep's milk curdled with wild cardoon thistle instead of animal rennet, then ripened until soft and runny
• **Vinho verde** from the Minho, traditionally light and slightly sparkling
• Table olives, cured in brine
• Bread made with a piece of old dough kept back as starter
### Flavour from microbes
Microbes don't just preserve; they create flavour. They break proteins and fats into hundreds of aromatic compounds, which is why a mature cheese tastes so different from fresh milk.
!? Traces of wine residue in pottery from Georgia suggest people were making wine about 8,000 years ago; Korean kimjang, the communal making of kimchi, was listed by UNESCO in 2013.
> Fermentation is controlled rotting: we let friendly microbes win so harmful ones can't.

# Wine regions
Portugal is small but grape-rich: over 250 native varieties, many grown nowhere else. It has 14 official wine regions, from the Minho to the Azores.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Touriga Nacional", "b": "Douro, Dão: dark, floral"}, {"a": "Alvarinho", "b": "Monção e Melgaço: fresh"}, {"a": "Baga", "b": "Bairrada: tannic red"}, {"a": "Arinto", "b": "Lisbon: crisp white"}]}
### The Douro, 1756
The Marquis of Pombal created a company to regulate port wine and had the Douro vineyards mapped and marked with stone posts. It's one of the oldest demarcated wine regions in the world. The terraced hills of the Alto Douro became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2001.
### How port is made
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Grapes crushed, ferment", "Grape spirit added", "Yeasts stop, sugar stays", "Aged in Gaia lodges"]}
Fermentation is stopped after a couple of days by adding grape spirit (aguardente) at about 77% alcohol. The yeasts die, so natural sugar remains, and the finished wine reaches about 19–22% alcohol. Traditionally, barrels went downriver on flat-bottomed **rabelo** boats to the lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia.
ex: Wine at ~6–9% alcohol, still sweet | Add about 1 part spirit to 4 parts wine | Port at ~19–22%, sweetness kept
### Why England loved port
The Methuen Treaty of 1703 lowered duties on Portuguese wine in England, at a time when French wine was hit by war. Fortified wine also survived the sea journey well. That's why many port houses carry British names.
!? Pico island in the Azores grows vines inside a grid of black volcanic stone walls, called currais, listed by UNESCO in 2004.
> Port is wine with its fermentation stopped halfway, so the grape's sweetness is kept and the alcohol boosted.

# The Maillard reaction
Named after the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard, who described it in 1912, the reaction happens when **amino acids** (from proteins) meet certain **sugars** under heat. It creates hundreds of new flavour and aroma compounds, and brown pigments.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Proteins + sugars", "Heat above ~140 °C", "Hundreds of new molecules", "Brown colour, rich aroma"]}
### Why boiling doesn't brown
Water can't get hotter than 100 °C at normal pressure, so a boiled steak stays grey. Browning needs a dry, very hot surface. That's why chefs pat meat dry, don't overcrowd the pan, and let it sit before turning.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 200.0, "unit": "temperature, °C", "items": [{"l": "Boiling", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Maillard", "v": 140.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Caramel", "v": 170.0}]}
### Not caramelisation
Caramelisation is sugar alone breaking down at higher temperatures. Maillard browning needs protein too. Both often happen together, for example on a crusty loaf.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Toast, bread crust", "b": "starch + gluten"}, {"a": "Coffee roasting", "b": "beans turn brown"}, {"a": "Grilled sardines", "b": "crispy skin"}, {"a": "Seared steak", "b": "brown crust"}]}
ex: Searing a steak | Pat dry, pan very hot | Surface passes 140 °C while inside stays rare | Brown, savoury crust
!? Over-browned starchy foods can form **acrylamide**, a probable carcinogen; EU rules since 2018 require food businesses to keep levels low, hence the advice to toast to gold, not dark brown.
> Flavour is made, not just found: heat turns plain proteins and sugars into hundreds of new aromas.

# Ancient games
People were playing board games before they were writing novels. **Senet**, a race game on a board of 30 squares, appears in Egyptian tomb paintings from around 3000 BCE. Tutankhamun was buried with several senet boxes, because the game came to stand for the soul's journey through the afterlife.
The **Royal Game of Ur** was dug up by the archaeologist Leonard Woolley in the 1920s, in graves at Ur in today's Iraq, dated to around 2500 BCE. Its rules were lost until Irving Finkel of the British Museum reconstructed them from a clay tablet written much later, around 177 BCE.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 3000", "l": "Senet in Egypt (BCE)"}, {"y": "c. 2500", "l": "Royal Game of Ur (BCE)"}, {"y": "c. 500", "l": "Go in China (BCE)"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "AI beats Go champion"}]}
### Two families of games
Most ancient games fall into two groups. Race games, like Senet and Ur, use dice or throwing sticks to move pieces home, so luck matters a lot. Strategy games, like Go, have no dice at all.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Race games", "items": ["Senet, Ur, backgammon", "dice decide moves", "luck plus tactics"]}, "r": {"t": "Strategy games", "items": ["Go, draughts, chess", "no dice", "pure planning"]}}
**Go** (weiqi in Chinese) is played with black and white stones on a 19 × 19 grid. The rules fit on a postcard, yet the number of possible positions dwarfs the number of atoms in the observable universe.
• Nine men's morris boards are scratched into Roman and medieval stones across Europe, Portugal included
• Mancala games, sowing seeds round pits, are played across Africa and Asia
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "30", "l": "squares on a Senet board"}, {"v": "361", "l": "points on a Go board"}]}
!? In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat the Go champion Lee Sedol 4–1, a feat many experts had expected to be at least a decade away.
> Games are among humanity's oldest inventions: rules, luck and rivalry needed no screen.

# Chess
Chess began in India around the 6th century as **chaturanga**, a game modelled on an army. Its four divisions became the pieces: infantry (pawns), cavalry (knights), elephants and chariots.
In Persia it became **shatranj**. The Persian "shāh māt", often translated as "the king is helpless", gave us "checkmate", and Portuguese "xeque-mate". After the Arab conquest of Persia, chess spread across the Islamic world and into Iberia, reaching Christian Europe by around 1000.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["India: chaturanga", "Persia: shatranj", "Arab world", "Iberia, then Europe"]}
### Pieces that changed
The pieces were renamed as the game travelled. The elephant became a bishop; the general beside the king, a slow piece that moved one square diagonally, became the queen.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Elephant", "b": "bishop (bispo)"}, {"a": "Chariot", "b": "rook (torre)"}, {"a": "Counsellor", "b": "queen (dama, rainha)"}, {"a": "Foot soldier", "b": "pawn (peão)"}]}
In the late 1400s, probably in Spain, the queen suddenly gained her modern power to sweep across the board. Games became faster and more aggressive; some called the new style "chess of the mad queen". Rules were then fairly stable for 500 years.
### Machines and minds
In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov in a match. Today a free phone app plays better than any human, yet more people play chess than ever, boosted by online play and the 2020 series *The Queen's Gambit*.

# Video games
The first games ran on lab computers: *Tennis for Two* (1958) on an oscilloscope, and *Spacewar!* (1962) at MIT. **Pong**, released by Atari in 1972, was the breakthrough: a simple bat-and-ball game in bars that showed people would pay to play.
### From arcades to everywhere
Home consoles brought games into living rooms, then PCs, then phones. Each wave widened the audience: today about 3 billion people play, and the average player is an adult, not a teenager.

• *Tetris* (1984) was created by Alexey Pajitnov in Moscow
• *Minecraft* has sold over 300 million copies, the best-selling game ever
• The market crashed in 1983 in the US, flooded by poor games, before Nintendo revived it
### How big is it?
Global game revenue is roughly US$180 billion a year. Cinema box office takes about US$30 billion and recorded music about US$30 billion, so games are bigger than both together, though comparisons depend on what you count.

Most of that money now comes from mobile games, often free to download and paid for with small in-game purchases.

# Are games art?
In 2010 the film critic Roger Ebert wrote that video games "can never be art", arguing that a player's choices undermine an author's vision. Thousands of replies poured in, and the debate became famous.
### The museums answer
In 2012 New York's Museum of Modern Art acquired 14 video games for its design collection, including *Pac-Man*, *Tetris*, *Myst* and *Portal*. The same year the Smithsonian American Art Museum staged *The Art of Video Games*, and London's V&A held a major games exhibition in 2018.

In 2011 the US Supreme Court ruled that games deserve the same free-speech protection as books and films, because they communicate ideas through story, characters and dialogue.
### What games can do that others can't

• *Shadow of the Colossus* makes victory feel like loss
• *Papers, Please* makes you a border guard facing moral dilemmas

# Game design
Game designers deliberately shape how a game feels. One idea they borrow is **flow**, described by psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi: the absorbed state where you lose track of time because the task is just hard enough.
### The flow channel
If a challenge is far above your skill, you feel anxious. If it's far below, you're bored. Good games keep you in the channel between, raising difficulty as you improve.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too hard", "items": ["anxiety, frustration", "players quit"]}, "r": {"t": "Too easy", "items": ["boredom", "players drift away"]}}
### Ingredients of a good game
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Clear goals", "Instant, satisfying feedback", "Challenge that matches skill", "Meaningful choices"]}
Many games are built around a **core loop**, a short cycle of actions you repeat, each round giving a little reward and a reason to go again.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Try an action", "See feedback", "Earn reward", "Get stronger"]}
• The first level of *Super Mario Bros.* (1985) teaches jumping and enemies without a single word of instructions
• Some games adjust difficulty quietly if you keep failing
• Playtesting, watching real people play, reveals what designers can't see themselves
!? Atari's founder Nolan Bushnell summed it up as a rule: a game should be "easy to learn and difficult to master".
> A well-designed game is a machine for producing focus and satisfaction.

# Comics
Comics combine pictures in sequence with words to tell a story. The Swiss teacher Rodolphe Töpffer was publishing picture stories in the 1830s, and many see him as the medium's father.
### Newspaper strips
In the 1890s, rival New York newspapers fought for readers with colour Sunday supplements. Richard Outcault's *The Yellow Kid* (from 1895) made a cartoon character a star and popularised the speech balloon.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1830s", "l": "Töpffer's stories"}, {"y": "1895", "l": "The Yellow Kid"}, {"y": "1929", "l": "Tintin appears"}, {"y": "1938", "l": "Superman debuts"}]}
### The superhero boom
In June 1938 *Action Comics* No. 1 introduced Superman, created by Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster. Batman followed in 1939, and cheap comic books sold in the millions during the Second World War. Top-condition copies of that first issue now sell for millions of dollars.
In Europe, Belgian and French comics took another path, with albums like Hergé's *Tintin* (from 1929) and *Astérix* (from 1959).

# Manga
Japanese is traditionally written in vertical columns read from right to left, so manga pages and panels follow the same order. A manga book opens from what Europeans would call the back.
The word manga roughly means "whimsical pictures". Hokusai used it for his famous sketchbooks from 1814, but modern manga took shape after the Second World War.
### The god of manga
Osamu Tezuka (1928–1989) borrowed ideas from Disney cartoons and from cinema: close-ups, changing angles, motion stretched over many panels. *Astro Boy*, from 1952, is a robot boy with a heart. Tezuka produced around 150,000 pages and helped found Japanese TV animation.

# Graphic novels
A **graphic novel** is a long comic that tells one complete story, published as a book. Will Eisner helped popularise the term with *A Contract with God* (1978), stories of life in a New York tenement.
### Maus
Art Spiegelman's *Maus* tells how his father, Vladek, survived Auschwitz, and how hard it was for father and son to talk about it. Jews are drawn as mice, Germans as cats and Poles as pigs. The animal masks make the horror bearable to look at, while questioning the very idea of dividing people into "races".

# Portuguese comics

# Board game renaissance
In 1995 the German designer Klaus Teuber published *The Settlers of Catan*. Players trade wood, brick, sheep, grain and ore to build roads and towns. Nobody is eliminated, games last about an hour, and everyone stays involved, even on other players' turns.
Catan has sold more than 40 million copies and helped spark the "Eurogame" style: clever systems, little luck, and indirect competition.

Germany's **Spiel des Jahres** (Game of the Year) prize, since 1979, gives a sales boost to winners. The Essen Spiel fair is the world's largest board game show, with well over 100,000 visitors each autumn.
### Playing together
*Pandemic* (2008), by Matt Leacock, made cooperative games popular: all players fight disease outbreaks together and win or lose as a team.

# The Starry Night
In May 1889, after a breakdown in Arles, Vincent van Gogh admitted himself to the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. From his bedroom window he could see the hills before sunrise, and in June 1889 he painted *The Starry Night*.
### Memory and imagination
He couldn't paint outside at night there, so he worked in the day, combining what he'd seen with what he imagined. The village and its church spire are invented, the church looking more Dutch than Provençal. The bright "star" near the horizon is probably Venus, which he mentioned seeing in a letter to his brother Theo.

The dark cypress, a tree linked with cemeteries, rises like a flame. The quiet village sits under a sky in violent motion. Van Gogh wasn't happy with it, calling it a failed attempt at abstraction.

# Las Meninas
*Las Meninas* (1656) hangs in the Prado in Madrid and is nearly 3.2 metres tall. It shows a scene in the royal palace: the five-year-old Infanta Margarita Teresa surrounded by her attendants. **Meninas** is a word of Portuguese origin for young ladies-in-waiting.

# Guernica
On 26 April 1937, a market day, German and Italian aircraft supporting Franco bombed the Basque town of Guernica for several hours. The death toll is still debated; modern historians estimate a few hundred. It was one of the first air raids to devastate a civilian town.
### Painted in weeks
Picasso had been asked by the Spanish Republic to make a mural for its pavilion at the 1937 Paris World's Fair. After reading reports of the bombing, he painted *Guernica* in about five weeks. His partner, the photographer Dora Maar, recorded each stage.

### Reading the picture
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Mother holding a dead child", "Screaming, wounded horse", "Bull watching, unmoved", "Light bulb like an eye"]}
There is no plane, no bomb and no town. Picasso painted pain itself, in black, white and grey like a newspaper photo. The broken, flattened figures make the scene feel shattered.

Picasso refused to let it go to Spain until democracy returned. It arrived in 1981, after Franco's death, and has been in the Reina Sofía museum in Madrid since 1992.

# The Great Wave
*The Great Wave off Kanagawa* is part of Katsushika Hokusai's series *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*, made when he was about 70. A huge wave curls like a claw over three fishing boats, while Mount Fuji sits small and calm in the distance.
### A print, not a painting
Woodblock prints were a team effort and a mass medium, sold cheaply in Edo (now Tokyo). Several thousand impressions were probably pulled from the first blocks, and many copies survive in museums worldwide.

### New blue
Hokusai used **Prussian blue**, a synthetic pigment that had recently become affordable in Japan. It is more intense and stable than older blues, and the series showed it off.

### Wave across the world
After Japan reopened trade in the 1850s, prints flooded into Europe, a craze called **japonisme**.

# The Gulbenkian
Calouste Sarkis Gulbenkian (1869–1955) was an Armenian born in Istanbul. He grew rich by brokering oil deals in the Middle East and became known as "Mr Five Per Cent" for his share in what became the Iraq Petroleum Company.
### A collector's eye
He bought only what he loved, aiming for quality over quantity. In 1928–30 the Soviet government secretly sold art from the Hermitage, and Gulbenkian bought treasures including a Rembrandt portrait and Houdon's marble *Diana*.

He arrived in 1942, during the Second World War, in neutral Portugal, and lived for 13 years in the Hotel Aviz. He left his collection and fortune to a foundation based in Lisbon, created in 1956.

# Visiting a big museum
The Louvre holds around 35,000 works on display. Trying to see them all is impossible, and trying to see too many leaves you remembering nothing. Psychologists even have a name for it: **museum fatigue**, first described in 1916.
ex: 35,000 works × 30 seconds each | = about 290 hours | = 36 eight-hour days, with no breaks
### Slow looking
Studies at big museums find people spend about 15–30 seconds per artwork, often reading the label first. Try the reverse: look for a few minutes before reading anything.
