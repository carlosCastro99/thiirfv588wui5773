== bo-heart

# A double pump
The heart is a muscle about the size of a clenched fist, weighing roughly 250–350 g. It sits in the middle of the chest, tilted slightly to the left. Inside are four chambers: on each side a thin-walled **atrium** that collects blood and a thick-walled **ventricle** that pumps it out.
A wall called the septum keeps the two sides apart, so oxygen-poor blood on the right never mixes with oxygen-rich blood on the left. Both sides squeeze at the same moment and move the same volume, about 70 ml per beat each.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Right side", "items": ["oxygen-poor blood", "pumps to lungs", "thin wall"]}, "r": {"t": "Left side", "items": ["oxygen-rich blood", "pumps to whole body", "thick wall"]}}
### Why the left side is stronger
The right ventricle only has to push blood a few centimetres into the lungs, which are delicate and need low pressure. The left ventricle has to drive blood all the way to your toes and brain, so its wall is about three times thicker.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "peak pressure, mmHg (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Right ventricle", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Left ventricle", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}]}
### The valves
Four one-way valves keep blood moving forwards. The familiar "lub-dub" of a heartbeat is not the muscle itself but the sound of the valves snapping shut: "lub" as the inlet valves close, "dub" as the outlet valves close.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tricuspid", "b": "right atrium → right ventricle"}, {"a": "Pulmonary", "b": "right ventricle → lungs"}, {"a": "Mitral", "b": "left atrium → left ventricle"}, {"a": "Aortic", "b": "left ventricle → body"}]}
!! The heart is on the left side of the chest => It sits in the centre, behind the breastbone, with its tip pointing left
> Two pumps share one heartbeat: a gentle one for the lungs and a powerful one for everything else.

# The circuit
Blood travels in two loops joined at the heart. The **pulmonary circuit** is the short loop through the lungs, where blood drops off carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen. The **systemic circuit** is the long loop that carries oxygen to every organ and brings waste back.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Body", "Right heart", "Lungs", "Left heart"]}
Because blood passes through the heart twice on each lap, this is called double circulation. Fish have a single loop, which is why their blood moves at lower pressure.
### How fast is a lap?
An adult has about 5 litres of blood, and at rest the heart pumps about 5 litres a minute. During hard exercise, output can rise to 20 litres a minute or more, so blood laps the body several times a minute.
ex: Blood volume ≈ 5 litres | Heart output at rest ≈ 5 litres per minute | 5 ÷ 5 ≈ one full lap per minute
### Who worked it out
For about 1,400 years, doctors followed the Greek physician Galen, who thought blood was made in the liver and soaked up by the body. The Arab physician Ibn al-Nafis described the lung circuit in the 13th century, and the English doctor William Harvey proved the whole circulation in 1628 by measuring how much blood the heart pumps: far too much to be made fresh every day.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 170", "l": "Galen: blood from liver"}, {"y": "c. 1242", "l": "Ibn al-Nafis: lung loop"}, {"y": "1628", "l": "Harvey: full circulation"}]}
> Blood doesn't get used up and remade; the same 5 litres go round and round, about once a minute at rest.

# A lifetime of beats
The numbers behind this card are simple multiplication, but they add up to something remarkable. The heart never gets a day off, and it beats whether you're awake, asleep or thinking about it.
ex: 70 beats × 60 minutes = 4,200 per hour | 4,200 × 24 hours ≈ 100,000 per day | × 365 days × 80 years ≈ 2.9 billion
### Why it doesn't get tired
Heart muscle is packed with mitochondria, the cell's power stations, which make up about a third of each cell's volume. It also has its own blood supply, the coronary arteries, and rests briefly between beats. Each beat lasts under a second, and the muscle relaxes for over half of that time.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 7,000 L", "l": "pumped per day"}, {"v": "≈ 2.9 bn", "l": "beats in 80 years"}]}
### Small animals, fast hearts
Heart rate falls as animals get bigger. A mouse's heart races at around 500–600 beats a minute; an elephant's plods at about 30. Across many mammals, a lifetime adds up to very roughly a billion beats. Humans, at around three billion, are an unusual exception.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "resting beats per minute (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Mouse", "v": 550.0}, {"l": "Cat", "v": 150.0}, {"l": "Human", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Elephant", "v": 30.0}]}
!? Your heart rate is not perfectly steady: it speeds up slightly as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out. This normal wobble is a sign of a healthy, responsive heart.
> At 100,000 beats a day, small changes add up: a heart that rests 10 beats slower saves about 14,000 beats daily.

# Arteries, veins, capillaries
Blood vessels form a branching network that reaches within a fraction of a millimetre of almost every cell. Each type of vessel is built for its job.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Arteries", "Arterioles", "Capillaries", "Venules", "Veins"]}
### Built for pressure, built for return
Arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls that stretch with each beat and spring back, smoothing the flow. That stretch is what you feel as your pulse. The aorta, the main artery, is about 2.5 cm wide.
Veins carry blood at low pressure, so their walls are thinner. In your legs, blood has to climb upwards against gravity. Valves stop it slipping back, and leg muscles squeeze the veins as you walk, acting as a second pump. When valves weaken, blood pools and veins bulge, causing varicose veins.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Arteries", "items": ["away from heart", "high pressure", "thick elastic wall"]}, "r": {"t": "Veins", "items": ["back to heart", "low pressure", "valves inside"]}}
### Capillaries: where the work happens
Capillaries are only 5–10 micrometres wide, so red blood cells pass through in single file. Their walls are a single cell thick, letting oxygen, sugar and waste pass across easily. Most of your blood is actually in your veins at any moment, about two thirds of it.
!! Blood in veins is blue => Blood is always red; veins look blue because of the way skin absorbs and scatters light
> Arteries deliver, capillaries trade, veins return.

# What's in blood
An adult has about 5 litres of blood, roughly 7–8% of body weight. Spin a tube of it in a centrifuge and it separates into layers you can see.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Plasma ≈ 55% (yellow)", "Thin layer: white cells, platelets", "Red cells ≈ 45% (red)"]}
### Plasma: the river
Plasma is about 92% water. It carries salts, sugar, fats, hormones, waste such as urea, and most carbon dioxide in dissolved form. Its proteins include albumin, which keeps water inside the vessels, antibodies, and clotting factors.
### The cells
• **Red cells** carry oxygen: about 5 million in every microlitre (a tiny drop)
• **White cells** fight infection: about 4,000–11,000 per microlitre
• **Platelets** are cell fragments that plug leaks and start clots: about 150,000–400,000 per microlitre
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "5 million", "l": "red cells per µL"}, {"v": "≈ 7,000", "l": "white cells per µL"}, {"v": "≈ 250,000", "l": "platelets per µL"}]}
A routine blood test, the full blood count, measures exactly these numbers. Too few red cells suggests anaemia; a high white count can point to infection.
!? In Portugal, a standard whole-blood donation is about 450 ml, roughly a tenth of your blood. The plasma is replaced within a day or two; red cells take several weeks.
> Blood is mostly a watery delivery service, with cells carried along in it.

# Red blood cells
A red blood cell is a flexible disc, about 7–8 micrometres across, dimpled in the middle on both sides. That shape gives a large surface for oxygen to cross and lets the cell fold to squeeze through capillaries narrower than itself.
### Packed with haemoglobin
To make room, red cells throw out their nucleus as they mature. Each one carries about 270 million haemoglobin molecules, and each haemoglobin can hold four oxygen molecules. The iron at its heart binds oxygen and gives blood its colour: bright red when loaded, darker when not.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Made in bone marrow", "Carries oxygen ≈120 days", "Worn cell removed by spleen", "Iron recycled"]}
### Why 2 million a second?
Red cells are the most numerous cells in your body, about 25 trillion, over 80% of all your cells by number. With a lifespan of around 120 days, replacing them takes an astonishing production rate.
ex: 25 trillion cells ÷ 120 days | 120 days ≈ 10 million seconds | ≈ 2.5 million new cells per second
The kidneys control production. When oxygen is low, they release a hormone called EPO, which tells the bone marrow to make more. This is why athletes train at altitude, and why EPO doping is banned.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Haemoglobin", "b": "iron protein that holds oxygen"}, {"a": "EPO", "b": "kidney hormone: make more"}, {"a": "Anaemia", "b": "too few red cells or too little Hb"}]}
!? Carbon monoxide sticks to haemoglobin about 200 times more tightly than oxygen does, which is why faulty gas heaters are so dangerous.
> A red cell is a bag of haemoglobin built to do one job for four months.

# Blood pressure
Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing on artery walls. It's written as two numbers in millimetres of mercury (mmHg), a unit from the old mercury gauges. The top number is the peak as the heart squeezes; the bottom is the lowest point as it refills.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Systolic (top)", "items": ["heart contracting", "about 120 mmHg"]}, "r": {"t": "Diastolic (bottom)", "items": ["heart relaxing", "about 80 mmHg"]}}
### How it's measured
A cuff squeezes the upper arm until blood stops flowing, then slowly lets air out. The pressure at which blood first spurts through is systolic; the point where flow becomes smooth and silent is diastolic.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 90.0, "hi": 180.0, "unit": "systolic, mmHg", "items": [{"l": "Typical", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "High", "v": 140.0}, {"l": "Very high", "v": 180.0}]}
### Why it matters
Readings of about 140/90 or above, repeated on different days, are usually classed as high blood pressure (hypertension). It rarely causes symptoms, but over years it strains the heart and damages arteries in the brain, kidneys and eyes. Stroke has long been one of the leading causes of death in Portugal, and high blood pressure is its biggest risk factor.
• Pressure naturally rises with exercise, stress and pain, and falls during sleep
• Many people read higher at the clinic than at home, the "white-coat" effect
• Salt, alcohol, inactivity, extra weight and age all tend to push it up
!! If I felt fine, my blood pressure would be fine => High blood pressure usually has no symptoms; only measuring tells you
> Two numbers, one silent risk: blood pressure is worth knowing even when you feel well.

# Blood types
In 1901 the Austrian doctor Karl Landsteiner noticed that mixing some people's blood made red cells clump together. He had discovered the ABO blood groups, which made safe transfusions possible and earned him a Nobel Prize in 1930.
### Markers and antibodies
Red cells can carry an A marker, a B marker, both (AB) or neither (O). Your plasma contains antibodies against the markers you lack. Give type A blood to a type B person and their antibodies attack it, which can be deadly.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Type A", "b": "can receive A or O"}, {"a": "Type B", "b": "can receive B or O"}, {"a": "Type AB", "b": "can receive any ABO type"}, {"a": "Type O", "b": "can receive only O"}]}
The Rh system, found in 1940, adds a positive or negative sign. O negative red cells carry none of these key markers, so they're used in emergencies before there's time to test. For plasma the logic flips: AB plasma suits everyone.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of people in Portugal (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "A", "v": 46.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "O", "v": 42.0}, {"l": "B", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "AB", "v": 3.5}]}
### Rh in pregnancy
If an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive baby, her body can make antibodies that harm a later Rh-positive baby. A routine injection of anti-D antibodies in pregnancy now prevents this almost completely.
!? There are over 40 recognised blood group systems, not just ABO and Rh. Most only matter for people who need many transfusions.
> Your blood type is a set of markers on your red cells, and transfusion is about not triggering the recipient's antibodies.

# The heartbeat's spark
The heart starts its own beat. A small patch of special cells in the wall of the right atrium, the **sinoatrial (SA) node**, slowly leaks charged particles until its voltage reaches a trigger point and it fires. Then it resets and starts again, about 60–100 times a minute.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["SA node fires", "Atria contract", "AV node pauses ≈0.1 s", "Ventricles contract"]}
### Timing is everything
The pulse spreads across both atria, squeezing blood down. It then reaches the **atrioventricular (AV) node**, which holds it back for about a tenth of a second so the ventricles can fill. Fast-conducting fibres then carry it to the bottom of the ventricles, which squeeze upwards, like toothpaste from a tube.
Nerves don't start the beat, but they adjust it: one set speeds the SA node up during exercise or fear, while the vagus nerve slows it down at rest. A transplanted heart, with its nerves cut, still beats on its own.
### Reading the ECG
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "P wave", "b": "atria activate"}, {"a": "QRS complex", "b": "ventricles activate"}, {"a": "T wave", "b": "ventricles recover"}]}
The Dutch physiologist Willem Einthoven recorded the first practical ECG in 1903 and won a Nobel Prize for it in 1924. When the SA node or the wiring fails, an implanted pacemaker can take over; the first was fitted in Sweden in 1958.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1903", "l": "Einthoven's ECG"}, {"y": "1958", "l": "First implanted pacemaker"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "Public defibrillators"}]}
> The heart has its own built-in spark; the brain only turns the dial up or down.

# Take your pulse
Your pulse is the stretch of an artery wall as each beat sends a surge of blood through it. The radial artery at the wrist is close to the surface, which makes it easy to feel.
### Doing it well
• Use your index and middle fingers, not your thumb, which has its own pulse
• Sit quietly for a few minutes first
• Count for 30 seconds and double it for a more accurate figure
• If the beat feels irregular, count for a full minute
ex: 18 beats in 15 seconds | 18 × 4 = 72 | Resting heart rate ≈ 72 beats per minute
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 40.0, "hi": 110.0, "unit": "beats/min", "items": [{"l": "Fit athlete", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Typical", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Upper normal", "v": 100.0}]}
### What changes it
Fitness lowers the resting rate because a trained heart pumps more blood per beat. Some endurance athletes rest in the 40s. Caffeine, stress, dehydration, fever and standing up all push it higher. Children's hearts naturally beat faster than adults'.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical resting beats per minute", "items": [{"l": "Newborn", "v": 140.0}, {"l": "Child (8 y)", "v": 85.0}, {"l": "Adult", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Athlete", "v": 50.0}]}
!? An irregular, chaotic pulse can be a sign of atrial fibrillation, the most common heart rhythm problem in older adults. Many smartwatches now flag it, but a doctor needs to confirm it with an ECG.
> Fifteen seconds and two fingers give you one of the most useful numbers about your body.

== bo-brain

# Neurons
Neurons are cells specialised to carry messages. A typical neuron has a cell body containing the nucleus, many short branching **dendrites** that collect incoming signals, and one long **axon** that carries the output. Axons can be tiny, or as long as a metre, running from your spine to your big toe.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Dendrites collect", "Cell body adds up", "Axon carries pulse", "Terminals pass it on"]}
### How many, and where?
The figure of 86 billion comes from a 2009 study by Brazilian neuroscientist Suzana Herculano-Houzel, who dissolved brains into a "soup" and counted nuclei. Surprisingly, about 69 billion of them are in the cerebellum, the small region at the back.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "86 billion", "l": "neurons"}, {"v": "≈ 100 trillion", "l": "synapses (estimate)"}]}
Each neuron connects to thousands of others, sometimes up to around 10,000. The number of connections is far larger than the number of cells, and it's the pattern of connections that stores what you know.
### Not just neurons
The brain also has glial cells, which insulate axons, feed neurons, clean up and help control signals. They were long said to outnumber neurons ten to one; the true ratio is closer to one to one.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dendrite", "b": "input branches"}, {"a": "Axon", "b": "output cable"}, {"a": "Myelin", "b": "fatty insulation on axons"}, {"a": "Glia", "b": "support and repair cells"}]}
!? The Spanish scientist Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed in the 1890s that the brain is made of separate cells, drawing them in exquisite detail. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize for it.
> You are not your neurons but their connections: 86 billion cells, linked in trillions of ways.

# Electric and chemical
A resting neuron holds a small voltage across its membrane, about −70 millivolts, by pumping charged particles (ions) in and out. When it's stimulated enough, tiny gates open, sodium rushes in and the voltage flips for about a millisecond. This **action potential** races along the axon like a wave.
### All or nothing
A neuron either fires fully or not at all. It signals "stronger" by firing more often, not by firing bigger pulses.
### Speed depends on insulation
Many axons are wrapped in **myelin**, a fatty sheath with small gaps. The signal jumps from gap to gap, reaching up to about 120 m/s. Bare axons crawl along at around 1 m/s. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system damages myelin and signals slow or fail.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "signal speed, m/s (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Myelinated", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Thin myelinated", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "Bare axon", "v": 1.0}]}
ex: Nerve from spine to toe ≈ 1 m | Speed ≈ 100 m/s | Time ≈ 1 ÷ 100 = 10 milliseconds
### Crossing the gap
Neurons don't quite touch. At the synapse, a gap of about 20 nanometres, the pulse makes the axon release neurotransmitters, which drift across and fit into receptors on the next cell.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pulse reaches terminal", "Chemicals released", "Cross the gap", "Bind to receptors"]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Glutamate", "b": "main 'go' signal"}, {"a": "GABA", "b": "main 'calm down' signal"}, {"a": "Dopamine", "b": "reward and movement"}, {"a": "Serotonin", "b": "mood, sleep, appetite"}]}
Most drugs that affect the mind, from caffeine to antidepressants and anaesthetics, work at synapses.
!? In 1921 Otto Loewi showed that nerves talk chemically by moving fluid from one frog heart to another and slowing it down. He said the experiment came to him in a dream.
> Neurons speak electricity inside and chemistry in between.

# Brain regions
An adult brain weighs about 1.3–1.4 kg and has the texture of firm tofu. It has three main parts, stacked roughly from top to bottom, from the newest in evolution to the oldest.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Cerebrum: think, sense, move", "Cerebellum: balance, timing", "Brainstem: breathe, heartbeat"]}
### The cerebrum
The cerebrum makes up about 85% of the brain's weight. Its wrinkled outer layer, the cortex, is where perception, language, planning and voluntary movement happen.
### The cerebellum
The "little brain" at the back fine-tunes movement, balance and timing, and helps you learn physical skills. It's one of the first areas alcohol affects, which is why police sobriety tests check walking in a straight line.
### The brainstem
The brainstem connects the brain to the spinal cord. It runs breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing and the sleep–wake switch. You never have to think about these, and damage here is often life-threatening.
### Deep inside
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thalamus", "b": "relays senses to the cortex"}, {"a": "Hypothalamus", "b": "temperature, hunger, hormones"}, {"a": "Hippocampus", "b": "forms new memories"}, {"a": "Amygdala", "b": "fear and emotion"}]}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of brain (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Cerebellum mass", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Cerebellum neurons", "v": 80.0, "hi": true}]}
!? The cerebellum is only about a tenth of the brain's mass but holds around four in five of its neurons, tightly packed tiny cells.
> Thinking happens on top, coordination at the back, and survival at the base.

# Four lobes
The cortex is a sheet only 2–4 mm thick, folded so that a large area fits inside the skull. Spread out, it would be roughly the size of a pillowcase. Deep grooves divide each hemisphere into four lobes.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Frontal", "b": "plans, decides, moves, speaks"}, {"a": "Parietal", "b": "touch, body map, space"}, {"a": "Temporal", "b": "hearing, language, memory"}, {"a": "Occipital", "b": "vision"}]}
### Clues from injury
Much of what we know came from people with damage to one area. In 1848 an iron rod shot through the frontal lobe of the American railway worker Phineas Gage. He survived, but friends said his personality changed. In 1861 the French doctor Paul Broca linked a patient's loss of speech to damage in the left frontal lobe, now called Broca's area.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1848", "l": "Phineas Gage's injury"}, {"y": "1861", "l": "Broca: speech area"}, {"y": "1927", "l": "Egas Moniz: angiography"}, {"y": "1990s", "l": "fMRI scans"}]}
### A Portuguese chapter
The Portuguese neurologist Egas Moniz developed cerebral angiography in 1927, a way of seeing brain blood vessels on X-rays that is still used. He also won the 1949 Nobel Prize for the prefrontal leucotomy, a brain operation for mental illness. That procedure is now regarded as harmful and has been abandoned.
### Lobes work together
Reading this sentence uses the occipital lobe to see, the temporal lobe to understand words and the frontal lobe to stay focused. Maps of lobes are a guide, not a set of separate boxes.
!! Each lobe works alone => Almost every task uses networks across several lobes at once
> The lobes are neighbourhoods of a city, not separate towns.

# Left and right
The brain's wiring crosses over. Most nerve fibres from the movement areas swap sides in the lower brainstem, so the left hemisphere moves the right hand. A stroke on the left side of the brain often weakens the right side of the body and can affect speech.
### The bridge between halves
The two hemispheres are linked by the **corpus callosum**, a thick band of about 200 million nerve fibres. In the 1960s, some people with severe epilepsy had it cut. Roger Sperry's studies of these "split-brain" patients, which won a Nobel Prize in 1981, showed each half could handle different information.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Left hemisphere", "items": ["right side of body", "language (usually)"]}, "r": {"t": "Right hemisphere", "items": ["left side of body", "space, faces, tone"]}}
### Specialised, not separate
There are real differences. Language is based mainly in the left hemisphere in about 95% of right-handers and most left-handers. The right side is more involved in spatial attention, recognising faces and the emotional tone of speech. But in healthy people both halves share information constantly.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Left-brained logical people", "r": "Both halves work on most tasks"}, {"w": "Right-brained creative people", "r": "Creativity uses networks on both sides"}]}
A 2013 study from the University of Utah scanned over 1,000 brains and found no evidence that people are more "left-brained" or "right-brained" overall.
!? Each eye sends information to both hemispheres. What crosses over is each half of your field of view: things to your left are processed by your right hemisphere.
> Your brain has two specialised halves, but you think with one connected brain.

# The 10% myth
The claim that we use only 10% of our brain has been around for over a century. Its origins are murky: it may come from misquoted psychologists saying people rarely reach their full potential, or from early scientists not knowing what some regions did. Films like *Lucy* (2014) keep it alive.
### Why it can't be true
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Scans show activity everywhere", "Small injuries cause real problems", "Brain uses 20% of our energy", "Unused neurons get pruned away"]}
Evolution is ruthless about waste. An organ that costs about a fifth of our energy would not have grown so large if 90% of it sat idle. And if most of the brain were spare, strokes in random places would mostly go unnoticed. They don't.
### The grain of truth
At any single moment, only a minority of neurons are firing. That's by design: information is coded by which cells fire, not by all of them firing. If every neuron fired at once, the result would be a seizure, not genius.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "At one instant", "items": ["some neurons firing", "sparse code"]}, "r": {"t": "Across a day", "items": ["virtually all used", "every region active"]}}
!! We only use 10% of our brain => We use virtually all of it, just not all at the same moment
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Unlock the unused 90%", "r": "Get better by training networks"}]}
> There is no hidden 90%; improvement comes from strengthening connections, not switching on spare parts.

# Energy hungry
The brain runs on about 20 watts, less than a typical light bulb, yet that's a fifth of everything your body burns at rest. Weight for weight it uses about ten times more energy than the body's average tissue.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of body total", "items": [{"l": "Brain weight", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Brain energy use", "v": 20.0, "hi": true}]}
ex: 20 watts × 86,400 seconds in a day | ≈ 1.7 million joules | ≈ 400 kcal per day
### Where the energy goes
Most of it isn't spent on "thinking hard". It goes to keeping neurons primed: pumps in every membrane constantly push sodium out and potassium in, restoring the voltage after each signal. Concentrating on a difficult task raises the brain's total use by only a few per cent.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Main fuel", "b": "glucose, about 120 g a day"}, {"a": "Backup fuel", "b": "ketones during long fasts"}, {"a": "Biggest cost", "b": "pumping ions after signals"}]}
### Hungry from birth
In young children the brain's share is far higher. Around the age of five, it uses roughly half of all the energy the body burns at rest, which may be one reason children grow slowly in those years.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "20 W", "l": "brain power at rest"}, {"v": "≈ 20%", "l": "of resting energy"}]}
!? The brain can't store much fuel itself. If its blood supply stops, consciousness is lost within about 10 seconds.
> A small organ with a big appetite: most of the brain's energy goes on being ready.

# Reflexes
A reflex is an automatic response that doesn't wait for a decision. The simplest route, the **reflex arc**, runs from a sensor to the spinal cord and straight back out to a muscle.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Skin senses heat", "Signal to spinal cord", "Relay to motor neuron", "Muscle pulls hand back"]}
At the same time, a signal travels up to the brain. By the time it arrives and you feel pain, your hand has already moved.
### Fast and slower routes
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "milliseconds (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Spinal reflex", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Conscious reaction", "v": 250.0}]}
Some pain fibres are fast and myelinated; others are thin and slow. That's why stubbing a toe gives a sharp first pain, then a dull throbbing a moment later.
### Reflexes you use every day
• **Blinking** when something flies at your eye
• **Pupils** shrinking in bright light
• **Coughing** and sneezing to clear airways
• **The knee jerk** doctors test with a small hammer, which checks the nerves and spinal cord
Newborns show special reflexes, such as gripping a finger tightly, which fade over the first months as the brain takes control. Doctors check them to judge how the nervous system is developing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Reflex", "items": ["automatic", "via spinal cord", "tens of ms"]}, "r": {"t": "Voluntary move", "items": ["decided", "via the brain", "hundreds of ms"]}}
!? You can partly override some reflexes. If you pick up a hot but precious plate, your brain can stop you dropping it, at the cost of a burnt hand.
> Reflexes are the body's shortcuts: act first, inform the brain afterwards.

# Neuroplasticity
Neuroplasticity means the brain physically changes with experience. Each time two connected neurons fire together, the link between them can grow stronger: more receptors, bigger synapses, even new branches. Links that go unused weaken and may be removed.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Practise", "Neurons fire together", "Connection strengthens", "Skill gets easier"]}
### How we know
In 1949 Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that connections strengthen with use, often summed up as "neurons that fire together wire together". In 1973 researchers found a lasting strengthening of synapses in the hippocampus, called long-term potentiation. Brain scans later showed visible changes in people learning skills.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1949", "l": "Hebb's learning rule"}, {"y": "1973", "l": "Synapses strengthen (LTP)"}, {"y": "2000", "l": "London taxi drivers"}]}
A famous 2000 study found that London taxi drivers, who memorise thousands of streets, had a larger rear part of the hippocampus, a region used for navigation.
### Plasticity over a lifetime
Children's brains are the most plastic, building and then pruning huge numbers of connections. Adults change more slowly but still learn languages, instruments and skills. After a stroke, rehabilitation helps other areas take over lost functions.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "The brain is fixed after childhood", "r": "It rewires throughout life"}]}
!? Whether adults grow many new neurons is still debated. Most plasticity comes from changing connections, not adding cells.
> Every time you practise, you are rebuilding your brain a little.

# Learning to juggle
In a study published in 2004, German researchers led by Bogdan Draganski scanned young adults before and after they learnt a three-ball juggling routine. A comparison group didn't juggle.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Month 0", "l": "Brain scan, start juggling"}, {"y": "Month 3", "l": "Can juggle: grey matter up"}, {"y": "Month 6", "l": "Stopped: growth fades"}]}
### What grew
After three months, the jugglers showed more grey matter in areas that track motion and link vision to hand movement. After three months without practice, the gain had partly shrunk back. A later study found similar, smaller changes in people in their 60s.
MRI can't show what exactly increased. It could be more synapses, more support cells or more tiny blood vessels, rather than new neurons.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Practising", "items": ["areas grow", "skill improves"]}, "r": {"t": "Stopped", "items": ["growth fades", "skill fades slowly"]}}
### What it means for you
• The adult brain changes visibly in weeks, not just in childhood
• Changes are specific: juggling grew the areas juggling uses
• Keeping a skill means keeping it in use
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["New challenge", "Regular practice", "Brain adapts", "Skill sticks if used"]}
!! Brain training games make you smarter overall => Practice mainly improves the specific skill you practise
> The brain builds what you use, and lets go of what you don't.

== bo-digest

# The journey
Your digestive tract is one long tube with a mouth at one end and an exit at the other. Food inside it is, strictly speaking, still outside your body until it's absorbed through the walls. The often-quoted 9 metres is a rough total; in a living person, muscle tone keeps the gut shorter than when measured after death.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mouth", "Oesophagus", "Stomach", "Small intestine", "Large intestine"]}
### Moved by waves
Food doesn't fall down the tube; it's pushed. Rings of muscle squeeze behind each mouthful in waves called **peristalsis**, like squeezing toothpaste along a tube. That's why astronauts can eat in orbit and you can swallow while lying down.
### Where the time goes
Food spends only seconds in the mouth and oesophagus. Most of the waiting happens further down, especially in the large intestine, where water is slowly reclaimed.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "hours spent (rough averages)", "items": [{"l": "Stomach", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Small intestine", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Large intestine", "v": 30.0, "hi": true}]}
• Times vary a lot between people and meals
• Fatty meals leave the stomach more slowly than sugary drinks
• Fibre, fluid and activity tend to speed things up
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 9 m", "l": "often-quoted gut length"}, {"v": "1–3 days", "l": "mouth to exit"}]}
!! Most digestion happens in the stomach => The stomach starts it; most digestion and absorption happen in the small intestine
> Digestion is a production line: each section does one stage, then passes the food along.

# Mouth and teeth
Digestion starts before you swallow. Teeth break food into small pieces, which gives enzymes far more surface to work on. Adults have up to 32 permanent teeth, replacing the 20 baby teeth of childhood.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Incisors", "b": "front: cut and bite"}, {"a": "Canines", "b": "pointed: grip and tear"}, {"a": "Premolars", "b": "crush"}, {"a": "Molars", "b": "back: grind"}]}
### Enamel
The outer coat of a tooth, enamel, is the hardest substance in the body, about 96% mineral. But it has no living cells, so it can't repair itself. Acid from bacteria feeding on sugar slowly dissolves it, which is how cavities form.
### Saliva does more than wet food
You make roughly 0.5–1.5 litres of saliva a day. It contains:
• **Amylase**, an enzyme that starts breaking starch into sugar
• **Mucus**, which binds food into a slippery ball for swallowing
• **Antibacterial proteins** that help protect teeth and gums
ex: Chew a piece of plain bread for about a minute | Amylase splits starch into sugars | It starts to taste slightly sweet
### Swallowing safely
When you swallow, a flap called the epiglottis folds over the windpipe so food goes down the oesophagus instead. Talking while eating interferes with this, which is why food sometimes "goes down the wrong way".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Teeth break food", "Saliva softens it", "Tongue forms a ball", "Swallow, airway closed"]}
!? The smell, sight and even thought of food make saliva flow before you take a bite, getting digestion ready in advance.
> Good chewing is the cheapest part of digestion: it multiplies the surface enzymes can work on.

# The stomach
The stomach is a stretchy, J-shaped muscular bag. Empty, it holds only a small cupful; after a big meal it can stretch to hold a litre or two. Its walls have three layers of muscle running in different directions, so it can knead food like dough.
### A pool of acid
Cells in the lining pour out hydrochloric acid. This kills most germs swallowed with food and unfolds proteins so the enzyme **pepsin** can cut them up. After a few hours, food becomes a thick soup called chyme.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 14.0, "unit": "pH", "items": [{"l": "Stomach", "v": 2.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Black coffee", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Small intestine", "v": 7.5}]}
### Why it doesn't digest itself
A thick layer of alkaline mucus coats the lining, and the cells beneath are replaced every few days. Pepsin is released in an inactive form and only switches on in the acid.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Food arrives", "Churned with acid", "Becomes chyme", "Squirted into intestine"]}
The exit valve, the pylorus, lets chyme out a little at a time so the intestine isn't overwhelmed.
### Ulcers: a surprising cause
For decades, stomach ulcers were blamed on stress and spicy food. In the early 1980s, Australians Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed most are caused by the bacterium *Helicobacter pylori*. Marshall even drank a culture of it to prove the point. They won the Nobel Prize in 2005, and ulcers are now usually cured with antibiotics.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Stress causes most ulcers", "r": "H. pylori and some painkillers do"}]}
> The stomach is a mixer and a steriliser more than a place where food is absorbed.

# Small intestine
The small intestine is "small" only in width, about 2.5 cm. It's the longest part of the gut, a few metres in a living adult, coiled into the middle of the abdomen. It has three sections: the duodenum, the jejunum and the ileum.
### Finishing the job
In the duodenum, chyme meets two key fluids. The pancreas sends enzymes that cut carbohydrates, proteins and fats, plus bicarbonate to neutralise stomach acid. The liver sends bile, which breaks fat into tiny droplets.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Carbohydrates", "b": "→ simple sugars"}, {"a": "Proteins", "b": "→ amino acids"}, {"a": "Fats", "b": "→ fatty acids, glycerol"}]}
### Folds on folds
To absorb all those small molecules, the lining is folded three times over.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Large circular folds", "Villi: ≈1 mm fingers", "Microvilli on each cell"]}
Sugars and amino acids pass into tiny blood vessels inside each villus and go straight to the liver. Fats take a different route, into lymph vessels, before joining the blood.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 30 m²", "l": "absorbing surface"}, {"v": "90%", "l": "of nutrients absorbed here"}]}
!! The gut's surface is the size of a tennis court => A 2014 measurement gave about 30 m², closer to half a badminton court
!? In coeliac disease, gluten triggers the immune system to flatten the villi. The surface shrinks and nutrients are poorly absorbed until gluten is removed from the diet.
> The small intestine is where food finally becomes you.

# Large intestine
The large intestine is about 1.5 m long but much wider than the small intestine. It starts at the caecum, where the small appendix hangs, loops around the abdomen as the colon and ends at the rectum.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Caecum", "Colon: water out", "Rectum: storage", "Exit"]}
### Reclaiming water
Each day around 8–9 litres of fluid pass into the gut: what you drink plus saliva, stomach juice, bile and other secretions. The small intestine reclaims most of it, the colon takes back most of the rest, and only about 0.1 litre leaves in stools. When the colon is rushed, as in many infections, you get diarrhoea.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "litres of fluid per day (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Into the gut", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Into the colon", "v": 1.5}, {"l": "Out in stools", "v": 0.1}]}
### A fermentation tank
Most of your gut bacteria live here. They feed on fibre you can't digest and produce short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the colon's own cells. They also make vitamin K and some B vitamins.
• Gas is a normal by-product, about half a litre to a litre and a half a day
• A large share of stool's dry weight is bacteria, alive and dead
• The appendix may act as a reserve of good bacteria after illness
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fibre", "b": "food for bacteria"}, {"a": "Fatty acids", "b": "fuel for colon cells"}, {"a": "Vitamin K", "b": "helps blood clot"}]}
> The large intestine is a water-recycling plant with a bacterial farm attached.

# The microbiome
Your gut microbiome is the community of bacteria, fungi and viruses living in your intestines, most of them in the colon. It includes hundreds of species, and the mix is as individual as a fingerprint.
### Counting the guests
For decades textbooks said bacteria outnumbered our own cells ten to one. A careful 2016 recount by Israeli and Canadian researchers found about 38 trillion bacteria against about 30 trillion human cells: roughly one to one, weighing around 0.2 kg in total.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "10 bacteria for each human cell", "r": "Roughly 1 to 1"}]}
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 38 trillion", "l": "gut bacteria"}, {"v": "≈ 0.2 kg", "l": "total weight"}]}
### What they do for us
• Break down fibre into compounds that feed the gut lining
• Make some vitamins, such as K and certain B vitamins
• Train the immune system and crowd out harmful germs
• Possibly affect mood and appetite, an active but still early area of research
### Shaping your microbiome
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Varied plants: more species", "Fibre: food for microbes", "Fermented foods: yoghurt", "Antibiotics only when needed"]}
Antibiotics can wipe out many species at once, letting harmful bacteria such as *C. difficile* take over. In severe cases, doctors now restore balance with a transplant of stool from a healthy donor.
!? In 2013 UNESCO extended its listing of the Mediterranean diet, rich in vegetables, legumes and olive oil, to include Portugal. That style of eating gives gut bacteria plenty of fibre to work with.
> You feed two lots of life at every meal: yourself and your microbes.

# Three macronutrients
Macronutrients are the nutrients we need in large amounts. All three can be burnt for energy, but each also has its own building job.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "kcal per gram", "items": [{"l": "Carbohydrate", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Protein", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Alcohol", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Fat", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why fat packs more energy
Fat molecules are long chains of carbon and hydrogen with little oxygen, so there's more to "burn". Carbohydrates are already partly oxidised and come bound to water. That makes fat the body's ideal long-term store: a kilogram of body fat holds about 7,000 kcal.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Carbohydrates", "b": "quick fuel, glucose"}, {"a": "Fats", "b": "energy store, cell walls"}, {"a": "Proteins", "b": "muscle, enzymes, repair"}]}
### Reading a label
EU food labels must show energy in both kilojoules and kilocalories. One kcal equals about 4.2 kJ.
ex: 10 g fat × 9 = 90 kcal | 20 g carbohydrate × 4 = 80 kcal | Total ≈ 170 kcal ≈ 710 kJ
### Small but essential
Proteins are built from 20 amino acids; nine of them must come from food because the body can't make them. Some fats are also essential, such as omega-3s found in oily fish like sardines. Fibre is a carbohydrate we mostly can't digest, but gut bacteria can.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Macronutrients", "items": ["grams per day", "carbs, fat, protein"]}, "r": {"t": "Micronutrients", "items": ["milligrams or less", "vitamins, minerals"]}}
!? The 4-9-4 numbers come from the American chemist Wilbur Atwater, who measured food energy in the 1890s. Labels still use his factors today.
> Gram for gram, fat carries more than twice the energy of carbohydrate or protein.

# The liver
The liver is the largest internal organ, about 1.5 kg, tucked under the right ribs. It receives around 1.5 litres of blood a minute, and most of it comes straight from the gut, so everything you absorb passes through the liver first.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "The liver", "items": ["Stores glycogen, vitamins", "Makes bile, proteins", "Clears toxins, alcohol"]}
### Many jobs at once
• Stores sugar as **glycogen** and releases it between meals
• Makes **bile**, about half a litre to a litre a day, stored in the gall bladder
• Makes blood proteins, including albumin and clotting factors
• Stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12
• Breaks down alcohol, medicines and old hormones
### Alcohol: one speed only
The liver clears alcohol at a fairly fixed rate, very roughly one standard drink an hour. Coffee, cold showers and fresh air don't speed it up.
ex: Glass of wine 150 ml at 13% | ≈ 15 g of pure alcohol | ≈ 1.5–2 hours to clear
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Alcohol absorbed", "Liver breaks it down", "Toxic acetaldehyde", "Harmless acetate"]}
### It can regrow
After part of the liver is removed, the rest grows back to nearly full size within months. This is why a living person can donate part of their liver to someone else.
!? Bile is what makes stools brown: old red blood cells are broken down into pigments that the liver adds to bile.
> The liver is the body's checkpoint: everything absorbed from the gut is sorted there first.

# Feeling full
Fullness is a conversation between gut and brain. Several signals arrive at different speeds, and the brain adds them up.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Stomach stretches", "Gut releases hormones", "Signals reach brain", "You feel satisfied"]}
### The messengers
Stretch sensors in the stomach wall send signals up the vagus nerve almost at once. As food reaches the intestine, it releases hormones such as CCK, GLP-1 and PYY. Meanwhile ghrelin, the hunger hormone, falls.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Stretch", "b": "fast: stomach is filling"}, {"a": "CCK, GLP-1, PYY", "b": "slower: food has arrived"}, {"a": "Ghrelin", "b": "falls after eating"}]}
### Why speed matters
Gut hormones take time to build up, so the brain lags behind the plate. Eat quickly and you can finish well past the point where you'd have felt satisfied.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Eating fast", "items": ["signals lag behind", "easy to overeat"]}, "r": {"t": "Eating slowly", "items": ["signals catch up", "stop when satisfied"]}}
• Protein and fibre tend to keep you full longer
• Bulky foods with lots of water, like soup and vegetables, stretch the stomach for fewer calories
• Distraction, such as eating in front of a screen, makes it harder to notice fullness
!? Recent weight-loss medicines such as semaglutide mimic the gut hormone GLP-1, slowing the stomach and boosting the brain's sense of fullness.
!? "Room for dessert" is real: the brain tires of one taste faster than of food in general, so a new flavour can revive appetite.
> Fullness arrives on a delay, so slowing down lets your brain catch up with your stomach.

# Water
Water makes up roughly 50–60% of an adult's body weight. It carries nutrients, removes waste, cushions joints and cools you through sweat. You lose some all day, so you need to replace it all day.

### Where the figure comes from
The European Food Safety Authority suggests total water of about 2.0 litres a day for women and 2.5 litres for men, from all sources. Around a fifth comes from food, which leaves the drinks figure on this card.

### Good sources
• Water, milk, tea and coffee all count
• Fruit and vegetables such as watermelon, cucumber and oranges are over 85% water
• Soups count too

### Listening to your body
Thirst usually kicks in after losing only 1–2% of body water, and for most healthy people it's a reliable guide. Pale straw-coloured urine is a good sign. Older adults feel thirst less strongly, so they may need to drink by habit, especially during Portuguese summer heatwaves.
!? Drinking huge amounts very fast can be dangerous: it dilutes the salt in your blood, a condition called hyponatraemia, occasionally seen in marathon runners.
> Your body is mostly water, and thirst, food and ordinary drinks keep it topped up.

== bo-immune

# Lines of defence
Your body is constantly exposed to bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites. Most never cause trouble because they have to get past three layers of defence, each slower but more targeted than the one before.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Barriers: keep germs out", "Innate: attack anything foreign", "Adaptive: target one enemy"]}
### Innate: fast and general
The innate immune system is ready from birth. It recognises general features shared by many germs, such as parts of bacterial walls, and responds within minutes to hours. It includes cells that swallow invaders, inflammation and fever.
### Adaptive: slow, precise, with memory
The adaptive system learns. B cells and T cells each recognise one specific target. The first time you meet a new germ, finding and multiplying the right cells takes about one to two weeks. That's why a first infection can make you quite ill.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Innate", "items": ["minutes to hours", "same for every germ", "no memory"]}, "r": {"t": "Adaptive", "items": ["days to weeks", "tailored to one germ", "remembers"]}}
### Working as a team
The two systems talk to each other. Innate cells capture pieces of a germ and show them to T cells, switching on the adaptive response. Antibodies made by the adaptive system then help innate cells find their targets.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Germ gets past barrier", "Innate cells attack", "Show pieces to T cells", "Adaptive army builds"]}
!? Some animals rely almost entirely on innate immunity. Insects, for example, have no antibodies at all, yet still fight off many infections.
> Defence in depth: keep germs out, fight them fast, then learn them for next time.

# Barriers
The best infection is one that never starts. Most of your defences are not cells at all but physical and chemical barriers.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Skin", "b": "tough, dry, slightly acidic"}, {"a": "Mucus", "b": "sticky trap in airways and gut"}, {"a": "Tears, saliva", "b": "contain germ-killing enzymes"}, {"a": "Stomach acid", "b": "kills swallowed germs"}]}
### Skin: a wall of dead cells
The outer layer of skin is made of flattened dead cells packed with tough keratin, constantly shed along with any germs on them. Its surface is mildly acidic, around pH 5, and coated with oils that discourage many microbes. Cuts and burns are dangerous partly because they breach this wall.
### Moving parts
Barriers aren't just passive. Cilia in the airways sweep mucus upwards; coughing and sneezing blast it out; urine flushes the bladder; tears wash the eyes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Germ is trapped", "Mucus swept up", "Coughed or swallowed", "Destroyed by acid"]}
### Friendly bacteria
Harmless bacteria on the skin and in the gut take up space and food, leaving little room for harmful ones. This is called colonisation resistance.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Washing hands: removes germs", "Covering coughs: blocks spread", "Clean wounds: protects skin"]}
!? Alexander Fleming discovered lysozyme, a germ-dissolving enzyme in tears and mucus, in 1922, partly by dripping his own nasal mucus onto bacteria. Six years later he discovered penicillin.
> Most germs are stopped before your immune cells ever see them.

# Inflammation
Inflammation is the body's emergency response to injury or infection. Its four classic signs were described by the Roman writer Celsus about 2,000 years ago, in Latin.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rubor", "b": "redness"}, {"a": "Calor", "b": "heat"}, {"a": "Tumor", "b": "swelling"}, {"a": "Dolor", "b": "pain"}]}
### What's happening underneath
When tissue is damaged or germs get in, sentinel cells release chemical alarms such as histamine. Small blood vessels widen, bringing more warm blood, hence redness and heat. Their walls become leaky, letting fluid and proteins out, hence swelling. Nerve endings become more sensitive, hence pain, which encourages you to protect the area.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Alarm chemicals", "Vessels widen, leak", "White cells arrive", "Germs, debris cleared"]}
Within hours, white cells called neutrophils squeeze out of the vessels and attack. Pus is mostly dead neutrophils and the debris they've cleared.
### When it goes wrong
Short-term (acute) inflammation is helpful. Long-lasting (chronic) inflammation, which smoulders for months, damages tissues and is involved in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and heart disease.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Acute", "items": ["hours to days", "clears infection", "then switches off"]}, "r": {"t": "Chronic", "items": ["months to years", "damages tissue", "keeps going"]}}
!? Allergies are inflammation aimed at the wrong target: harmless pollen triggers a flood of histamine, which is why antihistamines help hay fever.
> Redness, heat, swelling and pain are signs of repair crews arriving, not of the body failing.

# White blood cells
White blood cells, or leukocytes, are the immune system's mobile workforce. They're made in the bone marrow and travel in the blood, but most of their work happens in tissues and lymph nodes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of white cells in blood (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Neutrophils", "v": 60.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Lymphocytes", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Monocytes", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Eosinophils", "v": 3.0}]}
### The main types
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "White blood cells", "items": ["Eaters: neutrophils", "Eaters: macrophages", "Lymphocytes: B and T", "Killers: NK cells"]}
• **Neutrophils** arrive first and swallow bacteria. They live only about a day
• **Macrophages** are large, long-lived eaters that also clean up debris
• **B cells** make antibodies
• **Helper T cells** coordinate the response; **killer T cells** destroy infected cells
• **Natural killer (NK) cells** attack infected and cancerous cells without prior training
### Eating the enemy
In 1882, the Russian zoologist Ilya Metchnikov pushed thorns into transparent starfish larvae and saw cells gather and engulf them. He called them phagocytes, "eating cells", and shared the 1908 Nobel Prize.
### When numbers matter
HIV infects and destroys helper T cells, weakening the whole adaptive response. Chemotherapy can lower neutrophil counts, leaving patients vulnerable to infections.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "4,000–11,000", "l": "white cells per µL"}, {"v": "≈ 1 day", "l": "neutrophil lifespan"}]}
> White blood cells divide the work: some eat, some make weapons, some coordinate and some kill.

# Antibodies
Antibodies, also called immunoglobulins, are proteins made by B cells. Each is shaped like a Y. The stem is the same in all antibodies of a class, but the tips of the two arms are different in each, shaped to grip one particular target, called an antigen.
### What they do
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Block germs from entering cells", "Tag germs for eating", "Clump germs together", "Trigger blood's killer proteins"]}
### Billions of shapes
Your body can make billions of different antibodies, far more than your genes could code for one by one. B cells shuffle and combine gene pieces as they mature, generating huge variety. The Japanese scientist Susumu Tonegawa won the 1987 Nobel Prize for explaining this.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "IgG", "b": "most common; crosses placenta"}, {"a": "IgA", "b": "in mucus, tears, breast milk"}, {"a": "IgM", "b": "first made in an infection"}, {"a": "IgE", "b": "parasites and allergies"}]}
### Help from mother
IgG antibodies cross the placenta, and IgA comes in breast milk, giving newborns borrowed protection for their first months while their own immune system matures.
### Antibodies as medicines
Since the 1970s scientists have been able to make large amounts of a single antibody, called monoclonal antibodies. They are now used to treat some cancers, autoimmune diseases and infections; many modern drugs ending in "-mab" are antibodies.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1890", "l": "Antibodies discovered"}, {"y": "1975", "l": "Monoclonal antibodies"}, {"y": "1987", "l": "Nobel: antibody variety"}]}
!? Pregnancy tests use antibodies that grab a hormone made only during pregnancy; the coloured line appears where they catch it.
> An antibody is a molecular tag: it finds one target and marks it for destruction.

# Immune memory
After an infection, most of the B and T cells that fought it die off. But a small group stays behind as **memory cells**, sometimes for decades. They're already tuned to the germ, so a second encounter starts at a much higher level.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "antibody response (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "1st infection", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "2nd infection", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}]}
### Faster and stronger
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "First encounter", "items": ["1–2 weeks to peak", "smaller response", "you often feel ill"]}, "r": {"t": "Second encounter", "items": ["a few days", "much bigger response", "often no symptoms"]}}
### An old observation
During the plague of Athens in 430 BC, the historian Thucydides noticed that survivors could nurse the sick without catching the disease again. That's one of the earliest written descriptions of immunity.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "430 BC", "l": "Thucydides on immunity"}, {"y": "1796", "l": "Jenner's vaccine"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "Memory cells studied"}]}
### Why some illnesses come back
• **Flu** viruses change their surface each year, so memory cells don't recognise new versions well
• **Common colds** come from hundreds of different viruses
• **Measles** gives lifelong immunity, but can also erase memory of other infections, a temporary "immune amnesia"
!? Memory cells against measles and smallpox vaccines have been found in people decades after their last exposure.
> Immune memory turns a first battle into a quick victory next time.

# Vaccines
A vaccine trains your adaptive immune system safely. It shows it something that looks like a germ, without the danger of the real disease. The immune system builds memory cells, ready for the real thing.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Harmless germ piece", "Immune system learns", "Memory cells made", "Fast defence later"]}
### Where it started
In 1796 the English doctor Edward Jenner took material from a cowpox sore on a milkmaid's hand and scratched it into the arm of a boy, James Phipps. When later exposed to smallpox, the boy didn't fall ill. The word vaccine comes from the Latin *vacca*, cow.

### Different designs

Hungarian-born Katalin Karikó and American Drew Weissman won the 2023 Nobel Prize for the work that made mRNA vaccines possible.
### In Portugal
Portugal's National Vaccination Programme began in 1965. Vaccines in it are free, and childhood coverage is among the highest in Europe.

> A vaccine is a rehearsal: the immune system practises on a harmless copy so it's ready for the real attack.

# Herd immunity
When most people in a community are immune, a germ keeps running into dead ends. Each infected person passes it on to fewer than one other person on average, and the outbreak fizzles out.
### R0, the key number
The basic reproduction number, R0, is how many people one case infects in a fully susceptible population. The higher it is, the more people need to be immune.
= Herd immunity threshold ≈ 1 − 1 ÷ R0
ex: Measles R0 ≈ 15 | 1 − 1 ÷ 15 ≈ 0.93 | About 93–95% must be immune
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% immune needed (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Measles", "v": 95.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Polio", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Smallpox", "v": 80.0}]}
### Who it protects
Newborns too young for vaccines, people with weak immune systems, those having chemotherapy and the few for whom vaccines don't work all rely on the people around them being immune.
### Eradication
Smallpox killed hundreds of millions of people in the 20th century alone. A worldwide vaccination campaign tracked down the last cases; the last natural infection was in Somalia in 1977, and the World Health Organization declared it eradicated in 1980. Rinderpest, a cattle disease, followed in 2011.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1967", "l": "WHO campaign intensified"}, {"y": "1977", "l": "Last natural smallpox case"}, {"y": "1980", "l": "Smallpox declared gone"}]}
!? When measles vaccination rates drop even a few percent below the threshold, outbreaks return, as several European countries saw in the 2010s.
> Immunity is partly a team sport: enough immune people shield those who can't be.

# Bacteria vs viruses
Both can cause infections, but they are completely different kinds of thing. Bacteria are living single cells that eat, grow and divide on their own. Viruses are tiny packages of genes in a protein coat; outside a cell they do nothing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bacteria", "items": ["living cells", "1–5 µm long", "antibiotics work"]}, "r": {"t": "Viruses", "items": ["need a host cell", "about 100 times smaller", "antibiotics useless"]}}
### How a virus copies itself
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Virus enters cell", "Hijacks its machinery", "Copies made", "Copies burst out"]}
### Why antibiotics only hit bacteria
Antibiotics target parts bacteria have and our cells don't, such as bacterial cell walls or bacterial ribosomes. Viruses have none of these. Colds, flu and most sore throats are viral, so antibiotics won't help and only encourage resistance.
### Resistance
Every antibiotic course kills susceptible bacteria and leaves tougher ones behind. Over time, resistant strains spread. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control estimates that resistant infections cause over 35,000 deaths a year in the EU and EEA.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1928", "l": "Fleming finds penicillin"}, {"y": "1940s", "l": "Mass production"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "Resistance spreading"}]}
• Take antibiotics only when prescribed
• Don't save leftovers or share them
• Antiviral drugs exist for some viruses, such as flu and HIV, but work differently
!! Antibiotics help with a cold => Colds are viral; antibiotics don't affect them
> Antibiotics are for bacteria; viruses need vaccines, antivirals or your own immune system.

# Fever
Your body temperature is controlled by the **hypothalamus**, a small region at the base of the brain that works like a thermostat. During an infection, immune cells release chemical signals that travel to the hypothalamus and turn the setting up by a degree or two.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Immune signals", "Thermostat set higher", "Shivering, feeling cold", "Body heats up"]}
### Why you shiver, then sweat
When the setting rises, your normal 37 °C suddenly feels too cold. You shiver, get goosebumps and want blankets until you reach the new target. When the infection is under control, the setting drops, and you sweat and flush to cool down: the fever "breaks".
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 35.0, "hi": 42.0, "unit": "°C", "items": [{"l": "Normal", "v": 37.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Fever", "v": 38.0}, {"l": "High fever", "v": 40.0}]}
### Why heat helps
• Many bacteria and viruses multiply more slowly at higher temperatures
• Some immune cells move and work faster
• It is an old strategy: cold-blooded lizards and fish move to warmer spots when infected
### When to worry
Fever medicines such as paracetamol and ibuprofen lower the thermostat and make you more comfortable. A fever in a young baby, a very high temperature, a stiff neck, confusion or a fever lasting several days needs medical advice.
!? The figure of 37 °C as "normal" comes from the German doctor Carl Wunderlich, who published conclusions from about a million readings in 1868. Normal temperature actually varies by person and time of day, often between about 36 and 37.5 °C.
> A fever isn't the illness; it's your body deliberately turning up the heat on the invader.

# More than five senses
The list of five senses goes back to the Greek philosopher Aristotle. Modern biology counts many more, depending on how you define a sense. A useful definition is a type of receptor that detects one kind of signal and sends it to the brain.

### The hidden senses
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Balance", "b": "fluid-filled canals in the ear"}, {"a": "Proprioception", "b": "stretch sensors in muscles"}, {"a": "Temperature", "b": "separate hot and cold sensors"}, {"a": "Interoception", "b": "hunger, full bladder, heartbeat"}]}
### Proprioception: the sense you forget
Tiny sensors in muscles, tendons and joints report how stretched and loaded they are. Your brain combines these reports into a constant map of where every part of your body is. That's how you can walk downstairs without staring at your feet, or touch your nose in the dark.
The finger-to-nose test is used by doctors to check the cerebellum, and by police in some countries to check for alcohol, which blurs this sense.

# How eyes see
The eye works much like a camera. Light passes through a clear dome, the **cornea**, then through the pupil, an opening controlled by the coloured iris. The **lens** fine-tunes the focus and projects a sharp image onto the **retina**, the light-sensitive layer at the back.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cornea bends light", "Pupil sets the amount", "Lens fine-tunes focus", "Retina detects image", "Brain builds picture"]}
### Focusing
The cornea does about two thirds of the focusing but can't change shape. The lens can: a ring of muscle lets it bulge for near objects and flatten for far ones. With age the lens stiffens, which is why most people need reading glasses from their mid-40s.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Short-sighted", "b": "focus falls in front of retina"}, {"a": "Long-sighted", "b": "focus falls behind retina"}, {"a": "Presbyopia", "b": "stiff lens after about 45"}]}
### Upside down and with a hole
In 1604 the astronomer Johannes Kepler worked out that the image on the retina is upside down. The brain interprets it as upright without any effort. There's also a blind spot in each eye where the optic nerve leaves the retina. You never notice it because the brain fills in the gap.
ex: Close your left eye | Stare at a dot on a page with your right eye | Move a pen sideways about 15 cm away: its tip vanishes
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 2/3", "l": "of focusing by cornea"}, {"v": "1 per eye", "l": "blind spot"}]}
> The eye captures the light, but the brain does the seeing.

# Rods and cones
The retina contains two kinds of light detectors, named after their shapes. They divide the work of seeing between night and day.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rods", "items": ["≈ 120 million", "dim light, motion", "only grey shades"]}, "r": {"t": "Cones", "items": ["≈ 6 million", "bright light", "colour, detail"]}}
### Where they are
Cones are packed into a small central pit called the **fovea**, which gives sharp, colourful vision where you look. Rods dominate the edges. That's why, to see a faint star, astronomers look slightly to one side of it.
### Three cones, millions of colours
The three cone types respond best to short (blue), medium (green) and long (yellowish-red) wavelengths, with lots of overlap. The brain compares their signals to work out colour. Screens use the same trick: tiny red, green and blue dots mix into every colour you see.
### Getting used to the dark
Walking into a dark room, you're nearly blind at first. Rods need about 20–30 minutes to reach full sensitivity. Bright light resets them in seconds.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% with red–green colour blindness (N. Europe)", "items": [{"l": "Men", "v": 8.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Women", "v": 0.5}]}
Red–green colour blindness is far more common in men because the genes for those cones sit on the X chromosome. Men have only one X; women have a spare.
!? The chemist John Dalton described his own colour blindness in 1798. It was called "Daltonism" for years, and in Portuguese it still is: *daltonismo*.
> Rods let you see in the dark; cones let you see in colour.

# How ears hear
Sound is a wave of pressure travelling through air. Your ear turns these tiny pressure changes into nerve signals in a few steps.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sound enters canal", "Eardrum vibrates", "Three bones amplify", "Cochlea fluid moves", "Hair cells signal"]}
### The three bones
The hammer, anvil and stirrup form a lever chain. Together with the large eardrum pushing on the much smaller window into the cochlea, they boost the pressure about 20 times. Without this, most sound would bounce off the fluid in the inner ear.
### The cochlea: a sound map
The cochlea is a snail-shaped tube about 3.5 cm long if unrolled. Along it run about 15,000 hair cells. High-pitched sounds shake the start of the tube; low sounds travel to the far end. The brain reads pitch from which hair cells are firing.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "loudness, decibels (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Whisper", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Conversation", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Busy traffic", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Rock concert", "v": 110.0}]}
### Protect them: they don't regrow
Human hair cells don't regenerate. Loud noise bends and breaks them, causing permanent hearing loss and ringing (tinnitus). In the EU, employers must provide hearing protection where noise reaches 85 decibels. Because the decibel scale is logarithmic, every 10 dB step is ten times more sound energy.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 20.0, "hi": 20000.0, "log": true, "unit": "pitch, Hz", "items": [{"l": "Lowest heard", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Speech", "v": 500.0}, {"l": "Young ears' limit", "v": 20000.0}]}
!? With age, high-pitched hearing fades first. Many adults over 30 can't hear tones above about 15,000 Hz.
> Your ear turns air pressure into fluid waves, then into a map of pitch your brain can read.

# Taste is mostly smell
The tongue senses only five basic tastes. Everything else we call "flavour" is assembled by the brain from smell, texture, temperature and even sound.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sweet", "b": "sugars: energy"}, {"a": "Salty", "b": "sodium: minerals"}, {"a": "Sour", "b": "acids: unripe, spoiled"}, {"a": "Bitter", "b": "possible poisons"}]}

### Taste buds renew
You have a few thousand taste buds, mostly on the tongue but also on the palate and throat. Their cells are replaced roughly every two weeks, which is why a burnt tongue recovers quickly.

# The skeleton
The skeleton is the body's frame. It supports you, protects soft organs, anchors muscles, makes blood cells and stores minerals. It makes up roughly 15% of body weight.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Skeleton's jobs", "items": ["Support and shape", "Protect organs", "Levers for movement", "Make blood, store calcium"]}
### Why babies have more bones
A newborn has about 270–300 bones and bony pieces. Many are joined by flexible cartilage that later turns to bone and fuses. The skull, for example, has soft gaps called fontanelles that let it squeeze through the birth canal and grow with the brain; they close by about the age of two.
### Hands and feet dominate
Each hand has 27 bones and each foot 26, giving 106 of the 206. All those small bones allow fine movements and flexible, shock-absorbing feet.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "number of bones (adult)", "items": [{"l": "Hands and feet", "v": 106.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Rest of body", "v": 100.0}]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Skull", "b": "22 bones, most fused"}, {"a": "Spine", "b": "26 bones in adults"}, {"a": "Ribs", "b": "12 pairs, usually"}]}
The number 206 is typical, not fixed. Some people have an extra rib or extra small bones in tendons.
!? Your spine starts with 33 vertebrae. The lowest ones fuse into the sacrum and tailbone, leaving 26 separate bones in most adults.
> Over half your bones are in your hands and feet, the parts that do the most delicate work.

# Bones are alive
Bone looks like dead stone in a museum, but in your body it's a living tissue with blood vessels, nerves and busy cells. About a third is a flexible protein, collagen; the rest is hard calcium phosphate mineral. The mix makes bone both strong and slightly springy.
### Constant renovation
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Old bone dissolved", "Space cleared", "New bone laid down", "Mineral hardens it"]}
Two cell types share the work. **Osteoclasts** dissolve old or damaged bone; **osteoblasts** lay down new bone. Over about ten years, most of your skeleton is replaced.
### A calcium bank
About 99% of the body's calcium is in the skeleton. If blood calcium falls, hormones release some from bone, because nerves and muscles need a steady supply.
### Use it or lose it
Bone grows stronger where it's loaded and thins where it isn't. Tennis players have thicker bones in their racket arm. Astronauts lose bone in weightlessness despite daily exercise.
ex: Bone loss in space ≈ 1% per month in the hips | 6-month mission | ≈ 6% of hip bone lost
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "99%", "l": "of body calcium in bone"}, {"v": "≈ 10 years", "l": "to renew skeleton"}]}
Bone mass peaks in the late 20s and slowly declines. In osteoporosis, it becomes thin and fragile, a common cause of hip fractures in older people. Walking, running, dancing and lifting help keep bones strong.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Bones are dead scaffolding", "r": "Living tissue, rebuilt constantly"}]}
> Your skeleton is rebuilt throughout life, guided by the loads you put on it.

# Muscles
You have roughly 600 skeletal muscles, around a third to two fifths of body weight. They attach to bones through tough cords called tendons, and they work by shortening.
### Why muscles only pull
Inside each muscle fibre, two kinds of protein filaments, actin and myosin, overlap. Using ATP for energy, tiny myosin heads grab the actin and pull, sliding the filaments past each other. They can shorten the muscle but have no way to push it longer.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nerve signal arrives", "Calcium released", "Myosin pulls actin", "Muscle shortens"]}
### Working in pairs
Because muscles can only pull, they come in opposing pairs. When one contracts, its partner relaxes and is stretched back out.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Biceps", "items": ["front of upper arm", "bends the elbow"]}, "r": {"t": "Triceps", "items": ["back of upper arm", "straightens it"]}}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Quadriceps", "b": "straighten the knee"}, {"a": "Hamstrings", "b": "bend the knee"}, {"a": "Gluteus maximus", "b": "largest muscle in the body"}]}
### Records
• Largest: the gluteus maximus, in the buttock
• Smallest: the stapedius, about 1 mm, which steadies the stirrup bone in the ear
• Strongest for its size: often said to be the masseter, the jaw muscle
!? Even standing still, many muscles are working. Small, constant adjustments in your calves and back stop you from toppling over.
> Muscles can only pull, so movement comes from pairs taking turns.

# Three types of muscle
All muscle contracts using actin and myosin, but the body has three versions, each built for a different job.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Muscle", "items": ["Skeletal: moves bones", "Smooth: moves organs", "Cardiac: pumps blood"]}
### Skeletal muscle
Under the microscope, skeletal muscle looks striped. Its fibres are long cells with many nuclei. You control it consciously, it contracts quickly and powerfully, and it tires with hard use.
### Smooth muscle
Smooth muscle has no stripes. It lines the gut, blood vessels, airways and bladder, and controls the size of your pupils. It contracts slowly and can stay contracted for a long time, and you can't control it by will.
### Cardiac muscle
Heart muscle is striped like skeletal muscle but branched, with cells linked by special junctions so an electrical wave spreads through it. It is packed with mitochondria and has its own blood supply, so it keeps going for a lifetime.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Skeletal", "b": "striped, voluntary, tires"}, {"a": "Smooth", "b": "smooth, automatic, slow"}, {"a": "Cardiac", "b": "striped, automatic, tireless"}]}
### Blurred lines
The diaphragm is skeletal muscle, yet it keeps you breathing while you sleep. You can override it to hold your breath, but only for so long. Hiccups are involuntary spasms of this muscle.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Voluntary", "items": ["skeletal muscle", "you decide"]}, "r": {"t": "Involuntary", "items": ["smooth, cardiac", "runs itself"]}}
!! The heart muscle never rests => It relaxes for over half of every beat; that steady rhythm of rest is why it doesn't tire
> Three muscles, three jobs: move the body, move what's inside it, and keep the blood flowing.

# Smallest and largest
The extremes of the skeleton are found at opposite scales of work: a bone that transmits whispers and one that carries your weight.
### The stirrup
The stapes, or stirrup, is one of the three tiny bones in the middle ear. It's about 3 mm long and weighs only a few milligrams. Its footplate sits in the oval window of the cochlea, pumping sound vibrations into the fluid like a tiny piston.
### The femur
The thigh bone is the longest, heaviest and among the strongest bones. Its rounded head fits into the hip socket, and its shaft is a hollow tube, which gives great strength for little weight, the same principle as a bicycle frame.

== bo-lungs

# The airway
Air takes a branching route into the lungs, like a tree turned upside down. The windpipe divides into two, each branch divides again, and so on about 23 times until the tubes end in tiny air sacs.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nose or mouth", "Throat and larynx", "Trachea", "Two bronchi", "Bronchioles, air sacs"]}
### The nose does a lot
Breathing through your nose warms the air to near body temperature, adds moisture and filters out dust. By the time air reaches the lungs it is warm and almost fully humid, whatever the weather outside.
### Keeping the tubes open
The trachea, about 10–12 cm long, is held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage you can feel at the front of your neck. Smaller tubes, the bronchioles, have no cartilage but are ringed by smooth muscle. In asthma, that muscle tightens and the lining swells, narrowing the airways.
ex: Each tube splits in 2 | After 23 splits | 2 to the power 23 ≈ 8 million tiny tubes
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 23", "l": "levels of branching"}, {"v": "≈ 8 million", "l": "smallest airways"}]}
### Why things go right
The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left. That's why a peanut or small toy breathed in usually ends up in the right lung.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Conducting zone", "items": ["nose to bronchioles", "moves, cleans air"]}, "r": {"t": "Exchange zone", "items": ["smallest tubes, sacs", "swaps gases"]}}
!? When you swallow, the larynx rises and a flap, the epiglottis, tips over the airway entrance. You can't breathe and swallow at the same moment.
> The airway is a branching tree that cleans and warms the air before it reaches the leaves.

# Alveoli
The air sacs, or **alveoli**, are where the real business of breathing happens. Each is about 0.2 mm across, wrapped in a net of capillaries. A 2004 study counted about 480 million in an average adult's lungs, with more in larger lungs.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "480 million", "l": "alveoli"}, {"v": "≈ 70 m²", "l": "total surface"}]}
### Why so many tiny sacs?
Splitting the lungs into millions of small bubbles creates a huge surface in a small space. A single sac of the same volume would have only a tiny fraction of the area. The gap between air and blood is under a thousandth of a millimetre, much thinner than a sheet of paper.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "area, m² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Skin", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Alveoli", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Badminton court", "v": 82.0}]}
### Surfactant: anti-collapse coating
Water lining small bubbles pulls them inwards, tending to collapse them. Cells in the alveoli make **surfactant**, a soapy coating that reduces this pull. Babies born very early often lack it and struggle to breathe; giving surfactant through a tube has saved many premature babies since the 1990s.
### When the walls break down
In emphysema, usually caused by smoking, the walls between alveoli are destroyed. Small sacs merge into large baggy spaces, the surface area falls, and every breath delivers less oxygen.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Air fills alveolus", "Oxygen crosses wall", "Enters capillary blood", "Carried to heart"]}
!? The lungs are so light and airy that a healthy piece of lung tissue floats in water.
> Hundreds of millions of tiny bubbles turn your lungs into a huge surface folded into your chest.

# Gas exchange
Gas exchange needs no pumps or energy. It relies on **diffusion**: molecules spread from where they're concentrated to where they're scarce. The lungs simply keep the differences large.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Oxygen", "items": ["high in air sacs", "low in arriving blood", "moves into blood"]}, "r": {"t": "Carbon dioxide", "items": ["high in arriving blood", "low in air sacs", "moves into air"]}}
### Measuring the push
Physiologists measure gas pressures in mmHg. Blood arriving from the body has much less oxygen than the air in the sacs, so oxygen flows in fast.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "oxygen pressure, mmHg (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Air sacs", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Blood arriving", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Blood leaving", "v": 95.0}]}
### Fast enough to spare
At rest, a red blood cell spends about three quarters of a second in a lung capillary, but it's fully loaded with oxygen in about a quarter of a second. That spare time is a safety margin used during exercise, when blood rushes through faster.
### Carrying the gases
• Oxygen: about 98% rides on haemoglobin in red cells; only a little dissolves in plasma
• Carbon dioxide: most travels as bicarbonate dissolved in plasma
• Breathing out removes CO2, which also helps keep blood's acidity steady
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Body cells make CO2", "Blood carries it", "CO2 diffuses out", "Breathed away"]}
!? Fish gills use the same diffusion trick in water, but with blood flowing opposite to the water to keep the difference high along the whole gill.
> Gas exchange is just molecules moving downhill, from more to less, across a very thin wall.

# The diaphragm
The diaphragm is a dome-shaped sheet of muscle that separates the chest from the abdomen. It is the main breathing muscle and does most of the work at rest.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Breathing in", "items": ["diaphragm contracts", "dome flattens", "chest gets bigger"]}, "r": {"t": "Breathing out", "items": ["diaphragm relaxes", "dome rises", "lungs spring back"]}}
### How a muscle moves air
Lungs have no muscles of their own. They follow the chest wall. When the diaphragm pulls down, the chest cavity gets bigger, pressure inside the lungs drops slightly below the air outside, and air flows in. At rest, breathing out is passive: the elastic lungs simply recoil.
= Bigger volume → lower pressure → air flows in
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Diaphragm contracts", "Chest volume rises", "Lung pressure drops", "Air rushes in"]}
### Helpers
Muscles between the ribs lift and widen the ribcage, especially during exercise. For a forceful breath out, such as blowing out candles, the abdominal muscles squeeze upwards.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Diaphragm", "b": "main muscle, quiet breathing"}, {"a": "Rib muscles", "b": "widen the chest"}, {"a": "Abdominals", "b": "forced breathing out"}]}
### Hiccups
A hiccup is a sudden twitch of the diaphragm, followed by the vocal cords snapping shut, which makes the "hic". The reflex is involuntary and its purpose is still debated.
!? The diaphragm's nerve starts high in the neck and travels all the way down. That's why spinal injuries very high in the neck can stop breathing, while lower ones don't.
> Lungs are passive bellows; the diaphragm pulls them open.

# What triggers breathing
Breathing is controlled by a centre in the brainstem that sets a rhythm automatically. You can override it to talk, sing or hold your breath, but not for long.
### Carbon dioxide drives the urge
Sensors in the brainstem respond to CO2. When CO2 rises in the blood, it makes the fluid around the brain slightly more acidic. The sensors detect this and drive you to breathe faster and deeper.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["CO2 builds up", "Fluid more acidic", "Brainstem sensors fire", "Urge to breathe"]}
Sensors in the neck arteries also monitor oxygen, but they only kick in strongly when oxygen falls quite low.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "CO2 sensors", "items": ["brainstem", "main daily control"]}, "r": {"t": "Oxygen sensors", "items": ["neck arteries", "backup when very low"]}}
### A dangerous gap
Because the urge depends on CO2, you can run low on oxygen without feeling breathless. This causes real dangers:
• **Hyperventilating before free-diving** blows off CO2, delaying the urge to surface; divers can black out underwater
• **Carbon monoxide** poisoning starves you of oxygen without any feeling of suffocation
• **Thin air at altitude** lowers oxygen while CO2 stays normal, so the alarm is weak
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Breathlessness means low oxygen", "r": "It mostly means high CO2"}]}
!? You physically can't hold your breath until you pass out on dry land and stay that way: if you faint, the brainstem takes over and breathing restarts.
> Your body breathes to get rid of carbon dioxide; getting oxygen comes along with it.

# Breaths by the numbers
Breathing is so constant that the totals are huge, even though each breath is small.
ex: 15 breaths per minute × 60 | = 900 per hour × 24 | ≈ 21,600 breaths per day
### How much air?
A quiet breath moves about half a litre. But about 150 ml of it never reaches the air sacs; it stays in the windpipe and tubes, called "dead space". Deep breaths are more efficient than fast, shallow ones for that reason.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "litres (typical adult)", "items": [{"l": "Quiet breath", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "Biggest breath out", "v": 4.5}, {"l": "Total lung capacity", "v": 6.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Always left inside", "v": 1.2}]}
### Up and down with need
At rest you move about 6–8 litres of air a minute. In hard exercise, trained athletes can move over 100 litres a minute, breathing faster and much deeper.
ex: 7 litres per minute | × 1,440 minutes in a day | ≈ 10,000 litres of air a day
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 20,000", "l": "breaths a day"}, {"v": "≈ 10,000 L", "l": "air breathed a day"}]}
### Never completely empty
Even after the deepest exhale, about 1.2 litres stays in your lungs. This keeps the air sacs from collapsing and keeps gas exchange going between breaths.
!? Newborns breathe about 30–60 times a minute; the rate slows through childhood to adult levels.
> A small breath, repeated twenty thousand times, moves about ten thousand litres of air through you each day.

# Air we breathe
Dry air is mostly nitrogen, a gas your body breathes in and out unchanged. The oxygen that keeps you alive is only about a fifth of it.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of dry air", "items": [{"l": "Nitrogen", "v": 78.0}, {"l": "Oxygen", "v": 21.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Argon", "v": 0.9}, {"l": "CO2", "v": 0.04}]}
### What changes in one breath
Your body takes only about a quarter of the oxygen in each breath. The rest comes straight back out, along with carbon dioxide and water vapour.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Air breathed in", "items": ["≈ 21% oxygen", "≈ 0.04% CO2"]}, "r": {"t": "Air breathed out", "items": ["≈ 16% oxygen", "≈ 4% CO2, more water"]}}
### Why rescue breaths work
Exhaled air still has about 16% oxygen, plenty to help someone who isn't breathing. In modern first-aid training, though, chest compressions come first, because circulating the oxygen already in the blood matters most. In Portugal, the emergency number is 112.
### Nitrogen and divers
Under water, pressure forces extra nitrogen to dissolve into the blood. If a diver rises too fast, it forms bubbles, like opening a fizzy drink: decompression sickness, or "the bends".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Nitrogen", "b": "inert, but matters to divers"}, {"a": "Oxygen", "b": "what cells actually use"}, {"a": "Carbon dioxide", "b": "waste we breathe out"}]}
!? The CO2 in outdoor air has risen from about 0.028% before the Industrial Revolution to over 0.042% today, a rise of about half.
> Most of every breath goes straight back out; your body keeps only a quarter of its oxygen.

# Cleaning the air
Each day you breathe in thousands of litres of air, along with dust, pollen, smoke and germs. The airways have a multi-stage cleaning system.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nose hairs: big particles", "Mucus traps fine dust", "Cilia sweep it upward", "Swallowed or coughed out"]}
### The mucus escalator
The airway lining is coated with a thin layer of sticky mucus. Beneath it, millions of tiny hairs called **cilia** beat in waves, many times a second, pushing the mucus up towards the throat at a few millimetres a minute. You swallow it without noticing, and stomach acid destroys the trapped germs.
### The last line
Particles small enough to reach the air sacs are swallowed by **macrophages**, roaming immune cells that live in the alveoli.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "PM10 dust", "b": "caught in nose and throat"}, {"a": "PM2.5 particles", "b": "reach the deep lungs"}, {"a": "Ultrafine soot", "b": "can enter the blood"}]}
### What harms it
Tobacco smoke paralyses and destroys cilia. Mucus then builds up and has to be coughed out, which is the "smoker's cough". Fine particles from traffic and fires slip past the defences, which is why air-quality alerts matter during Portugal's summer wildfires.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Mucus is just a sign of illness", "r": "It's a constant cleaning system"}]}
!? A cough can blast air out of your airways at speeds often estimated at well over 50 km/h, carrying mucus with it.
> Your airways are self-cleaning: a moving carpet of mucus carries dirt out of the lungs every day.

# Altitude
The air higher up has the same 21% oxygen, but it's thinner: the pressure is lower, so each breath contains fewer molecules of everything. At about 5,500 m the pressure is half that at sea level.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "oxygen per breath, % of sea level (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Sea level", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Torre, 1,993 m", "v": 78.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "3,000 m", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Everest summit", "v": 33.0}]}
### Altitude sickness
Above about 2,500 m, some people develop headache, nausea, tiredness and poor sleep. In rare severe cases, fluid builds up in the lungs or brain, which is life-threatening. The key prevention is going up slowly and descending if symptoms get worse.
### How the body adapts
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Minutes", "l": "Breathe faster, heart races"}, {"y": "Hours", "l": "Kidneys release EPO"}, {"y": "Weeks", "l": "More red blood cells"}]}
This is why endurance athletes often train at altitude: they return to sea level with extra red cells, at least for a few weeks.
### Born for the heights
People whose ancestors lived for thousands of years on the Tibetan Plateau or in the Andes have inherited adaptations. Many Tibetans, for example, carry gene variants that let them thrive with lower oxygen without an extreme rise in red cells.
!? Portugal's highest point is Mount Pico in the Azores, at 2,351 m. On the mainland, Torre in the Serra da Estrela reaches 1,993 m, just below where altitude sickness usually starts.
> Up high, the oxygen share is the same but the air is thinner; the body adapts by making more red cells.

# Slow breathing
Breathing is one of the few automatic functions you can also control at will. That makes it a handle on the **autonomic nervous system**, the part that runs heart rate and stress responses without conscious thought.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fight or flight", "items": ["fast, shallow breaths", "heart speeds up"]}, "r": {"t": "Rest and digest", "items": ["slow, deep breaths", "heart slows down"]}}
### Why it works
Each breath already nudges your heart: it speeds up slightly as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out, via the vagus nerve. Making exhales longer gives the slowing phase more time. At around six breaths a minute, the rhythms of breathing, heart rate and blood pressure tend to line up, which amplifies the effect.
ex: Breathe in for 4 seconds | Breathe out for 6 seconds | 10 seconds per breath = 6 breaths a minute
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["In for 4 seconds", "Out for 6 seconds", "Heart rate dips", "Repeat calmly"]}
### What the evidence says
Studies show slow breathing can lower heart rate and blood pressure in the short term and help people feel calmer. It's a useful tool before an exam, a talk or sleep. It doesn't replace medical treatment for anxiety or high blood pressure.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Long slow exhale", "Vagus nerve active", "Heart slows", "Feeling of calm"]}
!! Deep breathing means huge gulps of air => Calming breaths are slow and gentle; big fast gulps can make you dizzy
> Your breath is a remote control for your nervous system: slow it down and your body follows.

# How many cells?
Nobody can count cells one by one, so scientists estimate them organ by organ. A widely cited 2013 study arrived at about 37 trillion for an adult. A more detailed 2023 estimate gave about 36 trillion for a typical man and 28 trillion for a typical woman, mostly because of body size.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 30–37 tn", "l": "human cells in an adult"}, {"v": "200+", "l": "cell types"}, {"v": "≈ 38 tn", "l": "bacteria living on and in you"}]}
### Small cells win the count
Counting by number and counting by weight give very different pictures. Red blood cells are tiny, about 7–8 thousandths of a millimetre across, but there are around 25 trillion of them, so they make up most of your cells. Fat and muscle cells are far fewer but much bigger, so they make up most of your weight.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of all human cells, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Red blood cells", "v": 84.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Platelets", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "All the rest", "v": 11.0}]}
### Extremes of size
• Longest: nerve cells running from the spine to the foot, up to about 1 m
• Largest by volume: the egg cell, about 0.1 mm, just visible to the eye
• Smallest: sperm cells and red blood cells
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.001, "hi": 1000.0, "log": true, "unit": "size, mm", "items": [{"l": "Red cell", "v": 0.008}, {"l": "Egg cell", "v": 0.1, "hi": true}, {"l": "Long neuron", "v": 1000.0}]}
!? You carry roughly as many bacterial cells as human ones, mostly in the gut. Because bacteria are so small, together they weigh only about 0.2 kg.
> Your body is a crowd of tens of trillions of cells, and most of them are tiny red blood cells.

# Inside a cell
A typical human cell is about 10–20 thousandths of a millimetre across, yet it holds dozens of specialised compartments called **organelles**. Each does one job, much as departments do in a factory.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Nucleus", "b": "head office: holds the DNA"}, {"a": "Mitochondria", "b": "power plants: make ATP"}, {"a": "Ribosomes", "b": "assembly lines: build proteins"}, {"a": "Membrane", "b": "gatekeeper: controls entry"}]}
### The shipping department
Many proteins are built on ribosomes attached to a folded network of membranes called the **endoplasmic reticulum**. They are then sent to the **Golgi apparatus**, which sorts, labels and packages them in tiny bubbles for delivery inside or outside the cell. **Lysosomes** are recycling bins full of enzymes that break down worn-out parts.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ribosome builds protein", "ER folds it", "Golgi labels and packs", "Bubble delivers it"]}
### The gatekeeper
The cell membrane is a double layer of fatty molecules about 5–10 millionths of a millimetre thick. Water and oxygen slip through, but most other things need special protein channels or pumps. The cell is not a bag of liquid either: a scaffold of protein fibres, the **cytoskeleton**, gives it shape and acts as tracks for moving cargo.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Passes easily", "items": ["oxygen, CO2", "water (slowly)"]}, "r": {"t": "Needs a channel", "items": ["sugar, salts", "proteins"]}}
!! A cell is a blob of jelly with a nucleus => It's a crowded, organised factory with sorting, transport and recycling systems
> Every cell runs its own factory: instructions, power, assembly lines and a guarded gate.

# Mitochondria
Mitochondria are bean-shaped organelles a few thousandths of a millimetre long. Inside, a heavily folded inner membrane gives a huge surface for the chemistry of **cellular respiration**: breaking down sugar and fat with oxygen to make ATP, and releasing carbon dioxide and water.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Food and oxygen in", "Electrons passed along", "Protons pumped", "ATP made", "CO2 and water out"]}
### Energy currency
ATP works like a rechargeable battery. Using it releases energy and leaves ADP, which mitochondria recharge. You hold only about 50–100 g of ATP at any moment, but you recycle roughly your own body weight of it every day.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ body weight", "l": "of ATP recycled a day"}, {"v": "1,000s", "l": "mitochondria per heart cell"}, {"v": "37", "l": "genes in mitochondrial DNA"}]}
### Ancient lodgers
Mitochondria were once free-living bacteria. Around 1.5–2 billion years ago, one was swallowed by another cell and survived, and the partnership stuck. The American biologist Lynn Margulis championed this idea from 1967; it is now accepted. That's why mitochondria have their own small, circular DNA.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "≈ 2 bn yr", "l": "bacterium moves in"}, {"y": "1967", "l": "Margulis's theory"}, {"y": "1981", "l": "mtDNA fully sequenced"}]}
Sperm do carry a few mitochondria, but the egg destroys them after fertilisation. So your mitochondrial DNA is a copy of your mother's, her mother's, and so on. Geneticists use this to trace maternal ancestry back many thousands of years.
!? Cells that work hardest, such as heart, muscle and liver cells, pack the most mitochondria. In heart muscle they take up about a third of each cell.
> Mitochondria are tamed ancient bacteria that recharge the cell's batteries, passed down only from mothers.

# DNA in every cell
Your full set of DNA, the **genome**, has about 3.2 billion letters (base pairs) from each parent, so about 6.4 billion per cell. Stretched out, each DNA molecule is a thread only 2 millionths of a millimetre wide, and together they reach about 2 metres.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 2 m", "l": "of DNA per cell"}, {"v": "46", "l": "chromosomes (23 pairs)"}, {"v": "≈ 6 µm", "l": "width of a nucleus"}]}
### How it fits
The packing ratio is like stuffing about 40 km of fine thread into a tennis ball. DNA wraps around spool-like proteins called **histones**, the spools coil into a fibre, and the fibre folds into loops. Before a cell divides, each chromosome compacts even more into the familiar X shape.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Chromosome", "Folded loops", "Coiled fibre", "DNA wound on histones", "Double helix"]}
### What's on it
Only about 1–2% of the genome codes for proteins, in about 20,000 genes. The rest includes switches that turn genes on and off, structural regions and long stretches whose role is still debated.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of genome, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Protein-coding", "v": 1.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Everything else", "v": 98.5}]}
Almost every cell has the same DNA. A nerve cell and a skin cell differ because they read different genes. Mature red blood cells are the exception: they throw out their nucleus to make room for haemoglobin.
!? All the DNA in your body laid end to end would reach to the Sun and back many times over.
> Two metres of instructions are folded into every nucleus, and each cell type reads a different chapter.

# From gene to protein
In 1958 Francis Crick summed up the flow of genetic information as "DNA makes RNA makes protein". He called it the central dogma. It happens in two stages.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gene in DNA (nucleus)", "Copied into mRNA", "mRNA leaves nucleus", "Ribosome reads it", "Protein folds"]}
### Transcription
In the nucleus, an enzyme unzips a short stretch of DNA and builds a matching strand of messenger RNA. The DNA stays safely at home; only the working copy travels.
### Translation
At the ribosome, each group of three letters, a **codon**, stands for one amino acid. Transfer RNA molecules bring the right amino acid for each codon, and the ribosome links them into a chain, a few amino acids per second. The chain then folds into a precise 3D shape that decides what the protein does.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "AUG", "b": "start (methionine)"}, {"a": "UUU", "b": "phenylalanine"}, {"a": "UAA, UAG, UGA", "b": "stop"}]}
ex: 4 letters (A, C, G, U) | 3 letters per codon | 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 codons for 20 amino acids + stop signals
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "4³ = 64 codons", "items": [{"s": "4", "m": "RNA letters"}, {"s": "3", "m": "letters per codon"}, {"s": "64", "m": "possible codons"}]}
!? The mRNA COVID-19 vaccines used this machinery: they delivered a message, and your own ribosomes built a harmless piece of the virus's spike protein to train the immune system.
> A gene is a recipe; mRNA is the photocopy taken to the kitchen; the ribosome is the cook.

# Cell division
Your body makes roughly 330 billion new cells a day, nearly 4 million a second, mostly blood and gut cells. Almost all come from **mitosis**, which produces two identical copies of a cell.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Growth", "DNA copied", "Mitosis", "Cell splits"]}
### The steps of mitosis
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Prophase", "b": "chromosomes condense"}, {"a": "Metaphase", "b": "they line up in the middle"}, {"a": "Anaphase", "b": "copies pulled apart"}, {"a": "Telophase", "b": "two new nuclei form"}]}
In a fast-dividing human cell, the whole cycle takes about a day, and mitosis itself only about an hour. Before division, checkpoints test whether the DNA is copied correctly and undamaged. If not, the cell pauses for repairs or destroys itself.
### Limits and exceptions
Most body cells can only divide about 40–60 times, a ceiling found by Leonard Hayflick in 1961. Each division shortens the protective caps on chromosomes, called **telomeres**, a little.

!! Cells divide forever => Most body cells stop after about 40–60 divisions; stem cells and cancer cells get around this limit
> Mitosis copies a cell perfectly; meiosis shuffles genes to make eggs and sperm.

# Cells renew at different rates
How fast a tissue renews depends on how much wear it takes. The gut lining is scraped by food and acid, so it is rebuilt in days. Brain cells hold memories and connections built over a lifetime, so most are never replaced.

### Dating cells with bombs
How do we know? In the 2000s the Swedish scientist Jonas Frisén used an unusual clock. Nuclear bomb tests in the 1950s and early 1960s doubled the amount of carbon-14 in the air, and it then fell steadily. Cells lock in the carbon-14 level of the year their DNA was made, so measuring it reveals a cell's "birth year".

### What the dating showed
• Heart muscle cells renew slowly: about 1% a year at age 25, falling with age
• Fat cells turn over about every 8–10 years, though their number stays stable
• Neurons in the cortex date from birth; a few areas may add new ones, still debated

> Your body is part brand new and part original: gut lining from this week, cortex from your birth.

# Stem cells
Most cells are specialists that cannot change job. Stem cells are different: when they divide, one daughter can stay a stem cell while the other matures into a specialised cell. That lets tissues refill themselves for decades.

### Kinds of stem cells

# When cells go wrong
Normal cells obey a strict rulebook: divide only when told, stop when crowded, repair damage or self-destruct. Cancer appears when a cell's DNA collects enough mutations to break several of those rules.
### Accelerators and brakes
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Oncogenes", "items": ["stuck accelerator", "push division"]}, "r": {"t": "Tumour suppressors", "items": ["broken brakes", "e.g. p53, BRCA1"]}}
Usually one mutation is not enough; it takes several in the same cell. That's a main reason cancer becomes more common with age: there has been more time for mistakes to pile up. Some come from random copying errors; others from tobacco smoke, UV light, alcohol or certain viruses.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["First mutation", "More mutations pile up", "Growth escapes control", "Tumour forms", "Cells may spread"]}
### Benign or malignant?
A benign tumour grows but stays in place. A malignant one invades nearby tissue and can **metastasise**: cells break off, travel in blood or lymph, and seed new tumours elsewhere. Most cancer deaths are caused by this spread.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 2.7 m", "l": "new cancer cases a year, EU"}, {"v": "≈ 4 in 10", "l": "cases linked to avoidable risks"}]}
!? The HPV vaccine, given to pre-teens in Portugal's national vaccination plan, protects against the virus behind most cervical cancers. Countries with high uptake are seeing those cancers fall.
> Cancer is a cell that has lost its brakes and gained an accelerator, usually after many mutations.

# Seeing cells
Cells are far too small to see with the naked eye, so their story begins with lenses. Seeing them changed biology from guessing to observing.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1665", "l": "Hooke names cells"}, {"y": "1676", "l": "bacteria first seen"}, {"y": "1839", "l": "cell theory"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "electron microscope"}]}
### Hooke and Leeuwenhoek
Robert Hooke looked at thin slices of cork through his compound microscope and saw rows of empty boxes, which reminded him of monks' small rooms. He published the drawings in his book **Micrographia** in 1665. He was seeing only dead cell walls.
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch cloth merchant, ground his own tiny single lenses that magnified around 270 times, far better than others of his day. In letters to the Royal Society in London he described swimming "animalcules": the first bacteria, protists and sperm cells ever seen.
### Cell theory
In 1838–39 the German scientists Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann proposed that all plants and animals are made of cells. In 1855 Rudolf Virchow added that every cell comes from another cell. Later, the Spanish scientist Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed that the brain is also made of separate cells, winning a Nobel Prize in 1906.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.0, "hi": 1000000.0, "log": true, "unit": "magnification, ×", "items": [{"l": "Hooke", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Leeuwenhoek", "v": 270.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Electron micr.", "v": 500000.0}]}
> Better lenses revealed a hidden world: every living thing is built from cells, and every cell comes from a cell.

# How much sleep?
Expert panels in the US and Europe recommend similar amounts. Needs fall with age, fast in childhood and slowly in adulthood.

### Can anyone get by on less?
A few people genuinely need only about 6 hours. In 2009 researchers in California found a rare gene variant in a mother and daughter who felt fully rested on about 6 hours, and more such genes have since been found. They are very rare. Most people who claim to cope on 5–6 hours perform worse in tests and simply don't notice.
### Too little, and too much
Large population studies link regularly sleeping under 6 hours to higher risks of heart disease, diabetes, obesity and depression. Very long sleep is also linked to poor health, though often as a sign of illness rather than the cause.

### How to tell
• You wake without an alarm on most free days
• You don't nod off when sitting quietly in the afternoon
• You don't need a long lie-in every weekend

# Sleep cycles
Sleep isn't one state. Scientists record brain waves, eye movements and muscle tone and split the night into stages: three of non-REM sleep (N1 to N3) and one of REM sleep.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["N1: drifting off", "N2: light sleep", "N3: deep sleep", "REM: dreaming"]}
### What happens in each stage
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "N1", "b": "a few minutes; easily woken"}, {"a": "N2", "b": "half the night; spindles"}, {"a": "N3", "b": "slow waves; hard to wake"}, {"a": "REM", "b": "vivid dreams; body still"}]}
N2 has bursts of brain activity called **sleep spindles**, which seem to help store new skills and facts. Brief awakenings between cycles are normal; you usually don't remember them.
### The night changes shape
The cycles aren't identical. Early in the night they are rich in deep sleep. Towards morning, deep sleep fades and REM periods get longer, up to about an hour. That's why you often wake from a dream.

# Deep sleep
Deep sleep, or N3, is also called **slow-wave sleep**. Huge numbers of neurons fire together and fall silent together, producing big, slow brain waves about once or twice a second. It's the hardest stage to wake from, and sleepwalking and night terrors start here.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Awake", "items": ["fast, small waves", "neurons out of step"]}, "r": {"t": "Deep sleep", "items": ["slow, big waves", "neurons in step"]}}
### Body repair
Breathing and heart rate slow, and blood pressure drops. The pituitary gland sends out its biggest pulse of **growth hormone** of the day, mostly in the first deep sleep after falling asleep. In children it powers growth; in adults it supports the repair of muscle, bone and other tissues.
### Brain cleaning
In 2013 the Danish-American scientist Maiken Nedergaard reported that in sleeping mice, the spaces between brain cells widen and fluid flushes through faster. She called it the **glymphatic system**. It may help clear waste such as amyloid-beta, a protein linked to Alzheimer's disease. How it works in humans is still being studied, and some recent findings question parts of the theory.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Brain cells shrink a little", "Gaps widen", "Fluid washes through", "Waste carried away"]}
### Memory
During deep sleep, the hippocampus replays the day's experiences and passes them to the cortex for long-term storage.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Deep sleep", "items": ["Growth hormone", "Tissue repair", "Waste clearance", "Memory storage"]}
!? Deep sleep falls a lot with age. A 70-year-old may get only a fraction of the slow-wave sleep they had at 20.
> Deep sleep is the body's night shift: repair crews in the body, cleaners and filing clerks in the brain.

# REM sleep
REM stands for rapid eye movement. It was discovered in 1953 at the University of Chicago by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman, who noticed sleepers' eyes darting under closed lids. People woken during REM usually reported vivid dreams.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1953", "l": "REM discovered"}, {"y": "1959", "l": "muscle paralysis shown"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "REM disorder described"}]}
### A paralysed body, a busy brain
During REM, brain activity looks a lot like being awake. A circuit in the brainstem switches off almost all voluntary muscles. The eye muscles and the diaphragm are spared, so you keep breathing and your eyes can move.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Still working", "items": ["eye muscles", "diaphragm", "heart"]}, "r": {"t": "Switched off", "items": ["arms and legs", "trunk muscles"]}}
### When the switch fails
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sleep paralysis", "b": "waking while still paralysed"}, {"a": "REM behaviour disorder", "b": "acting out dreams"}]}
Sleep paralysis is frightening but harmless and usually lasts seconds to a couple of minutes. In REM sleep behaviour disorder, the paralysis fails and people punch or kick in their sleep. It often appears years before Parkinson's disease, so doctors take it seriously.
### What is REM for?
REM seems to help with emotional memories, creativity and linking ideas, but its exact purpose is still debated.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of sleep spent in REM, %", "items": [{"l": "Newborns", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Adults", "v": 22.0}]}
!! You only dream in REM sleep => You can dream in any stage, but REM dreams are longer, stranger and more vivid
> In REM, the brain is wide awake while the body is switched off, which keeps your dreams safely in your head.

# The body clock
Your master clock is the **suprachiasmatic nucleus**, a cluster of about 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus, just above where the optic nerves cross. Nearly every organ also has its own clock; the master clock keeps them in step.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Morning light", "Special eye cells", "Master clock resets", "Hormones and temperature", "Organ clocks follow"]}
### Made of genes
Inside each clock cell, a set of genes switches on, builds proteins that slowly pile up, and those proteins then switch the genes off. One loop takes about 24 hours. Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young won the 2017 Nobel Prize for working out this mechanism.
### Not quite 24 hours
Left without daylight, most people's clock runs slightly long, about 24.2 hours. In 1962 the French caver Michel Siffre lived two months underground without a clock, and his days drifted. Morning light nudges the clock back each day. Light-sensitive cells in the retina, separate from rods and cones, report it.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "About 07:00", "b": "cortisol rising, alertness up"}, {"a": "Afternoon", "b": "a natural dip"}, {"a": "About 21:00", "b": "melatonin starts"}, {"a": "About 04:00", "b": "lowest body temperature"}]}
### Jet lag and time zones
The clock shifts by roughly an hour a day, so a long flight east takes days to recover from. Portugal uses Western European Time, while Spain, at a similar longitude, uses Central European Time, adopted in 1940. Its clocks run an hour ahead of the sun compared with Portugal, which is one reason for Spain's famously late dinners.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 24.2 h", "l": "natural clock period"}, {"v": "≈ 20,000", "l": "neurons in master clock"}]}
> Your body clock runs a little long each day, and morning light is what sets it back on time.

# Melatonin
Melatonin is made by the **pineal gland**, a pea-sized structure deep in the brain. The master clock tells it when to start: in dim light, levels usually begin rising about two hours before your normal bedtime, peak in the middle of the night and fall towards morning.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Evening darkness", "Master clock signals", "Pineal makes melatonin", "Body prepares for night"]}
### A darkness signal, not a sleeping pill
Melatonin tells your body that it is night. It doesn't knock you out. Nocturnal animals such as rats also make melatonin at night, when they are most active. In humans it lowers body temperature slightly and makes sleep easier to start.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Humans", "items": ["melatonin at night", "night = sleep"]}, "r": {"t": "Night animals", "items": ["melatonin at night", "night = active"]}}
### Light switches it off
Light-sensitive cells in the retina are most responsive to blue light. Bright light at night suppresses melatonin within minutes. Screens matter, but less than people think: in lab studies, reading on a backlit tablet before bed delayed melatonin and made sleep start about 10 minutes later. Bright overhead lights, exciting content and staying up later play a bigger role.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.0, "hi": 100000.0, "log": true, "unit": "light, lux", "items": [{"l": "Phone screen", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Room light", "v": 200.0}, {"l": "Cloudy day", "v": 10000.0, "hi": true}]}
Daylight is far brighter than any screen, even on a cloudy day. That is why morning outdoor light does the most to set your clock.
!! Melatonin is a natural sleeping pill => It is a timing signal; it helps most for shifting the clock, as in jet lag
> Melatonin is the body's announcement that night has arrived, and light cancels the announcement.

# Caffeine
While you're awake, brain cells use energy and release **adenosine**. It builds up through the day and binds to receptors that make you drowsy. This is called sleep pressure. Sleep clears it away.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Adenosine builds up", "Fits drowsiness receptors", "Caffeine blocks them", "You feel alert"]}
### A key in the wrong lock
Caffeine looks enough like adenosine to sit in its receptors without activating them. The adenosine keeps piling up behind the block. When caffeine wears off, it all hits at once: the afternoon "crash".
### Half-life
Caffeine peaks in your blood about 30–60 minutes after a drink. Your liver then removes about half of it every 5 hours or so, though this varies from about 2 to 10 hours between people. It is slower in pregnancy and faster in smokers.
ex: Two espressos at 15:00 ≈ 150 mg | 20:00 ≈ 75 mg left | 01:00 ≈ 40 mg still active
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "caffeine left, mg (example)", "items": [{"l": "15:00", "v": 150.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "20:00", "v": 75.0}, {"l": "01:00", "v": 38.0}]}
### Typical amounts
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Espresso (bica)", "b": "≈ 60–80 mg"}, {"a": "Mug of filter coffee", "b": "≈ 100 mg"}, {"a": "Cup of black tea", "b": "≈ 40–50 mg"}, {"a": "Can of cola", "b": "≈ 30–40 mg"}]}
In one 2013 study, caffeine taken 6 hours before bed still cut total sleep by about an hour. Caffeine also reduces deep sleep, even in people who fall asleep easily after coffee.
!! If I can fall asleep after coffee, it isn't affecting me => Caffeine can still make deep sleep shallower without delaying sleep
> Caffeine doesn't remove tiredness; it hides it, and the adenosine is waiting when it wears off.

# Sleep debt
When you sleep less than you need, the shortfall builds into a debt. The brain pays it back partly by forcing in **microsleeps**: lapses of a few seconds when you stop processing the world, often without noticing.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Too little sleep", "items": ["Attention lapses", "Worse mood", "Weaker memory", "Lower immunity"]}
### Like being drunk
A 1997 study in the journal Nature compared sleep loss with alcohol. After about 17 hours awake, performance matched a blood alcohol level of about 0.05%. After about 24 hours, it matched about 0.10%. Portugal's legal limit for most drivers is 0.5 g/l, which is 0.05%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "blood alcohol with similar impairment, %", "items": [{"l": "17 h awake", "v": 0.05, "t": "≈ 0.05"}, {"l": "24 h awake", "v": 0.1, "t": "≈ 0.10", "hi": true}, {"l": "PT legal limit", "v": 0.05}]}
### Chronic short sleep
In a 2003 US experiment, people who slept 6 hours a night for two weeks ended up as impaired on attention tests as people kept awake for one or two full nights. Their sense of sleepiness levelled off, so they thought they were fine.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "How they felt", "items": ["a bit tired", "then adapted"]}, "r": {"t": "How they did", "items": ["worse every day", "like 1–2 nights lost"]}}
### Paying it back
A lie-in or two can restore alertness after a short spell of poor sleep, but it doesn't fully undo weeks of short nights. In studies, people given only 4–5 hours also made fewer antibodies after a flu vaccine.
> Sleep debt builds quietly: the worse it gets, the less you notice how impaired you are.

# Larks and owls
Your **chronotype** is when your body naturally prefers to sleep. It's set partly by genes, including variants of clock genes such as PER3, and partly by age and light exposure. Most people sit somewhere between the extremes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Larks", "items": ["wake early easily", "peak in the morning"]}, "r": {"t": "Owls", "items": ["sleepy at 07:00", "peak in the evening"]}}
### How it shifts with age
Children tend to be early risers. In puberty, melatonin release shifts later and sleep pressure builds more slowly, so teenagers can't easily fall asleep early. The German researcher Till Roenneberg found that people are latest at about 20 and then slowly shift earlier. Older adults are often larks.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Child", "l": "early riser"}, {"y": "Teen", "l": "shifts later"}, {"y": "About 20", "l": "latest of all"}, {"y": "Over 60", "l": "early riser again"}]}
### Social jet lag
Owls forced onto early work or school times live as if flying a time zone or two east every Monday. Roenneberg called this **social jet lag**. It is linked to more tiredness, more caffeine and alcohol, and poorer health.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Weekday wake", "b": "07:00 for work"}, {"a": "Free-day wake", "b": "09:30, no alarm"}, {"a": "Social jet lag", "b": "about 2.5 hours"}]}
Some schools and countries have trialled later school start times. Teenagers then tend to sleep more, and studies often report better attendance and mood.
!! Teenagers who sleep late are lazy => Their body clock genuinely runs later; it is biology, not attitude
> Larks and owls are born and shaped, not made by willpower, and teenagers are owls for real biological reasons.

# Better sleep habits
These habits work by supporting the two systems that control sleep: the body clock, which sets the timing, and sleep pressure, which sets how tired you are.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Good sleep", "items": ["Clock: regular times", "Clock: morning light", "Pressure: daytime activity", "Calm, cool, dark room"]}
### The basics
• Get up at the same time every day, weekends included; bedtime will follow
• Get outdoor light within an hour or so of waking
• Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet; around 16–19 °C suits many people
• Stop caffeine by early afternoon, and keep alcohol low: it helps you nod off but fragments sleep later in the night
### Winding down
Your core temperature needs to drop about 1 °C to fall asleep. A warm shower or bath an hour or two before bed helps: it draws blood to the skin, which then sheds heat. Late, heavy dinners, common in Portugal and Spain, can make settling harder.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Warm bath", "Blood moves to skin", "Heat lost", "Core cools", "Sleep comes easier"]}
### If you can't sleep
Lying awake for a long time teaches the brain that bed is for worrying. Sleep specialists suggest getting up after about 20 minutes, doing something calm in dim light and returning when sleepy. For long-term insomnia, European guidelines recommend **cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia** (CBT-I) as the first treatment, before sleeping pills.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Stay in bed and try harder", "r": "Get up, reset, return when sleepy"}, {"w": "Lie in to catch up", "r": "Keep wake time fixed"}]}
!? Trying hard to sleep makes it harder. Some therapies even ask people to try to stay awake, which often helps them drop off.
> Fix your wake time, catch the morning light, and let sleep come to you rather than chasing it.

# The largest organ
An adult's skin covers about 1.5–2 m², roughly the size of a single bed sheet. The epidermis and dermis together weigh about 3–4 kg; count the fatty layer beneath and it's much more. Its thickness ranges from about 0.5 mm on the eyelids to over 4 mm on the back and the soles of the feet.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 2 m²", "l": "surface area"}, {"v": "≈ 3–4 kg", "l": "weight, without fat layer"}, {"v": "0.5–4 mm", "l": "thickness range"}]}
### Four big jobs
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Barrier", "b": "keeps germs out, water in"}, {"a": "Sensing", "b": "touch, pain, heat, cold"}, {"a": "Temperature", "b": "sweat and blood flow"}, {"a": "Vitamin D", "b": "made using UVB light"}]}
### The water seal
Without skin, you would dry out in hours. The outer layer of flattened dead cells, glued together with fats, works like a brick wall with mortar. That's why severe burns are so dangerous: patients lose fluid and heat fast and are open to infection.
### Making vitamin D
When UVB light hits the skin, it turns a cholesterol-like molecule into vitamin D, which the body needs to absorb calcium for bones. In winter, the sun in Portugal is too low for much UVB to get through, roughly from November to February. That's partly why low vitamin D is common in Portugal, despite its sunny reputation.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["UVB reaches skin", "Cholesterol relative", "Vitamin D made", "Liver and kidney activate", "Calcium absorbed"]}
!? Skin is an important part of the immune system. Special cells in the epidermis catch invaders and carry them to lymph nodes to show the immune system what to attack.
> Skin is a living, self-repairing barrier that also senses, cools and even makes a vitamin.

# Three layers
Each layer of skin is built for a different job, from the tough outer shield to the soft cushion underneath.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Epidermis: barrier, no vessels", "Dermis: collagen, nerves, glands", "Hypodermis: fat, cushion"]}
### The epidermis
The epidermis has no blood vessels; it gets food by diffusion from below. Its main cells, **keratinocytes**, are born at the bottom and move up, filling with the tough protein keratin and dying as they go. It also holds **melanocytes**, which make pigment, and immune cells that guard against germs.
### The dermis
The dermis is the thick, strong layer. A mesh of **collagen** gives it strength and **elastin** lets it spring back. It holds blood vessels, nerve endings, sweat glands, oil glands and the roots of hairs. Leather is animal dermis, preserved by tanning.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Collagen", "b": "strength, like rope"}, {"a": "Elastin", "b": "stretch and recoil"}, {"a": "Oil glands", "b": "soften skin and hair"}, {"a": "Nerve endings", "b": "touch, pain, heat"}]}
### The hypodermis
Below lies a layer of fat and loose tissue. It stores energy, insulates against cold and cushions knocks. It is thickest on the buttocks and belly and thinnest on the eyelids.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Young skin", "items": ["lots of collagen", "springy elastin"]}, "r": {"t": "Older skin", "items": ["less collagen", "worn elastin, wrinkles"]}}
!? Fingerprints form at the boundary between epidermis and dermis before birth, around weeks 10–17 of pregnancy. Even identical twins have different ones.
> Three layers, three jobs: shield on top, strength and services in the middle, padding below.

# Skin renews
The bottom of the epidermis is a sheet of stem cells that divide constantly. Each new cell takes about two weeks to reach the surface and spends roughly another two weeks as part of the dead outer layer before it flakes off. In total, the epidermis renews in about a month, a little longer as we age.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Born at the base", "Pushed upwards", "Fills with keratin", "Dies and flattens", "Flakes off"]}
### How much do you shed?
Estimates are rough, but you lose somewhere around 30,000–40,000 skin cells a minute, mostly invisible.
ex: About 35,000 cells per minute | × 60 × 24 | ≈ 50 million cells a day
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 35,000", "l": "cells shed per minute"}, {"v": "≈ 1 month", "l": "to renew the epidermis"}]}
### The dust question
Skin flakes are a real part of household dust, and they feed tiny **dust mites**, a major cause of allergies. But studies suggest much of the dust inside homes actually comes from outside: soil and street dirt carried in on shoes and through windows, plus fibres from clothes and furniture.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "House dust", "items": ["Outdoor soil, pollen", "Fabric fibres", "Skin flakes", "Pet hair, mites"]}
### When renewal speeds up
In **psoriasis**, an immune problem makes skin cells race to the surface in only a few days instead of weeks. They pile up as thick, scaly patches. Dandruff is also partly faster shedding on the scalp.

!! Most house dust is dead skin => Skin is part of it, but much indoor dust is tracked in from outside
> Your outer skin is a conveyor belt: new cells rise from below while the dead top layer quietly wears away.

# Sweat
Water absorbs a lot of heat when it evaporates. Evaporating 1 litre of sweat removes about 2,400 kJ, enough to heat about 7 litres of water from room temperature to boiling point. Sweat that drips off doesn't cool you at all; only evaporation does.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Body heats up", "Brain signals glands", "Sweat on skin", "Sweat evaporates", "Heat carried away"]}
### Two kinds of glands
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Eccrine", "items": ["all over the body", "watery, for cooling"]}, "r": {"t": "Apocrine", "items": ["armpits, groin", "oily, from puberty"]}}
Fresh sweat is almost odourless. Body odour comes from skin bacteria breaking down the oily sweat from apocrine glands.
### Built for endurance
Most furry mammals cool by panting, which can't work while galloping. Humans lost most body hair and gained millions of sweat glands, so we can keep cool while moving for hours. In 2004 the biologists Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman argued that endurance running shaped human evolution, perhaps for **persistence hunting**: chasing animals in the heat until they overheated.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2–4 million", "l": "sweat glands"}, {"v": "1–2 l", "l": "sweat an hour in hard exercise"}]}
### When sweating fails
In humid air, sweat can't evaporate well, so it stops cooling. That's why a humid 32 °C day can feel more dangerous than a dry 38 °C in the Alentejo. Heat waves in southern Europe now cause thousands of extra deaths each summer, especially among older people, who sweat less and feel thirst less.
!? Trained athletes can sweat over 2 litres an hour, and their sweat is less salty, because training teaches glands to keep more salt in.
> Sweat cools you by evaporating, and that simple trick makes humans some of the best endurance runners on Earth.

# Skin colour
Almost everyone has roughly the same number of pigment cells, **melanocytes**. Differences in skin colour come from how much melanin they make and which kind.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Eumelanin", "b": "brown-black, strong UV shield"}, {"a": "Pheomelanin", "b": "red-yellow, weaker shield"}]}
### A balancing act
Skin colour evolved to balance two needs. Strong sunlight destroys **folate**, a vitamin vital for healthy pregnancies and sperm, so dark skin protects it. But skin needs some UVB to make vitamin D, so where sunlight is weak, lighter skin is an advantage. The anthropologist Nina Jablonski developed this explanation from around 2000.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strong sun", "items": ["dark skin favoured", "protects folate"]}, "r": {"t": "Weak sun", "items": ["light skin favoured", "more vitamin D"]}}
### A recent change
Ancient DNA shows that very pale skin spread through Europe relatively recently, within roughly the last 8,000 years, after farming arrived. Many earlier European hunter-gatherers had dark skin and often blue eyes. Several separate gene changes for lighter skin also arose in East Asia.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "≈ 1.2 m yr", "l": "dark skin in Africa"}, {"y": "≈ 60,000", "l": "humans leave Africa"}, {"y": "≈ 8,000", "l": "pale skin spreads, Europe"}]}
### Tanning
UV damage tells melanocytes to make more melanin and pass it to nearby cells, where it forms caps over the nuclei. A tan builds over a few days and offers only modest protection, roughly like a low-SPF sunscreen.
!! A tan protects you from the sun => A tan is a sign of DNA damage and only blocks a little UV
> Skin colour is an evolutionary compromise between blocking too much sun and getting too little.

# Sun protection
Sunlight carries two kinds of UV that harm skin. **UVB** causes sunburn and most skin cancers. **UVA** goes deeper, ages the skin and also contributes to cancer. Both damage DNA directly, fusing neighbouring letters of the code together.

### What SPF means
SPF measures protection against UVB. It doesn't rise in a straight line: going from 30 to 50 adds only about one percentage point. In real life people apply about half the amount used in testing, so the actual protection is much lower than the label suggests.

The **UV index** forecasts strength of sunlight on a scale where 8 and above is very high. In Portuguese summers it often reaches 9–10. Because Portugal's clocks are set ahead of the sun, solar noon there falls around 13:30–14:00 in summer, so the most intense period is roughly 12:00–16:00.

### Best order of defence
• Shade, especially around midday
• Clothing, a wide-brimmed hat and UV-blocking sunglasses

# Hair
You are born with all your hair follicles, about 5 million, and you never grow new ones. Most produce fine, nearly invisible hair. Hair itself is dead keratin; the living part is the bulb at the root.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Growing: 2–7 years", "Transition: weeks", "Resting: ~3 months", "Shed and restart"]}
### The growth cycle
Each follicle runs its own cycle. At any moment about 85–90% of scalp hairs are growing. Because they're out of step, you lose hairs constantly but never all at once. Losing about 50–100 hairs a day is normal.
ex: About 1 cm a month | Growing phase of 6 years = 72 months | Maximum length ≈ 72 cm
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 5 million", "l": "hair follicles"}, {"v": "≈ 100,000", "l": "on the scalp"}, {"v": "50–100", "l": "hairs shed a day"}]}
### Why eyelashes stay short
The length of hair depends on how long its growing phase lasts. Scalp hair grows for years; eyebrows and eyelashes only for a few months, so they stop short.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Blonde", "b": "most hairs, ≈ 150,000"}, {"a": "Brown, black", "b": "≈ 100,000"}, {"a": "Red", "b": "fewest, ≈ 90,000, thicker"}]}
### Grey hair
Hair colour comes from melanocytes in the bulb. With age, their stem cells run out, so new hairs grow without pigment. Grey hair is a mix of white and coloured hairs.
!! Shaving makes hair grow back thicker => It only cuts a tapered tip, leaving a blunt end that feels coarser
> Hair grows in cycles from a fixed set of follicles, and the length of the growing phase decides how long it gets.

# Goosebumps
Each hair follicle has a tiny muscle attached, the **arrector pili**. When it contracts, it pulls the hair upright and puckers the skin around it into a bump. The signal comes from the sympathetic nervous system, the same system that releases adrenaline.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cold or strong emotion", "Sympathetic nerves fire", "Tiny muscles contract", "Hairs stand, skin bumps"]}
### In animals, it still works
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Birds, mammals", "b": "fluff up to trap warm air"}, {"a": "Cats", "b": "puff up to look bigger"}, {"a": "Porcupines", "b": "raise their quills"}]}
In humans, with so little body hair, goosebumps trap almost no heat and scare nobody. That's why they are usually called a vestigial reflex.
### Not entirely useless
In 2020 a team at Harvard led by Ya-Chieh Hsu found that the nerve and muscle behind goosebumps also help control hair follicle stem cells in mice. Long cold spells may prompt new hair growth through this route.
### Music and chills
Many people get goosebumps from moving music, a speech or a film. This **frisson** is linked to the brain's reward system releasing dopamine. Studies suggest more than half of people experience it.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Goosebump triggers", "items": ["Cold", "Fear", "Moving music", "Awe"]}
!? In Portuguese they're called *pele de galinha*, chicken skin. Many languages use a bird: goose in English, hen in French, chicken in Chinese.
> Goosebumps are a fur-fluffing reflex we kept after we lost the fur.

### Growth rates
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "growth, mm per month (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Fingernails", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Toenails", "v": 1.0}]}
ex: Fingernail about 15 mm long | 3 mm a month | ≈ 5–6 months to replace fully
Toenails take about 12–18 months to grow out, which is why fungal toenail infections take so long to clear. Nails grow faster in summer, when you're young, on your dominant hand and on longer fingers.
### What nails can tell you
Doctors look at nails for clues. A serious illness or high fever can briefly slow growth and leave a groove across every nail, called a **Beau's line**. By measuring how far it has grown out, you can estimate when the illness happened.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "White spots", "b": "usually minor knocks"}, {"a": "Groove across nails", "b": "past illness or fever"}, {"a": "Spoon-shaped", "b": "can signal low iron"}]}
!! White spots on nails mean you lack calcium => They are almost always small bumps to the nail matrix
!? Nails and hair don't keep growing after death. The skin dries and shrinks back, making them look longer.
> Nails are slowly growing keratin plates, and they keep a record of what your body has been through.

# Healing a cut
Wound healing runs in four overlapping stages, starting within seconds and finishing a year or more later.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Minutes", "l": "clot stops bleeding"}, {"y": "Days 1–4", "l": "inflammation, cleanup"}, {"y": "Weeks 1–3", "l": "new tissue fills gap"}, {"y": "Months", "l": "scar remodels"}]}
### Stop the bleeding
Blood vessels narrow, and **platelets** stick to the damaged edges. A protein called fibrin weaves a mesh through them, forming a clot. On the surface, the clot dries into a scab.
### Clean up
White blood cells arrive. Neutrophils eat bacteria first; then larger cells called **macrophages** clear debris and send signals that start the rebuilding. Redness, warmth and swelling in the first days are normal signs of this work.
### Rebuild
Cells called **fibroblasts** lay down new collagen, and new capillaries grow in, making pink, bumpy tissue. Some cells contract like muscle, pulling the edges together. Skin cells crawl across from the edges to close the surface.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Platelets plug", "Fibrin clot", "White cells clean", "Fibroblasts rebuild", "Scar remodels"]}
### Remodel
For months, the hastily laid collagen is reorganised. The scar fades and flattens but never fully matches normal skin. It has no hair or sweat glands.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "strength vs normal skin, %", "items": [{"l": "After 1 week", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "After 3 weeks", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Fully healed", "v": 80.0, "hi": true}]}
!? Wounds kept moist and covered heal faster than wounds left to dry out, as George Winter showed in 1962. That's why modern plasters and dressings trap moisture.
> Healing is a relay: clotting, cleaning, rebuilding and years of quiet remodelling.

== bo-hormones

# Chemical messages
In 1902 two British physiologists, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling, found that a chemical from the gut told the pancreas to release digestive juice, even with its nerves cut. They called it secretin. In 1905 Starling coined the word **hormone**, from the Greek for "to set in motion".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gland senses a need", "Hormone into blood", "Travels everywhere", "Fits target receptor", "Cell responds"]}
### Lock and key
A hormone only affects cells that have a matching **receptor**. Insulin reaches every tissue, but only cells with insulin receptors respond. The same hormone can even do different things in different tissues, depending on what the receptor is wired to.
### Two main chemical families
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Water-soluble", "items": ["insulin, adrenaline", "receptor on surface"]}, "r": {"t": "Fat-soluble", "items": ["steroids, thyroid", "receptor inside cell"]}}
Water-soluble hormones can't cross the fatty cell membrane, so they bind outside and trigger a chain of signals within. Fat-soluble ones slip through and usually switch genes on or off directly, so their effects take longer but last longer.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hormone", "b": "the key in the blood"}, {"a": "Receptor", "b": "the lock on the cell"}, {"a": "Target cell", "b": "the one with the lock"}]}
!? Hormones work at tiny concentrations, often just billionths of a gram per millilitre of blood. A small change in level can have big effects.
> Hormones are broadcast to the whole body, but only cells with the right receptor are listening.

# Nerves vs hormones
The body has two communication systems. The nervous system works like a phone call to one person. The hormone system works like a radio broadcast: slower, but heard everywhere and for longer.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Nerves", "items": ["electrical, wired", "milliseconds", "precise target"]}, "r": {"t": "Hormones", "items": ["chemical, via blood", "seconds to days", "whole body"]}}
### Speed in numbers
The fastest nerve fibres carry signals at up to about 120 m/s. Blood takes about a minute to make a full circuit of the body, and many hormones then act over minutes, hours or longer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "time to act (rough, seconds)", "items": [{"l": "Reflex (nerve)", "v": 0.05}, {"l": "Adrenaline", "v": 5.0, "t": "≈ 5"}, {"l": "Cortisol", "v": 1800.0, "t": "≈ 30 min", "hi": true}]}
### Where they meet
The two systems are tightly linked. The **hypothalamus**, at the base of the brain, is made of nerve cells but releases hormones that control the pituitary gland. It turns nerve signals, such as stress, cold or hunger, into hormone signals.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Senses and thoughts", "Hypothalamus", "Pituitary", "Glands round the body"]}
### Molecules with two jobs
Some chemicals work both ways. Noradrenaline is a neurotransmitter between nerve cells and also a hormone from the adrenal glands. Oxytocin is a brain messenger and a hormone that triggers labour contractions and milk release.
> Nerves are for speed and precision; hormones are for reach and duration, and the brain controls both.

# The glands
Endocrine glands release their products into the blood rather than through a tube. Most are small. The pituitary weighs well under 1 g, yet it influences growth, metabolism, stress, reproduction and water balance.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Pituitary", "b": "controls other glands"}, {"a": "Thyroid", "b": "metabolic rate"}, {"a": "Adrenals", "b": "adrenaline, cortisol"}, {"a": "Pancreas", "b": "insulin, glucagon"}]}
### The chain of command
The pituitary is called the "master gland", but it takes orders from the hypothalamus just above it. Together they run several hormone chains, or **axes**.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Hypothalamus: the boss", "Pituitary: middle manager", "Thyroid, adrenals, gonads", "Every tissue"]}
### More glands
• The **pineal** gland makes melatonin at night
• Four tiny **parathyroids** behind the thyroid control calcium
• **Ovaries** make oestrogen and progesterone; **testes** make testosterone
### Surprise hormone makers
Many organs that aren't called glands also make hormones. Fat releases leptin; the stomach releases ghrelin; the kidneys make a hormone that tells bone marrow to produce red blood cells; and the heart releases a hormone that lowers blood pressure.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Hidden glands", "items": ["Fat: leptin", "Stomach: ghrelin", "Kidney: EPO", "Heart: ANP"]}
!? The kidney hormone EPO is made as a medicine for anaemia, and was also the notorious doping drug in 1990s cycling.
> Hormones come from a network of small glands commanded from the brain, plus organs you'd never think of.

# Insulin
Your blood holds only about 4–5 g of sugar in total, roughly a teaspoon, yet the brain depends on it every second. Keeping that level steady is the job of two hormones from the **islets of Langerhans**, small clusters of cells that make up only about 1–2% of the pancreas.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Insulin", "items": ["after meals", "sugar into cells", "stored as glycogen"]}, "r": {"t": "Glucagon", "items": ["when fasting", "liver releases sugar", "level rises"]}}
### How insulin works
Insulin binds receptors on muscle, fat and liver cells. In muscle and fat it brings glucose transporters to the cell surface, opening doors for sugar. In the liver it switches on storage as **glycogen** and, when there's a surplus, conversion to fat.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Meal: sugar rises", "Insulin released", "Cells take up sugar", "Sugar falls, glucagon"]}
### Diabetes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Type 1", "b": "immune attack, no insulin"}, {"a": "Type 2", "b": "cells resist insulin"}]}
Type 1 usually starts in childhood and needs insulin from the start. Type 2 is far more common and is strongly linked to weight, inactivity and age. Portugal has one of the highest rates in Europe, with roughly 1 in 7 adults aged 20–79 affected, many of them undiagnosed.
### A Canadian discovery
In 1921 Frederick Banting and Charles Best extracted insulin in Toronto. In January 1922 a 14-year-old boy, Leonard Thompson, became the first person treated. Before then, type 1 diabetes was a death sentence within months or a few years.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1921", "l": "insulin extracted"}, {"y": "1922", "l": "first patient treated"}, {"y": "1923", "l": "Nobel Prize"}, {"y": "1978", "l": "made by bacteria"}]}
!? Lisbon's diabetes association, the APDP, was founded in 1926 by the doctor Ernesto Roma and is often described as the oldest in the world.
> Insulin opens the doors for sugar after a meal; glucagon opens the larder when you're running low.

# Adrenaline
Adrenaline (also called epinephrine) comes from the inner part of the adrenal glands, which sit on top of the kidneys. In 1901 the Japanese chemist Jokichi Takamine purified it, the first hormone ever isolated. In 1915 the American physiologist Walter Cannon described its role in the **fight-or-flight** response.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Threat seen", "Brain alarm", "Nerves to adrenals", "Adrenaline released", "Body ready in seconds"]}
### What it does
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Heart", "b": "beats faster and harder"}, {"a": "Airways", "b": "widen for more air"}, {"a": "Liver", "b": "releases sugar"}, {"a": "Gut", "b": "slows down"}]}
Blood is diverted from skin and gut to muscles, which is why people can go pale with fear. Pupils widen, and pain is dulled. All of it helps you fight, run or freeze in place.
### Fast on, fast off
Adrenaline is broken down within a few minutes, so the surge is brief. The shaky, jittery feeling after a near miss on the road is its after-effect.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Adrenaline", "items": ["seconds", "short emergencies"]}, "r": {"t": "Cortisol", "items": ["minutes to hours", "longer stress"]}}
### A life-saving medicine
In **anaphylaxis**, a severe allergic reaction, airways swell and blood pressure crashes. An injection of adrenaline reverses it fast. That's why people with severe allergies carry auto-injector pens.
!! Adrenaline gives people superhuman strength => It does boost effort and dull pain, but stories of lifting cars are unverified and probably exaggerated
> Adrenaline is the body's emergency switch: seconds to turn on, minutes to wear off.

# Cortisol
Cortisol is made in the outer layer of the adrenal glands. It's controlled by a chain of three signals, called the **HPA axis**: the hypothalamus prompts the pituitary, which prompts the adrenals.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hypothalamus: CRH", "Pituitary: ACTH", "Adrenals: cortisol", "Cortisol damps the loop"]}
### What it does
Cortisol makes energy available: it raises blood sugar, frees up fat and protein for fuel and helps maintain blood pressure. It also reins in inflammation and the immune system, so the response to stress doesn't run out of control.
### A daily rhythm
Cortisol is lowest around midnight. It climbs in the early morning and jumps by roughly half in the first 30–45 minutes after waking, helping you get going. Then it falls through the day.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "cortisol level (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Midnight", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Waking", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "30 min later", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Afternoon", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Evening", "v": 20.0}]}
### Too much for too long
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Short bursts", "items": ["fuel for action", "calm inflammation"]}, "r": {"t": "Chronically high", "items": ["poor sleep", "weaker immunity", "belly fat"]}}
In 1948 the American doctor Philip Hench gave cortisone, a close relative, to patients with severe arthritis, with dramatic results. Steroid medicines based on cortisol now treat asthma, allergies, eczema and many inflammatory diseases.
!! Cortisol is just the "stress hormone" and is bad for you => You can't live without it; problems come from levels that stay high or too low
> Cortisol is the slow, steady stress hormone: it fuels you and calms inflammation, but it's built for bursts, not for months.

# Thyroid
The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland of about 20 g at the front of the neck, below the Adam's apple. It makes two hormones named after how many iodine atoms they carry: **T4** (four) and **T3** (three). T4 is mostly converted to the more active T3 in the tissues.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "T4 → T3", "items": [{"s": "T4", "m": "thyroxine, 4 iodine"}, {"s": "T3", "m": "active form, 3 iodine"}, {"s": "TSH", "m": "pituitary's control"}]}
### Too fast, too slow
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Overactive", "items": ["weight loss, heat", "fast heartbeat"]}, "r": {"t": "Underactive", "items": ["tiredness, cold", "weight gain"]}}
Both are fairly common, especially in women, and are often caused by the immune system attacking the gland. A simple blood test of TSH, the pituitary's thyroid-stimulating hormone, is usually the first check.
### Why iodine matters
The body can't make iodine; it comes from seafood, dairy, eggs and iodised salt. Without enough, the thyroid swells into a **goitre**. In pregnancy, a shortage can harm the baby's brain development. Iodine deficiency is the world's leading preventable cause of intellectual disability.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1922", "l": "Swiss iodised salt"}, {"y": "1924", "l": "iodised salt in US"}, {"y": "1990s", "l": "WHO global push"}, {"y": "2013", "l": "PT pregnancy advice"}]}
Surveys in the 2000s found many pregnant women in Portugal had low iodine. Since 2013, Portugal's health authority, the DGS, has advised iodine supplements before and during pregnancy and while breastfeeding.
!? Before iodised salt, goitres were so common in some Alpine valleys that they were considered normal.
> The thyroid sets the body's pace, and it needs a steady trickle of iodine to do the job.

# Growth hormone
Growth hormone is made by the pituitary in pulses, with the biggest one usually in the first deep sleep of the night. Much of its effect on growth works indirectly: it prompts the liver to make **IGF-1**, which tells cartilage at the ends of bones to grow.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Deep sleep", "Pituitary pulse", "Liver makes IGF-1", "Bones and muscle grow"]}
### Childhood and adulthood
Children grow in length at growth plates near the ends of the long bones. After puberty, sex hormones close these plates, so height stops increasing. Growth hormone carries on in adults at lower levels, helping maintain muscle, bone and fat balance.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "growth hormone release (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Child", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Teenager", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Age 40", "v": 35.0}, {"l": "Age 70", "v": 15.0}]}
### Too much or too little
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Too much in childhood", "b": "gigantism"}, {"a": "Too much in adults", "b": "acromegaly: big hands, jaw"}, {"a": "Too little in childhood", "b": "short stature, treatable"}]}
The tallest person on record, the American Robert Wadlow, reached 2.72 m because of a pituitary tumour. He died in 1940, aged 22.
### A medical lesson
Until 1985, growth hormone for treatment was extracted from the pituitaries of people who had died. Some batches carried prions and caused the fatal brain disease Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease years later. Since 1985 it has been made safely by genetically engineered bacteria.
!! Growth hormone keeps adults young => Its use for anti-ageing is unproven and can cause side effects such as joint pain and diabetes
> Growth hormone is released mainly in deep sleep; in children it builds the body, in adults it helps maintain it.

# Hunger hormones
Appetite is controlled by signals on several timescales. Fast ones from the gut say "hungry" or "full" around meals. A slow one from fat reports on long-term energy stores. The hypothalamus weighs them all up.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ghrelin", "items": ["from the stomach", "rises before meals", "says: eat"]}, "r": {"t": "Leptin", "items": ["from fat tissue", "tracks fat stores", "says: enough"]}}
### Discoveries
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1994", "l": "leptin discovered"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "ghrelin discovered"}, {"y": "2005", "l": "first GLP-1 drug"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "first for obesity"}]}
Jeffrey Friedman found leptin in 1994 by studying mice that were enormously obese because they couldn't make it. Hopes of a leptin cure for obesity faded quickly: most people with obesity have plenty of leptin, but the brain stops responding to it, called **leptin resistance**.
### Fullness signals
After eating, the gut releases hormones such as **GLP-1** that slow the stomach and signal fullness. Drugs that mimic GLP-1, such as semaglutide, were first developed for type 2 diabetes. Several are now also approved for treating obesity, as they reduce appetite.
### Sleep and hunger
In short-sleep experiments, ghrelin goes up and leptin goes down, and people report more hunger, especially for sugary and starchy food.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Short sleep", "Ghrelin up", "Leptin down", "Hungrier next day"]}
!! Hunger is just willpower => It's driven by hormones that the brain can't simply ignore
> Ghrelin says "eat now", leptin says "we have enough stored", and the brain tries to balance the two.

# Feedback loops
Hormone levels are kept in a narrow range by **negative feedback**: the output of a process switches the process down. A home thermostat does the same: when the room is warm enough, it turns off the boiler.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Level falls", "Gland releases more", "Level rises", "Gland slows down"]}
### A worked example
The thyroid runs a three-step loop. If thyroid hormone drops, the hypothalamus and pituitary sense it and raise their signals. When levels recover, they ease off.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hypothalamus: TRH", "Pituitary: TSH", "Thyroid: T4, T3", "High T4 brakes both"]}
That's why doctors test TSH: a high TSH usually means the thyroid is underactive and the pituitary is shouting at it.
### Positive feedback: the exceptions
Occasionally the body needs a process to build to a climax. In **positive feedback**, the output pushes the process further.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Negative", "items": ["keeps things steady", "e.g. blood sugar"]}, "r": {"t": "Positive", "items": ["builds to a peak", "e.g. childbirth"]}}
During labour, contractions push the baby against the cervix, which triggers oxytocin, which strengthens the contractions, until the baby is born.
### Homeostasis
In the 19th century the French physiologist Claude Bernard described the body's stable "inner environment". In 1926 Walter Cannon named the process **homeostasis**. Feedback loops are its main tool.
!? Taking steroid hormones, such as anabolic steroids, can shut down the body's own production through feedback. That's why stopping them suddenly can be dangerous.
> Most hormones regulate themselves like a thermostat: too much switches production off, too little switches it on.

== bo-kidneys

# Two filters
Your kidneys sit high at the back of your abdomen, either side of the spine, partly sheltered by the lower ribs. Each is about 11–12 cm long and weighs roughly 150 g, yet together they receive about a fifth of all the blood your heart pumps.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "180 L", "l": "plasma filtered a day"}, {"v": "1–2 L", "l": "urine made a day"}, {"v": "~20%", "l": "of the heart's output"}]}
### Filter first, then take back
The kidneys don't pick out waste one molecule at a time. They push a huge volume of plasma (the liquid part of blood) through a filter, then reclaim almost everything useful. Your whole plasma volume, about 3 litres, passes through them around 60 times a day.
ex: Filtered per day: about 180 L | Leaves as urine: about 1.5 L | Share kept: about 99%
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Blood enters kidney", "Plasma squeezed through filter", "Useful parts reabsorbed", "Waste drains as urine"]}
This design is wasteful-looking but clever: anything the body hasn't specifically chosen to keep gets cleared, including new toxins and drugs the body has never met before.
!? The right kidney usually sits slightly lower than the left, because the large liver sits above it.
> Your kidneys clean your blood by filtering almost all of it and then taking back what you need, about 99% of the fluid.

# Nephrons
A nephron is a microscopic tube with a filter at one end. Each kidney has roughly a million of them, though the number varies a lot between people, from about 200,000 to over 2 million.
### Two parts doing two jobs
• **Glomerulus**: a tangled ball of tiny capillaries where blood pressure forces water and small molecules out
• **Tubule**: a long, looping tube where cells pull back glucose, salt, water and other useful substances
• **Collecting duct**: the final stretch, where hormones fine-tune how much water is kept
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Glomerulus filters", "Tubule reabsorbs", "Loop of Henle saves water", "Collecting duct fine-tunes"]}
The filter works by size and charge. Blood cells and most proteins are too big to pass, which is why protein in the urine can be an early sign of kidney damage.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Passes the filter", "items": ["water", "salt and glucose", "urea"]}, "r": {"t": "Stays in blood", "items": ["red blood cells", "most proteins", "platelets"]}}
### A slow decline
You can't grow new nephrons. From about age 40, people lose a small fraction each year, but there is plenty of spare capacity, so most people never notice. Doctors track kidney function with a blood test called eGFR, an estimate of how much the glomeruli filter each minute.
!? Laid end to end, the tubules of both kidneys would stretch for tens of kilometres.
> A million tiny filters per kidney do the work: filter by size, then reclaim by choice.

# What kidneys remove
Your cells constantly make waste, and the kidneys keep it from building up. Without working kidneys, waste and potassium would reach dangerous levels within days.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Urea", "b": "from breaking down protein"}, {"a": "Creatinine", "b": "from muscle wear and tear"}, {"a": "Extra salt and potassium", "b": "keeps nerves and heart steady"}, {"a": "Acid", "b": "keeps blood pH near 7.4"}]}
### Why urea?
When you digest protein, the nitrogen in it ends up as ammonia, which is toxic. The liver converts it into urea, which is much safer, and the kidneys flush it out. Doctors measure creatinine in the blood because its level rises when the kidneys slow down.
### Medicines too
Many drugs, including some antibiotics, are cleared mainly by the kidneys. That's why doses are often lowered for people with weak kidney function, and why some painkillers like ibuprofen are used cautiously in kidney disease.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 7.0, "hi": 7.8, "unit": "blood pH", "items": [{"l": "Danger (acid)", "v": 7.1}, {"l": "Normal", "v": 7.4, "hi": true}, {"l": "Danger (alkaline)", "v": 7.7}]}
When kidneys fail, dialysis takes over by passing blood through an artificial filter, usually three times a week for about four hours. In Portugal, around 13,000 people are on dialysis.
!! Kidneys simply dump waste => They balance salt, potassium, acid and water minute by minute
> Kidneys are less a rubbish bin than a chemist, constantly correcting the recipe of your blood.

# Water balance
Your body is about 60% water, and the kidneys keep that amount surprisingly steady. The key messenger is **antidiuretic hormone** (ADH, also called vasopressin), released from the pituitary gland at the base of the brain.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Too little water", "Blood gets saltier", "Brain releases ADH", "Kidneys save water"]}
### How the switch works
Sensors in the brain detect when blood becomes too concentrated, even by 1–2%. ADH then tells the collecting ducts to insert water channels called aquaporins into their walls. Water flows back into the blood, and the urine left behind is small in volume and dark.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Dehydrated", "items": ["lots of ADH", "little, dark urine", "thirst"]}, "r": {"t": "Well hydrated", "items": ["little ADH", "more, pale urine", "no thirst"]}}
### Alcohol and caffeine
Alcohol blocks ADH, which is why a night of drinking means many trips to the toilet and a dry, headachy morning. Caffeine has a mild effect, but regular coffee drinkers adapt, so coffee still counts towards daily fluid.
!! Coffee dehydrates you => In normal amounts it adds to your fluid intake
How much should you drink? It varies with heat, activity and food. European guidance suggests about 2 litres of fluid a day for women and 2.5 litres for men, including water from food, which supplies roughly a fifth.
> Thirst and ADH work together so you rarely need to count glasses: your brain keeps watch.

# Urine colour guide
Urine gets its yellow colour from **urobilin**, a pigment made when old red blood cells are broken down. The more water in your urine, the more diluted that pigment, and the paler the colour.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Clear: maybe drinking more than needed", "Pale straw: well hydrated", "Dark yellow: drink some water", "Amber or honey: likely dehydrated", "Brown or red: see a doctor"]}
### Other things that change colour
• B vitamins, especially riboflavin (B2), can turn urine bright neon yellow
• Beetroot can make it pink or red in some people
• Some medicines turn it orange, green or blue
• Asparagus doesn't change colour but famously changes smell, and not everyone can detect it
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Neon yellow", "b": "B vitamin supplements"}, {"a": "Pink or red", "b": "beetroot, or blood"}, {"a": "Orange", "b": "some drugs, or dehydration"}, {"a": "Foamy", "b": "possible protein, check"}]}
Colour is a rough guide, not a test. First urine of the morning is naturally darker because you haven't drunk overnight. Red urine with no beetroot to blame, or persistently foamy urine, deserves a medical check.
!? Doctors in ancient times and the Middle Ages examined urine in a glass flask, called a matula, and sometimes tasted it: sweet urine was a sign of diabetes.
> Aim for pale straw most of the day; a darker morning sample is normal.

# Blood pressure control
The kidneys act on blood pressure in two ways: by changing how much fluid is in your blood vessels, and by releasing hormones that change how tight those vessels are.
### The renin system
When the kidneys sense low blood pressure or low salt, they release an enzyme called **renin**. It sets off a chain that ends with angiotensin II, a powerful vessel-tightener, and aldosterone, a hormone that makes the kidneys keep salt and water.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Kidney senses low pressure", "Releases renin", "Angiotensin II forms", "Vessels tighten, salt kept", "Pressure rises"]}
### Salt and water
Where salt goes, water follows. Eating a lot of salt makes the body hold extra fluid, which raises blood volume and pressure. In some people, especially as they age, this effect is strong.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Raises pressure", "items": ["angiotensin II", "aldosterone", "high salt diet"]}, "r": {"t": "Lowers pressure", "items": ["ACE inhibitors", "diuretics", "less salt"]}}
Many common blood pressure pills target exactly this system. ACE inhibitors block the formation of angiotensin II; diuretics (water pills) make the kidneys release more salt and water.
!? ACE inhibitors were developed from a peptide found in the venom of a Brazilian pit viper, whose bite makes blood pressure crash.
The link runs both ways: high blood pressure damages the delicate kidney filters, and damaged kidneys raise blood pressure further. Along with diabetes, it is a leading cause of kidney failure in Europe.
> Your kidneys are a blood pressure control centre, which is why many pressure medicines work on them.

# More jobs
Kidneys are also hormone factories. Two of their products matter for almost everyone.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Kidney hormones", "items": ["EPO: more red cells", "Active vitamin D: bones", "Renin: blood pressure"]}
### EPO and red blood cells
When kidney cells sense low oxygen, they release **erythropoietin** (EPO). It travels to the bone marrow and tells it to make more red blood cells. This is why people living at altitude have more red cells, and why people with kidney failure often become anaemic.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Kidney senses low oxygen", "Releases EPO", "Bone marrow makes red cells", "Blood carries more oxygen"]}
Synthetic EPO, available since the late 1980s, transformed life for dialysis patients. It was also abused in endurance sport, notably cycling, to boost oxygen supply, which led to tests and bans.
### Vitamin D activation
The vitamin D made in your skin or eaten in food is inactive. The liver changes it once, and the kidneys complete the job, producing the active form that helps your gut absorb calcium. Kidney disease can therefore weaken bones.
!? Kidneys also help make glucose during long fasts, producing a meaningful share of the body's sugar overnight.
> The kidneys quietly influence your blood, your bones and your blood pressure, not just your urine.

# Kidney stones
A kidney stone forms when minerals in urine become so concentrated that they crystallise. Most stones, about 80%, are made of calcium oxalate. Others are uric acid or calcium phosphate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Concentrated urine", "Minerals crystallise", "Crystals grow into stone", "Stone moves down ureter"]}
### Why they hurt so much
The ureter, the tube from kidney to bladder, is only a few millimetres wide. When a stone gets stuck, the muscle in its wall squeezes hard to push it along, and urine backs up. The pain, called renal colic, comes in waves and is often described as one of the worst pains people experience.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "chance of passing on its own (approx. %)", "items": [{"l": "Under 5 mm", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "5–10 mm", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Over 10 mm", "v": 10.0}]}
### Prevention
• Drink enough to keep urine pale; this is the single biggest factor
• Cut down on salt, which pushes calcium into urine
• Don't avoid calcium foods: dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut
• Hot summers raise risk, as you lose more water through sweat
!! People with calcium stones should avoid calcium => Normal dietary calcium actually lowers stone risk
About one in ten people will have a kidney stone at some point, and stones often come back. Larger stones can be broken up with shock waves or lasers.
> Most stones start with concentrated urine, so water is the cheapest prevention there is.

# Living with one kidney
When one kidney is removed, the other grows a little and each of its nephrons works harder. Within weeks it typically reaches about 70–80% of the original combined filtering power, which is plenty for normal life.

### The first transplant
In December 1954, in Boston, surgeon Joseph Murray transplanted a kidney between identical twins, Ronald and Richard Herrick. Because the twins were genetically identical, the body didn't reject it. Richard lived for eight more years. Murray later won a Nobel Prize.

### Donation today
A living donor gives one kidney, often to a relative, and usually leads a normal life afterwards. Kidneys from living donors tend to last longer than those from deceased donors. In Portugal, everyone is a potential organ donor after death unless they register to opt out, a system called presumed consent.
!? Some people are born with only one kidney and never find out until a scan for something else.
> Your body carries a spare kidney's worth of capacity, which is what makes living donation possible.

# The bladder
The bladder is a hollow, stretchy bag of muscle. Its wall, the detrusor, is folded when empty and smooths out as it fills, a bit like a balloon.

Two rings of muscle, called sphincters, keep the exit closed. The inner one works automatically; the outer one is under your control. When stretch sensors report filling, the brain lets you postpone the urge until a convenient moment.

== bo-muscles

# How muscles contract
Each muscle fibre is packed with long threads of two proteins: thick **myosin** and thin **actin**. They are arranged in repeating units called sarcomeres, which give skeletal muscle its striped look under a microscope.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Myosin head grabs actin", "Head pivots, pulls", "ATP releases the head", "Head resets, grabs again"]}
### The sliding filament
Myosin heads act like oars. Each grabs actin, pulls it a few nanometres, lets go and reaches again. The filaments don't shrink; they slide over each other, so the sarcomere shortens. Thousands of sarcomeres in a row add up to visible movement.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nerve signal arrives", "Calcium floods the fibre", "Myosin binds actin", "Filaments slide, muscle shortens"]}
### Energy and release
Every stroke needs ATP, the cell's energy currency. ATP is also needed for myosin to let go. After death, ATP runs out and the heads stay locked on, which is why bodies stiffen in rigor mortis.
!? You have about 600 skeletal muscles, and they make up roughly 30–40% of an adult's body weight.
Muscles can only pull, never push. That's why they work in pairs: the biceps bends your elbow, the triceps straightens it.
> A muscle contraction is billions of tiny molecular oars rowing in step, powered by ATP.

# Fast and slow fibres
Muscles contain a mix of fibre types. They differ in the speed of their myosin, their blood supply and how they make energy.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Slow-twitch", "items": ["red, many mitochondria", "burn fat and oxygen", "tire slowly"]}, "r": {"t": "Fast-twitch", "items": ["pale, fewer mitochondria", "burn sugar fast", "tire quickly"]}}
### Dark meat, white meat
You can see the difference in poultry. Chicken legs, used for standing all day, are dark because they are rich in slow fibres and myoglobin, an oxygen-storing protein. Chicken breast, used for brief flapping bursts, is white and mostly fast fibres. Migratory ducks, which fly for hours, have dark breast meat.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "slow fibres in leg muscle, % (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Sprinter", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Average person", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Marathon runner", "v": 80.0, "hi": true}]}
### Born or made?
Your mix is largely set by genes, which is one reason some people are naturally faster or more enduring. Training can shift fast fibres between subtypes and makes all fibres better at their job, but turning fast into slow, or the reverse, is limited.
The body recruits fibres in order: slow ones first for gentle effort, then fast ones as force increases. A stroll uses mostly slow fibres; a jump calls on everything.
> Your fibre mix helps decide whether you're built more for the sprint or the marathon.

# Energy systems
Muscles need ATP for every contraction, but they store only enough for a couple of seconds. Three systems refill it, overlapping rather than switching on and off.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "main energy source by effort length", "items": [{"l": "Phosphocreatine", "v": 10.0, "t": "~10 s"}, {"l": "Anaerobic sugar", "v": 60.0, "t": "~1–2 min"}, {"l": "Aerobic", "v": 120.0, "t": "hours", "hi": true}]}
### Three gears
• **Phosphocreatine**: an instant reserve that recharges ATP; powers a 100 m sprint or a heavy lift
• **Anaerobic glycolysis**: splits glucose without oxygen; fast but limited, producing lactate
• **Aerobic**: burns sugar and fat with oxygen in mitochondria; slow to ramp up but almost unlimited
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Anaerobic", "items": ["fast power", "little ATP per sugar", "short efforts"]}, "r": {"t": "Aerobic", "items": ["slower power", "~15× more ATP", "long efforts"]}}
### Lactate is fuel
Lactate was long seen as waste. We now know the heart and other muscles burn it, and the liver turns it back into glucose. The burning feeling in hard efforts comes mainly from acid and other metabolites building up, not lactate itself.
!! Lactic acid is a waste product => Lactate is recycled as fuel by heart, muscles and liver
Training improves all three systems. Endurance work, especially, multiplies mitochondria so the aerobic gear kicks in earlier and handles more.
> Muscles shift through three energy gears, and training makes each one bigger.

# Why muscles grow
Muscle growth, or **hypertrophy**, happens mostly by making existing fibres thicker, not by adding new ones. Each fibre builds more actin and myosin and so can pull harder.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Training stresses fibres", "Signals turn on building", "Protein added during rest", "Stronger fibre, new stress"]}
### What triggers it
Three things seem to drive growth: mechanical tension from heavy or effortful work, metabolic stress, and some fibre damage. The most consistent factor is working muscles close to fatigue. Lifting lighter weights can build similar muscle, as long as sets are taken near failure.
### The early gains are in the brain
In the first weeks of strength training, people get stronger before their muscles visibly grow. The nervous system learns to recruit more fibres, fire them together and quiet opposing muscles.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "source of strength gain (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Weeks 1–6: nerves", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Weeks 1–6: muscle", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Months later: muscle", "v": 70.0}]}
### What helps recovery
• Enough protein across the day, from varied foods
• Sleep, when growth hormone and repair peak
• Rest days between hard sessions for the same muscles
!! Lifting makes women bulky => Big gains need years of focused effort and high testosterone
!? Muscle cells are unusual: each fibre has many nuclei, and training adds more from stem cells called satellite cells.
> Muscles grow during recovery, not during the workout itself.

# Use it or lose it
Muscle is costly to maintain, so the body breaks it down quickly when it seems unnecessary. This is called **atrophy**.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~1%", "l": "muscle lost per day in bed"}, {"v": "3–8%", "l": "lost per decade after 30"}, {"v": "faster", "l": "loss after about 60"}]}
### Bed rest and space
Studies of healthy volunteers show that complete bed rest can cost noticeable leg strength in a single week. Astronauts on the International Space Station exercise around two hours a day to slow muscle and bone loss in weightlessness.
### Sarcopenia
Age-related loss of muscle mass and strength is called **sarcopenia**. It's driven by fewer nerve connections to fibres, especially fast ones, hormonal changes and inactivity. It matters because weak legs lead to falls, and falls are a major cause of injury in older people.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Speeds loss", "items": ["sitting a lot", "illness and bed rest", "low protein"]}, "r": {"t": "Slows loss", "items": ["strength training", "walking daily", "enough protein"]}}
### Never too late
Strength training works at any age. Studies with people in their 80s and 90s found substantial strength gains within weeks, improving walking and balance.
!? Muscle memory is real: fibres keep extra nuclei after training, so regaining lost muscle is faster than building it the first time.
> Muscle follows demand: ask it to work and it stays, stop and it quietly leaves.

# Cardio benefits
Aerobic exercise is any steady activity that raises your heart rate and breathing for a sustained time: brisk walking, cycling, swimming, dancing.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Regular cardio", "items": ["Stronger heart", "Lower blood pressure", "Better mood and sleep", "Better blood sugar"]}
### A more efficient heart
With training, the heart muscle gets stronger and pumps more blood per beat. So it needs fewer beats at rest. Many untrained adults have a resting rate of 70–80 beats per minute; trained endurance athletes can be in the 40s.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "resting heart rate, beats per minute (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Untrained adult", "v": 75.0}, {"l": "Regular exerciser", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Endurance athlete", "v": 45.0, "hi": true}]}
### Beyond the heart
• Blood pressure falls on average by a few points, similar to some medicines
• Muscles take up glucose better, lowering type 2 diabetes risk
• The brain releases endorphins and other chemicals; exercise reduces symptoms of depression
• Sleep tends to deepen, especially with exercise earlier in the day
!? Regular moderate exercise is linked to roughly 20–30% lower risk of dying early, compared with being inactive.
You don't need to run marathons. The biggest gains come from going from nothing to something; benefits keep growing with more activity but level off.
> The first steps out of inactivity bring the biggest health returns of all.

# Activity guidelines
The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines are the reference across Europe. They count all movement, not just sport.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Moderate activity", "b": "150–300 min a week"}, {"a": "Or vigorous activity", "b": "75–150 min a week"}, {"a": "Strength work", "b": "2+ days a week"}, {"a": "Over 65s", "b": "add balance training"}]}
### Moderate or vigorous?
A simple test: during moderate activity you can talk but not sing. During vigorous activity you can only say a few words before pausing for breath. One minute of vigorous counts roughly as two of moderate.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Moderate", "items": ["brisk walking", "easy cycling", "gardening"]}, "r": {"t": "Vigorous", "items": ["running", "fast cycling", "football"]}}
ex: 150 minutes a week | ÷ 5 days | = 30 minutes a day
### Sitting less
The WHO also advises cutting down sedentary time and breaking up long periods of sitting. Long stretches of sitting carry risks even for people who exercise.
!? According to Eurobarometer surveys, Portugal is among the EU countries with the highest share of people who say they never exercise or play sport.
> Any activity is better than none, and more is better, up to a point.

# Stretching and warm-up
A warm-up does exactly what it says: muscles work better warm. Nerve signals travel faster, muscles contract more forcefully and joints move more freely once their lubricating fluid warms up.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Light cardio, 5 min", "Dynamic moves", "Sport-specific drills", "Start main activity"]}
### Dynamic beats static before exercise
**Dynamic stretching** moves joints through their range: leg swings, lunges, arm circles. **Static stretching** holds a position for 30 seconds or more. Long static holds just before sprinting or jumping can slightly reduce power for a short time, so they fit better after exercise or in separate sessions.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Dynamic", "items": ["before activity", "leg swings, lunges", "keeps power"]}, "r": {"t": "Static", "items": ["after or separately", "long holds", "improves range"]}}
### Does stretching prevent injury?
The evidence is mixed. Stretching on its own doesn't reliably prevent injuries. Structured warm-ups that include strength and balance work do. FIFA's "11+" programme, used by amateur football teams, cut injuries by around a third in trials.
!! Stretching prevents muscle soreness => Studies find stretching barely changes next-day soreness
Flexibility does improve with regular stretching, which is valuable for activities like dance and for keeping range of movement with age.
> Warm up by moving, not by holding still.

# Soreness
**Delayed onset muscle soreness** (DOMS) usually starts 12–24 hours after unaccustomed exercise and peaks around 24–72 hours.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "soreness after new exercise (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Same day", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Next day", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Day 2", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Day 4", "v": 30.0}]}
### Why it's not lactic acid
Lactate clears from muscles within an hour or so of exercise, long before soreness appears. So it can't be the cause. Instead, small disruptions in fibres and their connective tissue trigger inflammation, which sensitises nerve endings.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Lactic acid builds up", "r": "Micro-damage and inflammation"}]}
### Lowering, not lifting
Soreness comes mostly from **eccentric** movements, where a muscle lengthens under load: walking downhill, lowering a weight, landing jumps. After a long downhill hike, the front of your thighs is often sorest.
### The repeated bout effect
Do the same exercise again and you'll be much less sore, even a few weeks later. The muscle adapts quickly. So soreness is a sign of something new, not a measure of how good the workout was.
!? People new to hiking in mountains like the Serra da Estrela often find going down hurts more than going up.
> Soreness means your muscles met something new, not that you trained better.

# Small steps count
You don't need a gym. Short bouts of movement through the day add up, and the WHO dropped its old rule that activity only counts in blocks of 10 minutes or more.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Take stairs, not the lift", "Walk during phone calls", "Get off one stop early", "Stand and move each hour", "Walk 10 min after meals"]}
### Walking after meals
After eating, blood sugar rises. Working muscles pull glucose out of the blood without needing much insulin. Studies show that a short walk after a meal can noticeably flatten the sugar spike, especially in people with type 2 diabetes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Meal raises blood sugar", "Short walk", "Muscles absorb glucose", "Smaller sugar spike"]}
### Steps
The famous 10,000 steps goal came from a 1960s Japanese pedometer marketing campaign, not a study. Recent research suggests benefits rise steadily from about 4,000 steps and level off around 7,000–10,000 for many adults.
ex: 3 × 10-minute walks | + 2 flights of stairs a day | ≈ 30+ active minutes
!! Only proper workouts count => Every bit of movement adds to health benefits
> Movement snacks spread through the day are real exercise.

== bo-life

# One cell to trillions
A fertilised egg, or zygote, is about 0.1 mm across, just visible to the naked eye. Within hours it begins dividing: 2 cells, 4, 8, 16. By about day five it's a hollow ball of around 100–200 cells, called a blastocyst, which implants in the wall of the womb.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Day 1", "l": "Fertilised egg"}, {"y": "Day 5", "l": "Blastocyst forms"}, {"y": "Week 5", "l": "Heart starts beating"}, {"y": "Week 10", "l": "All organs outlined"}, {"y": "Week 40", "l": "Birth"}]}
### From copies to specialists
Early cells are all alike. Gradually, chemical signals tell them where they are in the embryo, and they switch on different sets of genes. One becomes a nerve cell, another a liver cell, though each carries the same DNA. This process is called **differentiation**.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "One fertilised egg", "items": ["Outer layer: skin, brain", "Middle layer: muscle, blood", "Inner layer: gut, lungs"]}
### Embryo, then fetus
For the first eight weeks after fertilisation the developing baby is called an embryo, when all major organs form. After that it's a fetus, and the main job is growing. A fetus goes from about 3 cm at week 10 to around 50 cm and 3.3 kg at birth.
ex: Start: 1 cell | About 40+ rounds of division | Trillions of cells by birth
!? Pregnancy is counted from the first day of the last period, so "40 weeks" is about 38 weeks from fertilisation.
> Every specialised cell in your body descends from one cell and carries the same genetic instructions.

# Newborn surprises
Newborns arrive with a body built for a tight squeeze and a fast-growing future.
### Around 300 bones
Many of a newborn's bones are still in separate pieces of bone and cartilage. Over childhood they fuse, leaving an adult with 206. The soft spots on a baby's skull, the fontanelles, let the head mould during birth and the brain grow; the largest closes by about 18 months.

### Senses at birth
• Sight is blurry, best at about 20–30 cm, roughly the distance to a parent's face during feeding
• Hearing works well; babies prefer their mother's voice and even the language heard in the womb
• Smell is keen; newborns can pick out the scent of their mother's milk
• Taste: a clear liking for sweet from day one

### Built-in reflexes
Newborns have reflexes that fade within months: grasping a finger tightly, turning towards a touch on the cheek to find food, and the startle reflex, flinging arms out at a sudden noise.

# Baby brains
At birth, the brain is roughly a quarter to a third of its adult size. It roughly doubles in the first year and reaches about 90% of adult volume by age five.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "brain size, % of adult (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Birth", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Age 1", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Age 5", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Adult", "v": 100.0}]}
### Connections first, pruning later
Babies are born with most of their neurons. What grows is the wiring. In early childhood, the brain forms connections, or synapses, at an astonishing rate, more than a million new ones per second by some estimates. Then it trims back the ones that aren't used.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Many connections form", "Experience strengthens some", "Unused ones pruned", "Efficient adult wiring"]}
### Why experience matters
Pruning follows a "use it or lose it" rule, so what a child hears, sees and does shapes the brain. Babies can distinguish the sounds of all the world's languages, but by about 10–12 months they become tuned to their own. A Portuguese baby loses the easy ear for some sounds that don't matter in Portuguese.
• Talking, reading and playing with babies supports language
• Responsive care builds secure emotional development
• Severe neglect in early years can have lasting effects
!! Classical music makes babies smarter => The "Mozart effect" was small, short-lived and in adults
!? Myelin, the insulating coat that speeds nerve signals, keeps being added through childhood and into adulthood.
> A baby's brain builds far more connections than it needs, then keeps the ones experience uses.

# Puberty
Puberty starts in the brain. The hypothalamus begins releasing a hormone in pulses, which tells the pituitary gland to signal the ovaries or testes. They then make oestrogen or testosterone, which drive the body's changes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hypothalamus signals", "Pituitary releases hormones", "Ovaries or testes respond", "Sex hormones rise", "Body changes"]}
### Timing
Girls usually start around ages 8–13, boys around 9–14. Breast budding is often the first sign in girls; enlargement of the testes in boys. The whole process takes several years.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Typical in girls", "items": ["starts ~8–13", "growth spurt early", "periods begin"]}, "r": {"t": "Typical in boys", "items": ["starts ~9–14", "growth spurt later", "voice deepens"]}}
### Getting earlier
In Europe, the average age of a first period fell from around 16–17 in the 1800s to about 12–13 today. Better nutrition and health explain most of the change. Body fat signals to the brain that there's enough energy for reproduction.
### Growth spurt
During peak growth, teenagers can grow 8–10 cm a year. Hands and feet grow first, then limbs, then the trunk, which is why teens can look briefly gangly. Growth stops when the growth plates at the ends of long bones fuse.
!? A boy's voice breaks because the larynx grows and the vocal cords lengthen and thicken by up to about 60%.
> Puberty is a brain-led programme, timed by genes and nutrition, that rebuilds body and mind over years.

# The teenage brain
The brain doesn't mature all at once. Brain scans show that it develops roughly from back to front, and the **prefrontal cortex**, just behind the forehead, is among the last areas to finish.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Matures earlier", "items": ["reward system", "emotion centres", "seeking new things"]}, "r": {"t": "Matures later", "items": ["prefrontal cortex", "planning, self-control", "weighing risks"]}}
### The mismatch
During adolescence, the reward system becomes highly sensitive, especially to social rewards. The brakes, controlled by the prefrontal cortex, are still being tuned. The result is more risk-taking and sensation-seeking, particularly when friends are watching.

### Not just a problem
This phase has a purpose. Taking risks and seeking novelty help young people leave home, explore and learn. Teenage brains are also very adaptable, making it a great time to learn skills.
### Sleep shifts
Puberty shifts the body clock later by an hour or two. Teenagers genuinely feel sleepy later at night and struggle with early mornings, which has led some schools to try later start times.
!! The brain is finished by 18 => Key areas keep maturing into the mid-20s
> Teenagers have a powerful accelerator and brakes still being fitted.

# Peak years
There's no single peak age. Different abilities rise and fall on different timelines.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 15.0, "hi": 75.0, "unit": "age at typical peak (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Processing speed", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "Muscle strength", "v": 28.0}, {"l": "Reading emotions", "v": 45.0}, {"l": "Vocabulary", "v": 67.0, "hi": true}]}
### Fluid and crystallised
Psychologists distinguish two kinds of intelligence. **Fluid intelligence**, solving new problems quickly, peaks in early adulthood and slowly declines. **Crystallised intelligence**, accumulated knowledge and vocabulary, keeps rising for decades.

### In sport
• Sprinters and gymnasts typically peak in their early-to-mid 20s
• Marathon runners often peak around 30
• Ultra-endurance athletes and golfers can stay competitive into their late 30s and beyond

!? Experience can offset slowing: older typists type as fast as younger ones because they look further ahead in the text.
> Your body may peak early, but your knowledge and judgement keep growing for decades.

# Why we age
Ageing isn't one process but many, all involving damage that builds up faster than it's repaired. Scientists group them into a list of "hallmarks of ageing".
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["DNA damage builds up", "Telomeres shorten", "Proteins misfold and clump", "Mitochondria work less well", "Senescent cells accumulate"]}
### Telomeres
Telomeres are protective caps at the ends of chromosomes, like the plastic tips on shoelaces. Each time a cell divides, they get a little shorter. When too short, the cell stops dividing. This acts as a brake on cancer but limits tissue renewal.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cell divides", "Telomeres shorten", "Too short: stop signal", "Cell enters senescence"]}
### Zombie cells
**Senescent cells** have stopped dividing but don't die. They release inflammatory signals that harm nearby tissue. In mice, clearing them extends healthy lifespan, and drugs called senolytics are being tested in people.
### Why evolution allowed it
Natural selection acts most strongly before and during reproduction. Genes that help early in life but cause harm decades later aren't removed effectively. So bodies are built to reproduce well, not to last for ever.
!? Some animals barely seem to age: the Greenland shark may live 250–500 years, and some jellyfish can revert to a young stage.
> Ageing is the slow build-up of damage that our repair systems never quite keep up with.

# Life expectancy
Portugal's life expectancy at birth has climbed by about 18 years since 1960, one of the steepest rises in Western Europe. Women in Portugal now live to about 84 on average, men to about 78.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "life expectancy at birth in Portugal, years (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1960", "v": 63.0}, {"l": "1980", "v": 71.0}, {"l": "2000", "v": 76.0}, {"l": "Today", "v": 81.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it rose
Most of the early gain came from saving babies and young children. Portugal's infant mortality in 1960 was around 78 deaths per 1,000 births; today it's about 2–3, among the lowest in the world.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why Portugal gained", "items": ["Vaccines", "Clean water, sanitation", "National Health Service", "Better nutrition"]}
The national health service, the SNS, created in 1979, extended care across the country. Later gains came from preventing and treating heart disease and stroke.
### A common misunderstanding
A low average life expectancy in the past doesn't mean most adults died at 35. High child deaths pulled the average down. People who survived childhood often lived into their 60s and 70s.
!! People used to die at 40 => Many died as children, which dragged the average down
> Life expectancy rose mostly by saving the young, and lately by keeping adults healthier for longer.

# Blue zones
"Blue zones" is a term popularised by writer Dan Buettner in the 2000s for places with unusually many long-lived people.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Okinawa, Japan", "Sardinia, Italy", "Nicoya, Costa Rica", "Ikaria, Greece", "Loma Linda, California"]}
### Shared habits
• Natural, all-day movement: walking, gardening, hill farming rather than gyms
• Mostly plant-based diets with beans, vegetables and whole grains
• Strong family and community ties
• A sense of purpose, and ways to unwind
• In some, moderate wine with meals
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Common habits", "items": ["Move naturally", "Plant-rich food", "Close social ties", "Sense of purpose"]}
### A note of caution
The idea has been criticised. In some regions, birth records were unreliable, and some apparent centenarians turned out to be younger than claimed. Okinawa's lifestyle has also changed, and younger generations there no longer stand out. So treat blue zones as an interesting pattern, not proof.
The traditional Mediterranean diet, which UNESCO lists as cultural heritage and which includes Portugal, shares many of the same features.
!? Sardinia's long-lived area is unusual because men there live nearly as long as women, a rare pattern.
> The habits linked to long lives are ordinary ones: movement, plants, people and purpose.

# Ageing well
No pill reliably slows human ageing yet. But large studies point to everyday habits that keep people healthier for longer, sometimes adding a decade of good health.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Move every day", "Keep muscles strong", "Sleep 7–9 hours", "Don't smoke", "Stay curious and connected"]}
### Why each matters
• **Activity** protects heart, brain and mood
• **Strength and balance** prevent falls and keep independence
• **Sleep** clears waste from the brain and supports memory
• **Not smoking** is the biggest single lever; quitting at any age helps
• **Learning and social contact** are linked to lower dementia risk; loneliness carries health risks comparable to well-known ones like smoking, though estimates vary
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lifespan", "items": ["how long you live", "years in total"]}, "r": {"t": "Healthspan", "items": ["years in good health", "goal of ageing well"]}}
### Hearing and dementia
A major report in The Lancet listed modifiable risk factors for dementia, including hearing loss, physical inactivity, smoking, high blood pressure and social isolation. Treating hearing loss with hearing aids is now seen as one way to protect the brain.
!! It's too late to start at 70 => Strength, fitness and balance improve at any age
> Ageing well is less about one secret than doing the ordinary things consistently.

== bo-genes

# Half from each parent
Your DNA is packaged into 46 chromosomes: 23 pairs. One of each pair came from your mother's egg and one from your father's sperm.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "46", "l": "chromosomes per cell"}, {"v": "~20,000", "l": "protein-coding genes"}, {"v": "2", "l": "versions of most genes"}]}
### Versions of genes
Both copies of a gene do the same job, but they can differ slightly. These versions are called **alleles**. One version of a gene might make an enzyme work well, another make it less active.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mum's egg: 23", "Dad's sperm: 23", "Fertilisation: 46", "Every cell gets a copy"]}
### Why siblings differ
When eggs and sperm form, each pair of chromosomes is split, and the pieces are shuffled. Chromosomes even swap segments, a process called crossing over. So each egg or sperm carries a unique mix.
ex: 23 pairs, each split two ways | 2^23 ≈ 8.4 million combinations | Before crossing over adds even more variety
That's why siblings share about half their DNA on average, but can look strikingly different. Only identical twins, from one fertilised egg, share virtually all.
!? Your mitochondria have their own small DNA, and you inherited it only from your mother.
> You're a fresh shuffle of your parents' genes, half from each, never quite the same mix twice.

# Mendel's peas
Gregor Mendel was an Augustinian friar in Brno, in today's Czech Republic. Between about 1856 and 1863 he grew and crossed some 28,000 pea plants, tracking clear-cut traits like flower colour, seed shape and plant height.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Purple × white", "All offspring purple", "Cross them together", "3 purple : 1 white"]}
### The insight
Mendel concluded that traits are carried by discrete units, which we now call genes, coming in pairs, one from each parent. They don't blend like paint; they're passed on intact and can skip a generation.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "second generation, purple vs white flowers", "items": [{"l": "Purple", "v": 75.0, "t": "3", "hi": true}, {"l": "White", "v": 25.0, "t": "1"}]}
### Ignored, then rediscovered
He published in 1866 in a local journal, and almost nobody understood its importance. In 1900, three botanists independently rediscovered his laws. Mendel had died in 1884, never knowing.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1866", "l": "Mendel publishes"}, {"y": "1884", "l": "Mendel dies"}, {"y": "1900", "l": "Work rediscovered"}, {"y": "1953", "l": "DNA structure found"}]}
!? Statistician Ronald Fisher later argued Mendel's results fitted his ratios almost too well, perhaps through unconscious tidying of data.
> Mendel showed that heredity works in units, not blends, decades before anyone knew what those units were.

# Dominant and recessive
If you have two different versions of a gene, often one shows its effect and masks the other. The one that shows is **dominant**; the hidden one is **recessive**.
### A classic cross
Take two parents who each carry one brown (B) and one blue (b) version. Each passes on one at random.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "BB (1 in 4)", "b": "brown eyes"}, {"a": "Bb (2 in 4)", "b": "brown eyes, carries blue"}, {"a": "bb (1 in 4)", "b": "blue eyes"}]}
ex: Both parents Bb | Chance child gets b from each: ½ × ½ | = 1 in 4 blue-eyed
### Eye colour is a simplification
Real eye colour involves several genes, the main one called OCA2 with its switch HERC2. So the textbook rule is a good teaching model, not the full story. Two blue-eyed parents can occasionally have a brown-eyed child.
### Carriers and disease
Many inherited diseases are recessive. Carriers, with one copy, are usually healthy. Cystic fibrosis is one example: about 1 in 25 people of Northern European ancestry carry the gene.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Recessive conditions", "items": ["cystic fibrosis", "sickle cell disease"]}, "r": {"t": "Dominant conditions", "items": ["Huntington's disease", "Portuguese FAP"]}}
!? Familial amyloid polyneuropathy, a dominant condition called "paramiloidose" or "doença dos pezinhos", is unusually common in northern Portugal, especially around Póvoa de Varzim and Vila do Conde.
!! Dominant means common => Dominant only describes how it shows, not how frequent it is
> Recessive traits can hide in carriers for generations, then appear when two copies meet.

# Boy or girl
Of your 23 chromosome pairs, one pair decides typical sex development. Usually, XX leads to female development and XY to male.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Egg always carries X", "Sperm carries X or Y", "X + X: usually female", "X + Y: usually male"]}
### The key gene
The Y chromosome carries a gene called **SRY**. Around week seven of development, it switches on the formation of testes. Their hormones then drive male development. Without SRY, the embryo develops along the female path.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "boys born per 100 girls (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Boys", "v": 105.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Girls", "v": 100.0}]}
### Not always simple
Biology has variations. Some people have different combinations, such as XXY (Klinefelter syndrome, about 1 in 600 males) or a single X (Turner syndrome, about 1 in 2,500 females). Others have XY chromosomes but bodies that can't respond to testosterone. Medicine calls these differences of sex development.
### Odds per child
Each pregnancy is roughly a coin toss, independent of previous children. Slightly more boys are born worldwide, about 105 per 100 girls.
!? In many reptiles, including some turtles and crocodiles, sex is set by the temperature of the egg, not by chromosomes.
> It's the sperm, carrying X or Y, that usually tips the balance.

# Most traits are complex
Mendel's peas had clean traits. Most human traits are more like a dimmer switch than an on-off switch: many genes each nudge them a little. These are called **polygenic** traits.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Your height", "items": ["Thousands of gene variants", "Childhood nutrition", "Health and illness", "Sleep and hormones"]}
### Height as an example
Height is highly heritable, about 80% of the variation within a well-fed population comes from genetic differences. Yet a 2022 study of over 5 million people found more than 12,000 genetic variants linked to height, each with a tiny effect.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Single-gene traits", "items": ["cystic fibrosis", "clear inheritance"]}, "r": {"t": "Polygenic traits", "items": ["height, weight", "heart disease risk"]}}
### Environment shifts the whole curve
Genes set a range, environment decides where you land. In about 150 years, the average Dutch man grew about 20 cm taller, far too fast for genes to change. Better food and health did it. Portuguese adults have also grown taller over the same period, though they remain among Europe's shorter populations.
!! There's a gene for intelligence => Thousands of variants plus environment shape it
### Polygenic scores
Scientists can add up many small genetic effects into a risk score for diseases like heart disease. These scores help at a population level, but they predict poorly for individuals and work less well in people of non-European ancestry.
> Most traits are shaped by many genes acting together, inside an environment that matters just as much.

# Nature and nurture
Twins are nature's experiment. **Identical twins** come from one fertilised egg and share almost all their DNA. **Non-identical twins** share about half, like ordinary siblings. Both usually grow up in the same home.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Identical twins", "items": ["one egg splits", "~100% DNA shared"]}, "r": {"t": "Non-identical", "items": ["two eggs", "~50% DNA shared"]}}
### The logic
If identical twins are much more alike for a trait than non-identical twins, genes probably matter for that trait. The difference is used to estimate **heritability**: the share of variation in a population linked to genetic differences.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "heritability estimates, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Height", "v": 80.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Adult IQ", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Personality", "v": 40.0}]}
### What heritability doesn't mean
• It describes populations, not individuals
• 50% heritable doesn't mean half your trait came from genes
• It can change with environment: where everyone gets good food, height differences become more genetic
A huge 2015 review of over 14 million twin pairs, studied over 50 years, concluded that nearly all traits are partly heritable, with an average across traits around 50%.
!? Identical twins raised apart, like those in the famous Minnesota study, often share surprising quirks but also differ in important ways.
> The real answer to nature or nurture is almost always both, working together.

# The Human Genome Project
Launched in 1990, the Human Genome Project was an international effort, led by the US and UK, to read the full sequence of human DNA. A draft was announced in 2000 and declared complete in 2003, two years ahead of schedule.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1990", "l": "Project begins"}, {"y": "2000", "l": "Draft announced"}, {"y": "2003", "l": "Declared complete"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "Last gaps filled"}]}
### Not quite finished
The 2003 version left about 8% unread, mainly repetitive regions that were hard to piece together. The first truly complete, gap-free human genome was published in 2022 by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium.
### The price crash
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2003", "l": "~$3 billion (project)"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "~$10 million"}, {"l": "~$1,000", "y": "2014"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "a few hundred dollars"}]}
The cost fell faster than computer chips improved, thanks to new methods that read millions of DNA fragments at once.
### What we learnt
• Humans have only about 20,000 protein-coding genes, far fewer than the 100,000 once expected
• Less than 2% of the genome codes for proteins
• Much of the rest regulates genes, or is left over from ancient viruses
!? About 8% of your genome is made of remnants of viruses that infected our ancestors.
> Reading the genome took 13 years and billions; now it takes about a day for a few hundred euros.

# We're 99.9% the same
Any two humans differ at roughly 1 in every 1,000 DNA letters. Across 3 billion letters, that's still about 3 million differences, enough to make each of us unique.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "DNA similarity to humans, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Another human", "v": 99.9, "hi": true}, {"l": "Chimpanzee", "v": 98.8}, {"l": "Mouse (genes)", "v": 85.0}, {"l": "Banana (genes)", "v": 60.0}]}
### Comparing to bananas
The banana figure is often misquoted. It doesn't mean 60% of your DNA matches banana DNA. It means around 60% of human genes have a recognisable counterpart in the banana, because all life shares ancient machinery for things like copying DNA and making energy.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "We're 60% banana", "r": "60% of genes have a banana version"}]}
### Most variation is within groups
Genetic studies show that most human variation, about 85–90%, exists within any population, rather than between continents. Two people from Portugal may differ more genetically than one of them does from someone in Asia.
### Africa holds the most diversity
Humans originated in Africa, and small groups later migrated out. So African populations hold more genetic diversity than the rest of the world combined.
!? Most Europeans and Asians carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA, from interbreeding tens of thousands of years ago.
> Tiny genetic differences make each of us unique, but we're overwhelmingly alike.

# Epigenetics
"Epi" means "on top of". **Epigenetics** is the layer of chemical marks on DNA and its packaging that controls which genes are read, without changing the sequence itself.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Genetic code", "items": ["the letters (A,C,G,T)", "fixed for life"]}, "r": {"t": "Epigenetic marks", "items": ["tags on DNA", "can change"]}}
### How it works
• **DNA methylation**: small chemical tags on DNA, often silencing a gene
• **Histone changes**: DNA is wound around proteins called histones; tightening or loosening the winding switches access on or off
This is how a liver cell and a nerve cell, with identical DNA, behave so differently.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["DNA wound on histones", "Tags added or removed", "Gene opened or closed", "Cell behaviour changes"]}
### Environment leaves marks
Diet, smoking, stress and ageing can all alter epigenetic tags. Children conceived during the Dutch Hunger Winter of 1944–45 showed different methylation patterns decades later, and higher rates of some health problems.
### Epigenetic clocks
Methylation patterns change predictably with age, so scientists use them as "clocks" to estimate biological age. Smokers and people under chronic stress often look older by these measures.
!! You can rewrite your genes with lifestyle => Lifestyle shifts gene activity, not the code itself
!? Identical twins start with near-identical epigenetic marks, but they drift apart as they age.
> Epigenetics is how your cells read the same book differently, and how life annotates the pages.

# Gene editing
CRISPR comes from a defence system bacteria use against viruses. Bacteria store snippets of viral DNA and use a protein, Cas9, to cut matching DNA if the virus returns. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed how to reprogramme it to cut any chosen DNA. They won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Guide RNA finds target", "Cas9 cuts DNA", "Cell repairs the cut", "Gene disabled or fixed"]}
### The first CRISPR medicine
In late 2023, regulators in the UK and US approved Casgevy, for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia; Europe followed in 2024. Doctors take a patient's blood stem cells, edit them to switch back on a form of haemoglobin normally made before birth, and return them.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2012", "l": "CRISPR tool shown"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "Edited babies scandal"}, {"y": "2020", "l": "Nobel Prize"}, {"y": "2023", "l": "First approval"}]}
### Ethics
Editing body cells affects only the patient. Editing embryos changes the DNA of future generations. In 2018 Chinese scientist He Jiankui announced the birth of gene-edited twins; he was widely condemned and jailed. Most countries, including Portugal through the Council of Europe's Oviedo Convention, ban heritable editing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Body cell editing", "items": ["treats one person", "allowed in trials"]}, "r": {"t": "Embryo editing", "items": ["passed to children", "widely banned"]}}
!? The cost of current CRISPR therapies runs to around €2 million per patient, a major challenge for health systems.
> CRISPR turned a bacterial immune system into a precise tool for rewriting DNA.

== bo-teeth

# Two sets
Humans are **diphyodont**: we grow just two sets of teeth in a lifetime. Sharks replace teeth endlessly; we get one swap, so the second set has to last.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "6 months", "l": "First baby teeth"}, {"y": "~3 years", "l": "All 20 baby teeth"}, {"y": "~6 years", "l": "First adult molars"}, {"y": "~12 years", "l": "Most adult teeth in"}, {"y": "17–25", "l": "Wisdom teeth, maybe"}]}
### Baby teeth matter
Baby teeth hold space for the adult teeth forming in the jaw beneath them, and they help children chew and learn to speak. Losing one early to decay can let neighbouring teeth drift, crowding the adult teeth later.
### The swap
As an adult tooth grows, it dissolves the root of the baby tooth above it, which loosens and falls out. The first adult molars appear behind the baby teeth at about age six, without replacing anything, so parents often mistake them for baby teeth.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Baby teeth", "items": ["20 teeth", "no premolars", "thinner enamel"]}, "r": {"t": "Adult teeth", "items": ["up to 32 teeth", "premolars added", "must last a lifetime"]}}
### Wisdom teeth
Our jaws have become shorter over human history, partly because of softer, cooked food. Wisdom teeth, the third molars, often lack room. Some people never develop some or all of them; this is more common in some populations.
!? Adult teeth are already forming in a newborn's jaw, hidden beneath the baby teeth that haven't erupted yet.
> You get exactly one replacement set, so adult teeth must last decades.

# Layers of a tooth
A tooth is built in layers, a bit like a castle with a hard wall around a living centre.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Enamel: hardest outer shell", "Dentine: tough, sensitive layer", "Pulp: nerves and blood vessels", "Cementum and root in the jaw"]}
### Enamel
Enamel is about 96% mineral, mainly a calcium phosphate crystal called hydroxyapatite. It's much harder than bone, but brittle and cannot regrow because the cells that make it are lost once the tooth erupts.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mineral content, % by weight (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Enamel", "v": 96.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Dentine", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Bone", "v": 65.0}]}
### Dentine and sensitivity
Dentine is softer and yellowish, which is why teeth look yellower when enamel thins. It's full of tiny tubes, about 30,000–50,000 per square millimetre, filled with fluid. When dentine is exposed, cold or sweet things move that fluid and trigger nerves: tooth sensitivity.
### The pulp
The living pulp supplies nutrients and senses. When decay or a crack reaches it, it can become inflamed and painful. Root canal treatment removes the pulp and seals the tooth so it can be kept.
!? Unlike enamel, dentine keeps forming slowly throughout life, so the pulp chamber shrinks with age.
> Enamel is the body's hardest material, but it can't heal itself, which is why prevention matters.

# How cavities form
Your mouth hosts hundreds of species of bacteria. Some, like **Streptococcus mutans**, ferment sugars and release acid. They live in plaque, a sticky biofilm on teeth.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sugar reaches plaque", "Bacteria make acid", "pH drops below 5.5", "Enamel minerals dissolve", "Cavity forms over time"]}
### A tug of war
Enamel is constantly losing and regaining minerals. When the pH in plaque falls below about 5.5, minerals leave the enamel. Between meals, saliva neutralises the acid and minerals return. Cavities form when losses repeatedly outweigh gains.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Demineralisation", "items": ["acid from sugar", "minerals leave"]}, "r": {"t": "Remineralisation", "items": ["saliva, fluoride", "minerals return"]}}
### From spot to hole
The first sign is a chalky white spot, which can still recover. Once the surface collapses, a true hole forms and needs a filling. Decay spreads faster in softer dentine, so a small entry can hide a bigger cavity underneath.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 4.0, "hi": 8.0, "unit": "pH in plaque", "items": [{"l": "After sugar", "v": 4.5}, {"l": "Danger line", "v": 5.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Resting", "v": 7.0}]}
Tooth decay is the most common disease in the world, affecting billions of people, according to the WHO.
!! Sugar itself rots teeth => The acid bacteria make from sugar does
> Cavities are a slow battle between acid attacks and saliva's repairs.

# How often, not how much
Each time sugar reaches plaque, the pH plunges within minutes and takes about 20–30 minutes, sometimes longer, to recover. What matters most is how many of those dips happen in a day.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sugar all at once", "items": ["one acid attack", "saliva recovers"]}, "r": {"t": "Sipped all day", "items": ["many attacks", "no time to repair"]}}
ex: 5 sweet snacks a day | × ~25 min each | ≈ 2 hours of acid attack
ex: Same sugar with one meal | 1 attack | ≈ 25 minutes
### Hidden culprits
• Fruit juice and smoothies, which free sugar from the fruit's cells
• Sweetened coffee or tea sipped slowly
• Sports and energy drinks
• Dried fruit and sticky sweets that cling to teeth
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "minutes of acid attack per day (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "3 meals", "v": 75.0}, {"l": "3 meals + 4 snacks", "v": 175.0, "hi": true}]}
### Acid erosion
Acidic drinks like cola, wine and citrus juice don't need bacteria: they dissolve enamel directly. Drinking through a straw, not swishing, and waiting to brush after acidic drinks all help.
!? Portugal introduced a tax on sugary drinks in 2017, and many manufacturers cut sugar to fall into lower tax brackets.
> Keep sugar to mealtimes: fewer attacks give saliva time to repair.

# Fluoride
Fluoride works in three ways: it helps minerals return to enamel, it swaps into the crystal to form a more acid-resistant mineral, and it slows acid production by bacteria.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Fluoride", "items": ["Speeds repair", "Tougher enamel crystals", "Slows bacteria"]}
### A discovery from stained teeth
In the early 1900s, dentist Frederick McKay noticed people in Colorado Springs with brown-stained teeth that were oddly resistant to decay. The cause turned out to be natural fluoride in the water. That led to the first planned water fluoridation in Grand Rapids, Michigan, in 1945.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1901", "l": "Stained teeth noticed"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "Fluoride identified"}, {"y": "1945", "l": "Water fluoridation"}, {"y": "1950s+", "l": "Fluoride toothpaste"}]}
### In Europe
Most of Europe, including Portugal, doesn't fluoridate water. Fluoride toothpaste is the main source. Adults usually use toothpaste with about 1,000–1,500 ppm fluoride; check the label.
### Too much
High fluoride during childhood, while teeth form, can cause **fluorosis**, white streaks or spots on enamel. That's why young children should use only a small smear or pea-sized amount of toothpaste and be supervised.
!! Fluoride is only for children => Adults benefit too, especially as gums recede
> Fluoride doesn't just protect enamel; it helps rebuild it after every acid attack.

# Brushing well
Good brushing is about technique and timing more than force.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Twice a day, 2 minutes", "Fluoride toothpaste", "Angle bristles at gumline", "Spit, don't rinse", "Clean between teeth daily"]}
### Spit, don't rinse
After brushing, a thin layer of fluoride stays on your teeth and keeps working. Rinsing with water washes most of it away. Spitting out the excess and leaving the rest is a simple change that helps.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Brush hard, rinse well", "r": "Brush gently, just spit"}]}
### Between the teeth
A toothbrush can't reach the tight surfaces between teeth, where much decay and gum disease start. Floss works for tight gaps; interdental brushes are often easier and better for larger gaps.
### Manual or electric?
Both work if used well. Reviews suggest powered brushes with rotating heads remove slightly more plaque and reduce gum inflammation a little more than manual brushes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "brushing time, seconds", "items": [{"l": "Typical person", "v": 45.0}, {"l": "Recommended", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}]}
!? Pressing too hard wears enamel and gums; many electric brushes now light up when you push too hard.
> Two minutes, twice a day, then spit and leave the fluoride to work.

# Gums matter
Gum disease comes in two stages. **Gingivitis** is inflammation of the gums caused by plaque: red, swollen gums that bleed when brushed. It's very common and fully reversible.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plaque at gumline", "Gingivitis: bleeding", "Tartar, deeper pockets", "Periodontitis: bone loss", "Loose teeth"]}
### Periodontitis
If plaque stays, it hardens into tartar and the inflammation can spread below the gumline. Pockets form between gum and tooth, and the bone holding the tooth slowly dissolves. This is **periodontitis**, a leading cause of tooth loss in adults. Severe forms affect around 1 in 5 adults worldwide.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gingivitis", "items": ["gums bleed", "no bone loss", "reversible"]}, "r": {"t": "Periodontitis", "items": ["pockets form", "bone lost", "manageable only"]}}
### Links beyond the mouth
• **Diabetes**: the link runs both ways; poorly controlled diabetes worsens gum disease, and treating gum disease can slightly improve blood sugar
• **Heart disease**: gum disease is associated with higher risk, possibly through body-wide inflammation, though cause and effect isn't proven
• **Smoking** is a major risk and hides bleeding, so damage goes unnoticed
!! Bleeding gums mean stop brushing there => Usually they need gentler, more thorough cleaning
> Bleeding gums are an early warning, and at that stage the damage can still be reversed.

# Saliva
Saliva is about 99% water, but the remaining 1% is busy: enzymes, antibodies, minerals and buffers. Three main pairs of glands make it, plus hundreds of tiny ones.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Saliva", "items": ["Washes and lubricates", "Neutralises acid", "Starts digestion", "Dissolves taste molecules"]}
### Its jobs
• **Buffer**: bicarbonate neutralises plaque acid
• **Repair**: calcium and phosphate help remineralise enamel
• **Digestion**: amylase starts breaking down starch; chew bread long enough and it tastes sweeter
• **Defence**: antibodies and enzymes like lysozyme fight microbes
• **Taste**: food must dissolve in saliva to reach taste buds
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "saliva flow (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Sleep", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Resting, awake", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Eating", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}]}
### Dry mouth
Saliva drops almost to zero during sleep, which is why morning breath happens. Many medicines, ageing and some conditions cause chronic dry mouth, which raises the risk of decay sharply. Sugar-free gum stimulates flow and helps.
!? Chewing sugar-free gum after a meal boosts saliva flow, helping neutralise acid sooner.
> Saliva is your mouth's built-in repair kit, washing, neutralising and rebuilding all day.

# Tooth types
Human teeth are **heterodont**: different shapes for different jobs. An adult's full set has 8 incisors, 4 canines, 8 premolars and up to 12 molars.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Incisors (8)", "b": "thin edges for cutting"}, {"a": "Canines (4)", "b": "pointed, for gripping, tearing"}, {"a": "Premolars (8)", "b": "two cusps, crush and tear"}, {"a": "Molars (12)", "b": "broad, for grinding"}]}
### What teeth say about diet
Carnivores like cats have blade-like teeth for slicing meat. Cows have broad grinding molars for grass. Our mixed set, with modest canines and flat molars, fits an omnivore's diet.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Carnivore (cat)", "items": ["long canines", "blade-like cheek teeth"]}, "r": {"t": "Herbivore (cow)", "items": ["no upper incisors", "wide grinding molars"]}}
### Small human canines
Compared with other apes, human canines are small in both sexes. Our ancestors' canines shrank millions of years ago, perhaps as fighting with teeth became less important and tools took over.
ex: 2 incisors + 1 canine + 2 premolars + 3 molars | = 8 per quarter of the mouth | × 4 = 32
!? Elephants' tusks are giant incisors; walrus tusks are canines.
> Each tooth shape is a tool, and together they show we evolved to eat almost anything.

# Teeth tell stories
Teeth are the body's best archive. Enamel forms in childhood and never remodels, and teeth survive long after bones have crumbled.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "A tooth reveals", "items": ["Age at death", "Childhood diet", "Where you grew up", "Illness in childhood"]}
### Chemical passports
Enamel absorbs chemical elements from food and water while it forms. The ratio of **strontium isotopes** reflects local rocks, so archaeologists can tell if someone grew up where they were buried. Oxygen isotopes reflect local water and climate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Child drinks local water", "Elements enter enamel", "Enamel locked for life", "Lab reads isotopes"]}
### Growth lines
Teeth grow in daily layers, like tree rings. Stress, illness or hunger in childhood leaves lines and grooves called enamel hypoplasia. A sharp "neonatal line" marks the moment of birth.
### In practice
Isotope studies have traced migrants buried at Stonehenge and helped study a 15th–16th-century burial ground in Lagos, in the Algarve, of Africans brought to Portugal by the slave trade. Teeth also help identify victims after disasters, by comparing them with dental records.
!? Ancient tartar traps food particles and DNA, letting scientists find what people ate thousands of years ago.
> Your teeth quietly keep a record of where you grew up and what you ate.

# Hippocrates
Hippocrates of Kos lived around 460–370 BCE in ancient Greece. We know little about him personally; the "Hippocratic Corpus" of about 60 texts was written by many authors over a long time.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before", "items": ["illness from gods", "cures by prayer"]}, "r": {"t": "Hippocratic idea", "items": ["natural causes", "observe, record"]}}
### Observation first
The Hippocratic writers urged doctors to watch symptoms closely, record how illnesses developed and consider diet, climate and lifestyle. One famous text argues that epilepsy, then called "the sacred disease", has natural causes like any other illness.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Observe the patient", "Record symptoms", "Predict the course", "Support natural healing"]}
### The Oath
The Hippocratic Oath included promises to help the sick, avoid harm, keep confidentiality and not give poison. Doctors today usually take a modern version, such as the World Medical Association's Declaration of Geneva, first adopted in 1948.
!! "First, do no harm" is in the Oath => The phrase came later; the Oath says something similar in other words
### A lasting legacy
• Clubbed fingertips, a sign of lung and heart disease, are still called "Hippocratic fingers"
• Clinical case notes trace back to these writings
• But much was wrong, including the theory of four humours
!? Doctors in Portugal sign up to the Ordem dos Médicos' code of ethics, which echoes Hippocratic principles.
> Hippocrates' big idea was simple: diseases have natural causes that careful observation can reveal.

# Four humours
Humoral theory, developed in Hippocratic writings and expanded by the Roman physician **Galen** (around 129–216 CE), dominated Western and Islamic medicine until the 1800s.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Blood", "b": "warm and moist; sanguine"}, {"a": "Phlegm", "b": "cold and moist; phlegmatic"}, {"a": "Yellow bile", "b": "warm and dry; choleric"}, {"a": "Black bile", "b": "cold and dry; melancholic"}]}
### How it worked
Health meant the four humours in balance. Illness meant too much or too little of one. Treatments aimed to rebalance: bloodletting, purging, vomiting, special diets and changes of climate.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Illness seen", "Excess humour blamed", "Bleed or purge", "Patient weakened"]}
### The cost of bloodletting
Removing blood weakens patients and can be fatal. George Washington was bled of a large volume of blood in his final illness in 1799. In the 1830s, French doctor Pierre Louis compared outcomes in pneumonia patients and found bleeding didn't help, an early use of statistics in medicine.
### Its echoes
• Words: sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric and melancholic still describe temperaments
• "Melancholy" literally means black bile
• Bloodletting survives in one place: for a few conditions like haemochromatosis, removing blood is correct treatment
!? Barbers performed bloodletting; the red and white barber's pole is said to recall bloody bandages.
> A theory can survive for 2,000 years without evidence, which is why medicine now insists on testing.

# Anatomy
Andreas Vesalius, a Flemish physician, taught anatomy at the University of Padua. In 1543 he published **De humani corporis fabrica** ("On the fabric of the human body"), seven books with detailed woodcut illustrations.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~170", "l": "Galen's anatomy"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "Vesalius's Fabrica"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "Copernicus, same year"}, {"y": "1628", "l": "Harvey on circulation"}]}
### Galen corrected
Galen had dissected animals such as monkeys and pigs, since human dissection was forbidden in Rome. Vesalius dissected human bodies himself and showed that many of Galen's claims applied to animals, not people.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Jawbone in two parts", "r": "One jawbone in humans"}, {"w": "Pores in heart wall", "r": "No holes in the septum"}]}
### Why it mattered
• Medicine began trusting direct observation over ancient authority
• The illustrations, probably from Titian's workshop, set a new standard
• Printing let the same accurate images reach doctors across Europe
!? Vesalius published the Fabrica aged just 28, and later served as physician to Emperor Charles V and King Philip II of Spain.
> Vesalius taught doctors to look at the body itself rather than trust old books.

# Circulation
English physician William Harvey published **De motu cordis** ("On the motion of the heart") in 1628. Galen had taught that the liver made blood from food, which was then consumed by the body like fuel.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Galen's view", "items": ["liver makes blood", "body uses it up"]}, "r": {"t": "Harvey's view", "items": ["heart pumps it", "blood circulates"]}}
### The killer calculation
Harvey estimated how much blood the heart pushes out with each beat and multiplied by beats per hour. The total was far more than a person eats or weighs, so the blood must be recycled.
ex: About 60 ml per beat | × 72 beats a minute × 60 | ≈ 260 litres an hour, far more than body weight
### More evidence
He tied tourniquets on arms to show blood flows outward in arteries and back in veins, and showed that valves in veins only let blood flow towards the heart.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Heart pumps out", "Arteries carry blood", "Tiny vessels link", "Veins return it"]}
### The missing link
Harvey couldn't see how blood got from arteries to veins. In 1661 Marcello Malpighi, using a microscope, found the capillaries in a frog's lung, completing the circle.
!? Harvey's ideas were mocked at first and some patients reportedly left his practice, but within a few decades they were widely accepted.
> Harvey proved the heart is a pump driving blood in a circle, using measurement and simple arithmetic.

# Germ theory
Before germ theory, many believed diseases spread through **miasma**, foul "bad air" from rotting matter. The idea wasn't silly: cleaning up filth did reduce disease, but for the wrong reason.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1676", "l": "Microbes first seen"}, {"y": "1854", "l": "Snow's cholera map"}, {"y": "1860s", "l": "Pasteur's experiments"}, {"y": "1882", "l": "Koch: TB bacterium"}]}
### Pasteur
Louis Pasteur showed that microbes cause fermentation and spoilage, and that boiled broth in a swan-necked flask stayed sterile, disproving spontaneous generation. Heating to kill microbes, now called pasteurisation, made milk and wine safer. He also developed vaccines for anthrax and rabies.
### Koch
Robert Koch identified the bacteria behind anthrax, tuberculosis (1882) and cholera (1883). His criteria, **Koch's postulates**, set out how to prove a particular microbe causes a particular disease.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Find microbe in sick", "Grow it in pure culture", "Infect a healthy host", "Recover the same microbe"]}
### What changed
• Surgeons adopted antiseptics, led by Joseph Lister from 1867
• Cities built sewers and treated water
• Food safety and hygiene rules took shape
!! Microbes are all harmful => Most are harmless or helpful; a small minority cause disease
> Germ theory showed that invisible living things cause disease, and that changed everything from surgery to sewers.

# Washing hands
In the 1840s, at Vienna General Hospital, the maternity unit run by doctors and medical students had a far higher death rate from childbed fever than the one run by midwives.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mothers dying of childbed fever, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Doctors' ward before", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Midwives' ward", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Doctors' ward after", "v": 1.5}]}
### Semmelweis's insight
Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that doctors came straight from performing autopsies to delivering babies. When a colleague died after cutting himself during an autopsy, with symptoms like childbed fever, Semmelweis suspected "cadaverous particles" were being carried on hands. In 1847 he ordered handwashing with chlorinated lime. Deaths fell sharply.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Doctor does autopsy", "Hands carry germs", "Infects new mother", "Chlorine wash stops it"]}
### Rejection
Many colleagues resented the suggestion that doctors were causing deaths, and germ theory didn't yet exist to explain it. Semmelweis was combative and published his data late. He died in an asylum in 1865, aged 47. Years later, Pasteur and Lister's work vindicated him.
### Today
Hand hygiene remains one of the most effective ways to prevent infection in hospitals. The WHO marks World Hand Hygiene Day on 5 May.
!? The "Semmelweis reflex" now describes the tendency to reject new evidence that contradicts established beliefs.
> Semmelweis saved lives with soap and chlorine before anyone could explain why it worked.

# Anaesthesia
Before anaesthesia, speed was a surgeon's main skill. A leg amputation might be done in under a minute while assistants held the patient down. Alcohol and opium dulled pain only slightly.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1846", "l": "Ether demo, Boston"}, {"y": "1847", "l": "Chloroform used"}, {"y": "1853", "l": "Queen Victoria, birth"}, {"y": "1940s", "l": "Muscle relaxants"}]}
### The demonstration
On 16 October 1846, at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, dentist William Morton gave ether to a patient while surgeon John Warren removed a tumour from his neck. The patient felt no pain. News spread across the world within months.
### Chloroform
In 1847 Scottish obstetrician James Young Simpson introduced chloroform, which was easier to use than ether. Queen Victoria used it during the birth of her eighth child in 1853, helping it gain acceptance. But chloroform could cause sudden heart problems and was later abandoned.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before 1846", "items": ["fast and brutal", "high shock risk", "few operations"]}, "r": {"t": "After", "items": ["slow and careful", "longer surgery", "new procedures"]}}
### Modern anaesthesia
Today's general anaesthesia combines several drugs: one for unconsciousness, one for pain, often one to relax muscles. Monitoring of oxygen, heart and breathing has made it very safe.
!? Scientists still don't fully understand how general anaesthetics switch off consciousness.
> Anaesthesia turned surgery from a race against agony into careful, precise work.

# Penicillin
In September 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from holiday to his lab at St Mary's Hospital in London and noticed that a mould, **Penicillium**, had killed the bacteria around it on a culture plate.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1928", "l": "Fleming sees mould"}, {"y": "1940", "l": "Oxford team tests it"}, {"y": "1941", "l": "First patient treated"}, {"y": "1944", "l": "Mass production"}, {"y": "1945", "l": "Nobel Prize"}]}
### From mould to medicine
Fleming couldn't purify enough to treat people. In 1939–40, at Oxford, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain led a team that extracted and tested it. Mass production in the US during the Second World War made it available for wounded soldiers by D-Day in 1944.
### How it works
Penicillin blocks bacteria from building their cell walls. As they grow, the weakened walls burst. Human cells don't have these walls, so they're unharmed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bacterium grows", "Penicillin blocks wall", "Wall weakens", "Bacterium bursts"]}
### The resistance problem
Fleming himself warned in his 1945 Nobel lecture that underuse could breed resistance. Bacteria evolve: when antibiotics are overused or stopped too early, survivors multiply. Resistant infections now cause over a million deaths a year worldwide.
!! Antibiotics treat colds and flu => They only work on bacteria, not viruses
> Penicillin started the antibiotic era; using antibiotics wisely is how we keep it.

# A Portuguese Nobel
António Egas Moniz (1874–1955) was a neurologist, professor in Lisbon and also a politician and diplomat. He won the 1949 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, Portugal's first Nobel of any kind.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1927", "l": "Cerebral angiography"}, {"y": "1935", "l": "First leucotomy"}, {"y": "1949", "l": "Nobel Prize"}, {"y": "1950s", "l": "Lobotomy declines"}]}
### Angiography: the lasting legacy
In 1927 Moniz injected a contrast dye into the arteries leading to the brain and took X-rays, revealing the blood vessels. This **cerebral angiography** helped locate tumours, aneurysms and strokes. Its modern descendants still guide treatment today.
### The lobotomy
In 1935 Moniz and surgeon Almeida Lima began cutting connections in the frontal lobes to treat severe mental illness, a procedure called leucotomy. Psychiatric hospitals then had few treatments, and some patients seemed calmer. But many were left apathetic or impaired.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Angiography", "items": ["sees brain vessels", "still used, refined"]}, "r": {"t": "Lobotomy", "items": ["damaged personality", "abandoned"]}}
### Why it faded
In the US, Walter Freeman spread a crude, quick version and performed thousands. When the first antipsychotic drug, chlorpromazine, appeared in the 1950s, lobotomies fell out of use. Some campaigners have called for Moniz's prize to be withdrawn; the Nobel committee has not done so.
!? Portugal's second Nobel came in 1998, when José Saramago won for literature.
> Moniz's story shows medicine's light and shadow: a lasting imaging tool and a discredited operation.

# Modern medicine
The past 75 years transformed what medicine can see and do.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1971", "l": "First CT scan"}, {"y": "1980s", "l": "Keyhole surgery spreads"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Targeted cancer pill"}, {"y": "2020", "l": "mRNA vaccines"}]}
### Seeing inside
CT scanning (1971) and MRI (late 1970s) made it possible to see inside the body without cutting it open. Ultrasound made pregnancy checks routine.
### Less cutting
Keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery uses small incisions, a camera and long instruments. Patients recover in days rather than weeks. Robotic systems now assist many operations.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Open surgery", "items": ["large incision", "longer stay"]}, "r": {"t": "Keyhole surgery", "items": ["small cuts", "faster recovery"]}}
### Smarter drugs and vaccines
• **Targeted cancer drugs**: imatinib (2001) turned a deadly leukaemia into a manageable condition
• **Immunotherapy**: helps the immune system attack cancer
• **mRNA vaccines**: give cells instructions to make a harmless virus protein; first approved for COVID-19 in 2020. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman won the 2023 Nobel Prize for the research behind them
• **Transplants**: kidneys, livers, hearts and more, made possible by anti-rejection drugs
!? Hungarian-born Katalin Karikó spent decades struggling for funding before her mRNA work changed medicine.

== bo-nutrition

# What vitamins are
The word comes from "vital amine", coined by Polish biochemist Casimir Funk in 1912, who thought these substances were all amines. Most aren't, so the "e" was dropped and "vitamin" stuck.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fat-soluble (4)", "items": ["A, D, E, K", "stored in liver and fat", "excess can build up"]}, "r": {"t": "Water-soluble (9)", "items": ["C and eight B vitamins", "little storage", "excess mostly in urine"]}}
### What they do
Vitamins don't provide energy. Most act as helpers, called **coenzymes**, that let enzymes do their chemical jobs: releasing energy from food, building DNA, making collagen, clotting blood.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "B vitamins", "b": "release energy from food"}, {"a": "Vitamin C", "b": "builds collagen"}, {"a": "Vitamin K", "b": "helps blood clot"}, {"a": "Vitamin E", "b": "protects cell membranes"}]}
### Why we can't make them
Over evolution, our ancestors lost the ability to make some vitamins because their diet supplied plenty. Humans, along with other apes, guinea pigs and some bats, can't make vitamin C; most other mammals can.
### Minerals are different
Minerals like iron, calcium and zinc are chemical elements, not compounds. Plants take them up from soil and we get them from food. Vitamins are made by living things and can be destroyed by heat, light or air.
!? Vitamin D is unusual: the body can make it, so it's arguably more a hormone than a vitamin.
> Vitamins are tiny helpers that keep the body's chemistry running, and most must come from food.

# Scurvy and vitamin C
Without vitamin C, the body can't make **collagen** properly. Collagen is the protein that holds skin, gums, blood vessels and wounds together. So scurvy causes bleeding gums, loose teeth, easy bruising, old wounds reopening and extreme tiredness.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["No fresh food at sea", "Vitamin C runs out", "Collagen fails", "Gums bleed, wounds open"]}
### Da Gama's voyage
On Vasco da Gama's first voyage to India in 1497–99, scurvy struck badly. Of roughly 170 men who set out, only about 55 returned; many of the deaths are attributed to scurvy. The chronicles describe gums swelling over teeth. Some sailors recovered after eating oranges on the East African coast.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "crew of Vasco da Gama's first voyage (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Set out", "v": 170.0}, {"l": "Returned", "v": 55.0, "hi": true}]}
### Lind's trial
In 1747, Scottish naval surgeon James Lind took 12 sailors with scurvy and gave pairs different treatments: cider, vinegar, seawater, oranges and lemons, and others. The pair given citrus recovered fastest. It's often called one of the first controlled clinical trials.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1497", "l": "Da Gama's crew"}, {"y": "1747", "l": "Lind's trial"}, {"y": "1795", "l": "Navy issues lemon juice"}, {"y": "1932", "l": "Vitamin C isolated"}]}
### Scurvy today
Scurvy still appears occasionally in people with very restricted diets. Signs usually appear after one to three months without vitamin C.
!? British sailors got the nickname "limeys" from the citrus juice issued on ships.
> Scurvy was a collagen failure, and a few pieces of fruit could cure it.

# Vitamin D
When ultraviolet B light hits your skin, it converts a cholesterol-like molecule into vitamin D. The liver and then the kidneys convert it into its active form.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["UVB hits skin", "Skin makes vitamin D", "Liver converts it", "Kidneys activate it", "Gut absorbs calcium"]}
### Why winter matters
UVB only reaches the ground in useful amounts when the sun is high. North of about 40–45°, roughly from northern Portugal and Spain upwards, winter sunlight is too weak for much vitamin D production for several months. Lisbon is at about 38.7°N, so there is somewhat more winter sun, but levels still dip.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 30.0, "hi": 65.0, "unit": "latitude, °N", "items": [{"l": "Lisbon", "v": 38.7}, {"l": "Porto", "v": 41.2, "hi": true}, {"l": "Paris", "v": 48.9}, {"l": "Oslo", "v": 59.9}]}
### Who runs low
• People who spend little time outdoors, or cover their skin
• People with darker skin living far from the equator, since melanin slows production
• Older people, whose skin makes less
• Babies who are breastfed, so many countries advise supplements for them
### Bones and beyond
Severe deficiency causes **rickets** in children, soft bent bones, and osteomalacia in adults. Studies have explored links with immunity, mood and other diseases, but large trials of supplements in people who aren't deficient have mostly been disappointing.
!! Portugal is sunny, so no one lacks vitamin D => Studies find many Portuguese adults have low levels, especially in winter
> Sunlight on skin is the main source of vitamin D, and winter sun often isn't enough.

# Vitamin A
Vitamin A comes in two forms: **retinol**, ready-made in animal foods like liver, eggs and dairy, and **carotenoids** like beta-carotene in orange and green plants, which the body converts as needed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Vitamin A in retina", "Combines with opsin", "Light splits pigment", "Signal to brain"]}
### Seeing in the dark
Your retina's rod cells use a pigment called rhodopsin, made from vitamin A. Light breaks it apart, triggering a nerve signal. Without enough vitamin A, rods can't rebuild their pigment fast enough, and night blindness is one of the first signs of deficiency.
### A global problem
Vitamin A deficiency is a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness in low-income countries and weakens defences against measles and diarrhoea. Supplement programmes have saved many young lives.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Retinol", "items": ["liver, eggs, dairy", "ready to use", "excess can be toxic"]}, "r": {"t": "Beta-carotene", "items": ["carrots, greens", "converted as needed", "safer in excess"]}}
### Too much
Because retinol is stored in the liver, too much is harmful, and high doses are dangerous in pregnancy. Polar bear liver contains so much that Arctic explorers who ate it became seriously ill.
!! Carrots give you night vision => They only help if you're deficient; it was a wartime myth
!? The carrot myth was spread by the British in World War II, reportedly to hide their use of new radar for night-time interceptions.
> Vitamin A is the raw material for the pigment your eyes use to see in dim light.

# B12
Vitamin B12 is made only by certain bacteria and archaea, not by plants or animals. Animals get it from microbes in their gut or food, and store it in meat, eggs and milk. That's why plant foods contain almost none.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Vitamin B12 is needed for", "items": ["Healthy red blood cells", "Nerve insulation", "Making DNA"]}
### What goes wrong without it
• Large, immature red blood cells form, causing **megaloblastic anaemia**: tiredness and breathlessness
• Nerves lose their protective coating, causing numbness, tingling and memory problems, which can become permanent
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["B12 in food", "Stomach releases it", "Binds intrinsic factor", "Absorbed in small intestine"]}
### Absorption can fail
B12 needs a stomach protein called **intrinsic factor** to be absorbed. Pernicious anaemia, an autoimmune condition, destroys it. Older adults with less stomach acid, and people on long-term metformin or acid-reducing drugs, are also at higher risk.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Diet risk", "items": ["vegan diets", "little animal food"]}, "r": {"t": "Absorption risk", "items": ["older age", "some medicines"]}}
### Stored for years
The liver stores several years' worth of B12, so deficiency develops slowly, sometimes years after a diet change.
!? Pernicious anaemia was fatal until the 1920s, when doctors found that eating large amounts of raw liver cured it, a discovery that won a Nobel Prize.
> B12 comes from microbes, reaches us through animal foods, and its lack slowly harms blood and nerves.

# Iron
About two-thirds of the body's iron sits in **haemoglobin**, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Iron is also stored in the liver and used in muscles and enzymes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Haem iron", "items": ["meat, fish", "well absorbed"]}, "r": {"t": "Non-haem iron", "items": ["beans, greens, grains", "less absorbed"]}}
### Absorption is tricky
The body has no active way to get rid of iron, so it controls absorption tightly. Haem iron from animal foods is absorbed well. Non-haem iron from plants is absorbed less and is strongly affected by what you eat alongside it.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Vitamin C", "b": "boosts absorption"}, {"a": "Meat with plants", "b": "boosts absorption"}, {"a": "Tea, coffee", "b": "tannins reduce it"}, {"a": "Calcium", "b": "reduces it at same meal"}]}
### Deficiency
Iron deficiency is the most common nutrient deficiency in the world. It causes anaemia: tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness and sometimes cravings to chew ice. Women of childbearing age, pregnant women, young children and frequent blood donors are most at risk.
### Too much
Iron overload is harmful too. **Haemochromatosis**, an inherited condition common in people of Northern European descent, makes the body absorb too much, damaging liver, heart and joints. Iron supplements should be taken only when needed.
!? The Portuguese "feijoada" pairs beans with meat, which helps the iron in the beans to be absorbed.
> What you eat with a plant-based meal can matter as much as how much iron it contains.

# Calcium
The body holds about 1 kg of calcium, almost all locked in bones and teeth as hydroxyapatite crystals. The remaining 1% is vital for nerves, muscle contraction and blood clotting.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "where the body's calcium is, %", "items": [{"l": "Bones and teeth", "v": 99.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Blood and cells", "v": 1.0}]}
### Bone as a bank
Blood calcium is kept within a tight range. When intake is low, hormones withdraw calcium from bone. Over years, this weakens the skeleton. Bone mass peaks in the late 20s, so childhood and youth are when the "bank" is filled.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Blood calcium dips", "Hormone signal", "Bone releases calcium", "Blood level restored"]}
### Sources
• Dairy: milk, yoghurt and cheese
• Canned sardines with their soft bones, a Portuguese favourite
• Tofu set with calcium, almonds, white beans
• Leafy greens like kale and couve galega; spinach has calcium but binds most of it
### Osteoporosis
Bones thin with age, especially in women after menopause, when oestrogen falls. Osteoporosis makes fractures of hip, spine and wrist more likely. Weight-bearing exercise, enough calcium and vitamin D and not smoking all help keep bones strong.
!! More calcium always means stronger bones => Beyond enough, extra calcium adds little; exercise matters too
> Bones are a calcium bank, filled when young and drawn on for life.

# Salt
Salt is sodium chloride, about 40% sodium. Sodium controls fluid balance and lets nerves fire. The WHO's adult limit of 5 g of salt a day is about one level teaspoon.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "salt per day, grams (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "WHO limit", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Portugal (est.)", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}]}
### Where it hides
Estimates for Portuguese adults range from about 7 to over 10 g a day. Most salt comes not from the shaker but from foods we buy. Bread, cheese, processed meats like chouriço and presunto, soups, sauces and ready meals are major sources.
ex: Salt label 1.5 g per 100 g of bread | 4 slices ≈ 150 g | ≈ 2.3 g salt, almost half the daily limit
### Why it matters
High salt intake raises blood pressure, a leading cause of stroke and heart disease. Portugal has historically had high stroke rates, and salt has been one target of public health efforts.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["High salt intake", "Body holds more water", "Blood pressure rises", "Higher stroke risk"]}
### Portugal acts
In 2009 Portugal passed a law capping salt in bread, one of the first in Europe, and later agreements lowered the limit further. Bakers reduced salt gradually so people barely noticed.
!? Taste buds adapt: after a few weeks of eating less salt, people often find their old foods too salty.
> Most of our salt is hidden in everyday foods, and cutting it lowers blood pressure.

# Fibre
Fibre is the part of plant foods that our own enzymes can't digest. That's exactly why it's useful: it passes to the large intestine, where it feeds trillions of bacteria.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Soluble fibre", "items": ["oats, beans, apples", "forms a gel", "lowers cholesterol"]}, "r": {"t": "Insoluble fibre", "items": ["whole grains, skins", "adds bulk", "keeps you regular"]}}
### Feeding your microbes
Gut bacteria ferment fibre into **short-chain fatty acids**, like butyrate. These feed the cells lining the colon, help control inflammation and may affect appetite and blood sugar.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Eat fibre", "Reaches the colon", "Bacteria ferment it", "Helpful fatty acids made"]}
### The evidence
A large 2019 review for the WHO, published in The Lancet, found people eating 25–29 g of fibre a day had about 15–30% lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer, compared with those eating little.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "fibre, grams per day (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Typical European", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "Recommended", "v": 27.0, "hi": true}]}
### Easy sources
• Beans, chickpeas and lentils, staples in Portuguese cooking
• Whole grain bread, like broa de milho
• Fruit and vegetables with skins
• Nuts and seeds
Increase fibre gradually and drink water, as a sudden jump can cause wind and bloating.
!? Eating a wide variety of plant foods is linked to a more diverse community of gut bacteria.
> Fibre isn't for you, it's for your gut bacteria, and they return the favour.

# Food first
A varied diet of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish and some dairy or alternatives supplies most nutrients most people need. Whole foods also contain fibre and many other compounds that supplements don't.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Vitamin D in winter, for some", "Folic acid before pregnancy", "B12 on vegan diets", "Iron if deficient", "As advised by a doctor"]}
### When supplements help
Some groups have well-established reasons to supplement. **Folic acid** taken before conception and in early pregnancy greatly reduces the risk of spina bifida. Vitamin D is advised for breastfed babies and many people in winter.
### When more isn't better
Large trials of high-dose antioxidants found no benefit and sometimes harm. Beta-carotene supplements raised lung cancer risk in smokers. Fat-soluble vitamins can build up to toxic levels.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Whole foods", "items": ["many nutrients", "fibre included", "hard to overdo"]}, "r": {"t": "Supplements", "items": ["one or few nutrients", "no fibre", "easy to overdo"]}}
!! Supplements are a safety net, so take lots => Excess can harm; take them for a clear reason
!? Water-soluble vitamins taken in excess largely leave in urine, which is why some critics call high-dose supplements "expensive urine".
> Eat a varied diet first; use supplements for specific, known gaps.

== bo-stress

# The stress response
When you face a threat, your body runs two systems in parallel, one fast and one slower.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fast: nerves", "items": ["seconds", "adrenaline", "heart races"]}, "r": {"t": "Slow: hormones", "items": ["minutes", "cortisol", "fuel released"]}}
### The fast lane
The **sympathetic nervous system** fires within a second. It triggers the adrenal glands, above the kidneys, to release adrenaline. Heart rate and blood pressure rise, airways widen, pupils dilate, blood shifts to the muscles and digestion pauses.
### The slower lane
The brain's hypothalamus also signals the pituitary gland, which signals the adrenals to release **cortisol**. This is called the HPA axis. Cortisol raises blood sugar for energy, sharpens memory for the event and dampens inflammation. It peaks about 20–30 minutes after a stressor.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Brain spots threat", "Hypothalamus", "Pituitary", "Adrenal glands", "Cortisol released"]}
### Switching off
Cortisol also feeds back to the brain to turn the system down once the threat passes. In chronic stress this off switch works less well.
!? The adrenaline rush can make you physically stronger and less sensitive to pain for a short time.
> Stress is an energy mobilisation system: fast with adrenaline, sustained with cortisol.

# Good stress
Psychologists sometimes call helpful stress **eustress**. It's the energising pressure before a match, a presentation or a deadline you care about.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Acute stress", "items": ["short burst", "sharpens focus", "recovery follows"]}, "r": {"t": "Chronic stress", "items": ["never switches off", "wears body down", "sleep suffers"]}}
### Why short stress helps
In the short term, stress hormones improve attention, speed up reactions and strengthen memories of important events. That's why you remember exactly where you were during a big moment.
### Growth through challenge
Muscles, bones and the immune system all grow stronger in response to manageable stress followed by recovery. Exercise is itself a stressor. The same principle seems to apply to the mind: people who face moderate challenges often cope better later.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Challenge", "Stress response", "Recovery", "Stronger, more resilient"]}
### When it turns bad
The problem is **allostatic load**, the wear and tear from a stress system that keeps running: ongoing work pressure, money worries, caring for a sick relative, loneliness. Without recovery, the same hormones that help in bursts begin to cause harm.
!! All stress is bad for you => Brief stress with recovery helps you adapt and grow
> Stress is healthy when it comes in waves with recovery in between.

# The performance curve
In 1908, psychologists Robert Yerkes and John Dodson studied mice learning a task while receiving mild electric shocks. Performance improved as shocks got stronger, up to a point, then fell. The principle became a famous inverted-U curve.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "performance vs arousal (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Too calm", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Moderate", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Too stressed", "v": 35.0}]}
### The sweet spot depends on the task
• **Simple or well-practised tasks**: higher arousal helps, like a sprinter psyching up
• **Complex or new tasks**: lower arousal works better, because high stress narrows attention and blocks flexible thinking
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simple task", "items": ["peak at high arousal", "e.g. lifting, sprinting"]}, "r": {"t": "Complex task", "items": ["peak at low arousal", "e.g. exams, surgery"]}}
### In everyday life
That's why a bit of nerves helps before a talk, but panic makes you forget your lines. Practice moves tasks into the "simple" category: well-rehearsed actions survive stress better. Pilots, surgeons and athletes rehearse emergencies for this reason.
!? The modern inverted-U is a simplification of the original study, but the basic pattern holds in many settings.
> A little stress lifts performance; the harder the task, the less stress it takes to tip over.

# Stress and the heart
Each time stress hits, the heart beats faster and blood vessels tighten. Repeated over years, this can contribute to high blood pressure and damage to artery walls.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chronic stress", "Higher blood pressure", "Inflammation", "Artery damage", "Heart disease risk"]}
### Indirect effects
Stress also pushes behaviour in unhealthy directions: smoking, drinking, eating poorly, sleeping badly and skipping exercise. Large international studies, like INTERHEART, found psychosocial stress linked to higher heart attack risk, alongside classic factors like smoking.
### Broken heart syndrome
**Takotsubo cardiomyopathy** was first described in Japan in 1990. After a sudden emotional or physical shock, like bereavement, part of the heart's main chamber weakens and balloons, resembling a Japanese octopus trap, a takotsubo. Symptoms mimic a heart attack, but arteries are clear.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Heart attack", "items": ["blocked artery", "permanent damage"]}, "r": {"t": "Takotsubo", "items": ["arteries clear", "usually recovers"]}}
### Who gets it
About 90% of cases are in women, mostly after menopause. Most recover within weeks, though it can be serious.
!? Heart attacks are more common on Monday mornings, and spike after major events like earthquakes and tense football matches.
> Chronic stress strains the heart over years, and extreme shock can stun it in minutes.

# Stress and the gut
Your gut has its own nervous system, the **enteric nervous system**, with around 500 million neurons, often called the "second brain". It talks to the brain mainly through the vagus nerve, in both directions.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Brain senses stress", "Signals via vagus nerve", "Gut changes speed", "Gut signals back to brain"]}
### What stress does
• Slows the stomach, causing nausea or loss of appetite
• Speeds the colon, causing urgency or diarrhoea
• Increases sensitivity, so normal gut activity feels painful
• Can make the gut lining slightly leakier
### Butterflies
The fluttering feeling before an exam comes from blood being diverted away from the gut and changes in muscle activity, driven by adrenaline. It's harmless.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Butterflies", "b": "blood shifts away from gut"}, {"a": "Nausea", "b": "stomach slows down"}, {"a": "Urgent toilet trip", "b": "colon speeds up"}]}
### Irritable bowel syndrome
IBS, which affects around 1 in 10 people, involves an oversensitive gut-brain connection. Stress often worsens symptoms. Treatments that calm the nervous system, like gut-directed hypnotherapy and cognitive behavioural therapy, can help.
!? About 90% of the body's serotonin, a chemical messenger, is made in the gut, not the brain.
> Gut and brain talk constantly, so stress is often felt first in the stomach.

# Stress and immunity
Stress affects immunity in a two-phase way, described in a major review of over 300 studies in 2004.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Brief stress", "items": ["immune cells mobilise", "ready for injury"]}, "r": {"t": "Chronic stress", "items": ["immunity suppressed", "slower healing"]}}
### Why brief stress boosts it
In evolution, a threat often meant a wound. So acute stress moves immune cells into the bloodstream and toward the skin, ready to fight infection.
### Why chronic stress harms
Prolonged cortisol suppresses parts of the immune system. At the same time, chronic stress can promote low-level inflammation, which is linked to many diseases.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Long-term stress", "Cortisol stays high", "Immune cells less active", "More infections, slow healing"]}
### Evidence
• Dental students given a small wound took longer to heal it during exams than during holidays
• In a famous series of experiments, volunteers exposed to a cold virus were more likely to get ill if they reported more stress
• Carers of people with dementia showed weaker responses to flu vaccines
### Sleep is a link
Stress disrupts sleep, and poor sleep weakens immunity: people sleeping under six hours are more likely to catch colds after virus exposure.
!? The research field studying how mind, nerves and immunity interact is called psychoneuroimmunology.
> Short stress prepares the immune system; long stress wears it down.

# Rest and digest
The autonomic nervous system has two main branches that work like an accelerator and brake.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sympathetic", "items": ["fight or flight", "heart speeds up", "digestion paused"]}, "r": {"t": "Parasympathetic", "items": ["rest and digest", "heart slows", "digestion restarts"]}}
### The vagus nerve
The **vagus nerve** is the main parasympathetic nerve. It runs from the brainstem down through the neck to the heart, lungs and gut. It slows the heart, stimulates digestion and helps calm inflammation.
### Why breathing works
Heart rate naturally rises slightly when you breathe in and falls when you breathe out. This is **heart rate variability**. Slow breathing with longer out-breaths, around 5–6 breaths a minute, strengthens the parasympathetic side.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Slow, long out-breath", "Vagus nerve activates", "Heart rate slows", "Body calms"]}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "breaths per minute (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Normal at rest", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "Calming breath", "v": 6.0, "hi": true}]}
### Other triggers
• Splashing cold water on the face, which triggers the diving reflex
• Humming or singing, which involves the vagus nerve
• Social connection and feeling safe
!? Heart rate variability is now measured by many smartwatches as an estimate of recovery.
> Your breath is one of the few direct handles you have on your nervous system.

# What helps
Not all stress relief is equal. Some approaches have solid evidence; others are mostly comforting habits.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Move: exercise burns off stress", "Sleep: resets the stress system", "Nature: lowers stress signs", "People: support buffers stress", "Limit news and alcohol"]}
### Why they work
• **Exercise** uses the energy stress mobilises and reduces anxiety
• **Sleep** restores emotional regulation; one bad night makes the brain's alarm centre more reactive
• **Nature**: studies find walks in green spaces lower heart rate and self-reported stress compared with urban walks
• **Social support** dampens the cortisol response
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Helps", "items": ["exercise, sleep", "friends, nature"]}, "r": {"t": "Backfires", "items": ["alcohol", "doom-scrolling"]}}
### The alcohol trap
Alcohol feels relaxing at first, but as it wears off it causes a rebound of anxiety and disrupts sleep. Over time, heavy drinking raises baseline stress.
### News and screens
Studies after major disasters found that people who watched many hours of coverage reported more acute stress than some who were directly exposed. Limiting news to set times helps.
!? In Japan, "shinrin-yoku", or forest bathing, is promoted as public health practice.
> The best stress relievers are simple: move, sleep, get outside and see people.

# Reappraisal
Reappraisal means changing how you interpret a situation, not ignoring it. Applied to stress, it means seeing the physical signs as fuel, not a warning.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "I'm panicking", "r": "My body is getting ready"}, {"w": "Stress will ruin this", "r": "Stress gives me energy"}]}
### The studies
In studies at Harvard led by Jeremy Jamieson, students told before a test that arousal can improve performance scored better on the maths section of a practice exam than those who weren't. In other experiments, people who reappraised stress showed healthier cardiovascular responses: the heart pumped more efficiently and blood vessels stayed more relaxed.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Threat response", "items": ["vessels tighten", "feel overwhelmed"]}, "r": {"t": "Challenge response", "items": ["vessels relax", "feel energised"]}}
### "I am excited"
Anxiety and excitement feel physically similar. Experiments by Alison Wood Brooks found that saying "I am excited" before singing or public speaking improved performance more than trying to calm down.
### Limits
Reappraisal works best for performance stress. It isn't a cure for chronic stressors like poverty or abuse, which need practical change and support.
!? Trying to calm down when you're highly aroused is hard; shifting to excitement takes less effort because the body stays aroused.
> The same pounding heart can be a threat or a boost, depending on how you read it.

# Box breathing
Box breathing, also called square breathing, is a simple paced-breathing technique used by soldiers, pilots and athletes to stay calm under pressure.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Breathe in for 4", "Hold for 4", "Breathe out for 4", "Hold for 4"]}
### Why it works
Counting gives the mind something to focus on, breaking anxious thought loops. Slowing breathing to around 4 breaths per minute activates the parasympathetic system. The holds may also let carbon dioxide rise slightly, which counters the light-headedness of fast breathing.
ex: 4 s in + 4 s hold + 4 s out + 4 s hold | = 16 s per cycle | ≈ 4 breaths a minute
### How to do it
• Sit upright and breathe out fully
• Breathe in through the nose, counting slowly to four
• Hold, count to four
• Breathe out through the mouth for four
• Hold empty for four; repeat
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Box breathing", "items": ["equal counts", "focus and steadiness"]}, "r": {"t": "Longer out-breath", "items": ["e.g. in 4, out 6", "calming, for sleep"]}}
### Adjust as needed
If holding feels uncomfortable, shorten the counts to three. For winding down before sleep, a longer exhale may work better. Feeling dizzy is a sign to slow down or stop.
!? Studies comparing breathing exercises found even five minutes a day of slow, controlled breathing improved mood over a month.
> Four counts, four sides: a calm rhythm you can carry anywhere.

== bo-pain

# Pain is a protector
Pain is unpleasant on purpose. It grabs your attention, makes you pull away from danger and then makes you rest an injured part so it can heal. A sprained ankle that hurts is an ankle you won't run on.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Damage or threat", "Pain signal", "Pull away", "Guard and rest", "Healing"]}
### Life without pain
A few people are born unable to feel pain, often because of a faulty gene for a sodium channel (called Nav1.7) that pain nerves need to fire. As children they may bite their tongues badly, break bones without noticing, or burn themselves on hot surfaces. Joints wear out early because they never shift position to ease the strain.
Leprosy shows the same thing from another angle. The bacteria damage nerves in the hands and feet, and much of the disfigurement comes from unnoticed cuts, burns and infections, not from the bacteria themselves.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "With pain", "items": ["limp on a bad ankle", "shift when sitting", "notice burns fast"]}, "r": {"t": "Without pain", "items": ["walk on fractures", "joints wear out", "injuries go unseen"]}}
### Pain as a teacher
Pain also helps you learn. One encounter with a hot pan or a nettle is usually enough to change your behaviour for life. That's why the memory of pain can be so vivid.
!? Researchers studying people who can't feel pain found the Nav1.7 channel, and drug companies are now trying to block it to make new painkillers.
> Pain is an alarm, and people born without it show how badly we need it.

# Nociceptors
Nociceptors are bare nerve endings spread through your skin, muscles, joints, bones and organs. Unlike touch receptors, they ignore gentle stimuli and only respond when something is strong enough to cause harm.
### Three kinds of danger
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Heat", "b": "above about 43 °C"}, {"a": "Mechanical", "b": "crushing, cutting, pinching"}, {"a": "Chemical", "b": "acids, toxins, inflammation"}, {"a": "Polymodal", "b": "respond to all of these"}]}
The heat sensor is a molecule called **TRPV1**. Capsaicin, the chemical in chilli peppers, switches on the same sensor, which is why chilli feels hot even at room temperature. Menthol does the opposite trick on a cold sensor. David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering these temperature and touch sensors.
### The route to the brain
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nociceptor fires", "Spinal cord", "Thalamus", "Cortex: 'ouch'"]}
The spinal cord can act before the brain knows anything. Touch a hot pan and a reflex loop in the cord pulls your hand back; the feeling of pain arrives a fraction of a second later.
!? The brain itself has no nociceptors, so surgeons can operate on brain tissue while the patient is awake. Headaches come from the membranes and blood vessels around it.
> Nociceptors are danger detectors, tuned to ignore the gentle and react to the harmful.

# Fast and slow pain
The two waves of pain come from two types of nerve fibre. **A-delta fibres** are thin but wrapped in a fatty coat called myelin, which lets signals jump along quickly. **C fibres** are thinner and have no myelin, so their signals crawl.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "A-delta fibres", "items": ["myelinated", "about 5–30 m/s", "sharp, well located"]}, "r": {"t": "C fibres", "items": ["no myelin", "about 0.5–2 m/s", "dull, burning ache"]}}
ex: Toe to brain ≈ 1.5 m | A-delta at 20 m/s ≈ 0.08 s | C fibre at 1 m/s ≈ 1.5 s
### Two jobs
The first pain tells you exactly where and acts as a "move now" signal. The second pain is vaguer and longer, and it pushes you to protect the injured spot. C fibres make up most of your pain nerves, so the ache usually outlasts the sting.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "signal speed, m/s (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Touch fibres", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "A-delta", "v": 20.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "C fibres", "v": 1.0}]}
### Why rubbing and cold help
Ice slows nerve conduction, especially in C fibres, which is one reason a cold pack eases the throbbing after a knock. Local anaesthetics at the dentist work by blocking the sodium channels all these fibres need to fire.
> Sharp first pain rides fast insulated fibres; the slow ache follows on bare ones.

# The brain decides
Nociceptors send signals, but pain is an experience the brain builds. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines it as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to actual or possible tissue damage. The key word is "experience".
### Turning the volume up
In 1995 the British Medical Journal reported a builder who jumped onto a 15 cm nail that went right through his boot. He was in agony and needed strong painkillers. When the boot was removed, the nail had passed between his toes without touching him.
### Turning it down
The American doctor Henry Beecher noticed during the Second World War that many badly wounded soldiers asked for little or no morphine. For them, the wound meant leaving the battlefield alive. Civilians with similar surgical wounds asked for far more.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Turns pain up", "items": ["fear and worry", "focusing on it", "threatening meaning"]}, "r": {"t": "Turns pain down", "items": ["distraction", "safety, calm", "hopeful meaning"]}}
### The brain's own brake
The brain can send signals down the spinal cord that dampen incoming pain. A region in the brainstem called the periaqueductal grey releases **endorphins**, the body's own opioids, which block pain signals.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Injury signal rises", "Brain weighs context", "Brainstem sends brake", "Pain dialled down"]}
!! Pain is a direct measure of damage => Pain reflects how much danger the brain thinks you're in
> Pain is the brain's verdict on danger, not a readout of tissue damage.

# The gate theory
In 1965 the Canadian psychologist Ronald Melzack and the British neuroscientist Patrick Wall published the **gate control theory** in the journal Science. It changed how scientists thought about pain.
### How the gate works
In the back of the spinal cord, pain fibres pass their signals to nerve cells that relay them to the brain. Thick touch fibres connect to the same area and switch on small inhibitory cells that quieten the relay. When you rub a bump, touch traffic partly "closes the gate".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bump: pain fibres fire", "You rub the spot", "Touch fibres fire", "Gate partly closes", "Less pain to brain"]}
### Signals from above
The gate is also controlled from the top. Signals coming down from the brain can open or close it, which helps explain why mood and attention change pain so much.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rubbing", "b": "touch signals close the gate"}, {"a": "TENS machine", "b": "mild electric pulses on skin"}, {"a": "Distraction", "b": "brain closes it from above"}, {"a": "Anxiety", "b": "can hold it open"}]}
### Is it right?
The exact wiring Melzack and Wall proposed has been revised, and the real circuits are more complex. But the core idea, that the spinal cord actively filters pain rather than just passing it on, has held up well. It also inspired TENS machines, small devices that send gentle pulses through the skin.
> Pain signals pass through a filter in the spinal cord, and touch and the brain can both narrow it.

# Referred pain
Referred pain happens because of **convergence**. Nerves from internal organs and nerves from the skin often feed into the same nerve cells in the spinal cord. The brain receives one signal and has to guess where it came from.
### Why the brain guesses wrong
Throughout life, signals on that pathway almost always come from skin and muscle, not from the heart or gut. So when an organ is in trouble, the brain maps the pain onto the familiar body surface.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Heart", "b": "left arm, jaw, chest"}, {"a": "Diaphragm", "b": "tip of the shoulder"}, {"a": "Appendix (early)", "b": "around the navel"}, {"a": "Kidney", "b": "side and groin"}]}
### Why the heart points to the arm
The heart grows in the embryo near the neck and arms before moving down into the chest. It keeps nerve connections at the same spinal levels as the arm, roughly the upper chest segments.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Heart lacks oxygen", "Shares spinal level", "Brain misreads source", "Pain felt in left arm"]}
### Taking it seriously
Pain or pressure in the chest, arm, jaw, neck or back, especially with breathlessness, sweating or nausea, can mean a heart attack. Women and older people more often have less typical symptoms. In Portugal and across the EU, the emergency number is 112.
!? Brain freeze is also referred pain: the cold is on the roof of your mouth, but you feel it in your forehead.
> Referred pain is the brain mislabelling a signal that shares wiring with another part of the body.

# Phantom limbs
The French surgeon Ambroise Paré described amputees feeling their missing limbs in the 1500s. The American doctor Silas Weir Mitchell coined the term "phantom limb" after treating soldiers from the US Civil War. Most amputees feel a phantom at some point, and many experience phantom pain.
### Why it happens
The brain keeps a map of the body in a strip of cortex. After amputation, the hand area no longer receives input, and neighbouring areas can spread into it. The face area sits right next to the hand area, so some people feel touches on their cheek as touches on the missing hand.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Limb removed", "Brain map loses input", "Neighbours invade", "Phantom sensations"]}
### Mirror therapy
In the 1990s the neuroscientist V. S. Ramachandran built a simple mirror box. The patient puts their intact arm on one side; its reflection appears where the missing arm would be. Moving the real hand makes the phantom seem to move too.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before mirror", "items": ["phantom feels stuck", "clenched, cramped", "brain gets no update"]}, "r": {"t": "With mirror", "items": ["eyes see it move", "cramp can release", "brain map updated"]}}
Some patients find their phantom cramps ease. Results vary, and larger studies show mixed but often helpful effects. Virtual reality versions now do the same thing.
!! Phantom pain is imaginary => It is real pain, generated by real changes in the brain and nerves
> A phantom limb shows that the body you feel is a map in your brain.

# Painkillers
Painkillers act at different points along the pain pathway. Some work at the injury, some on the nerves, and some in the brain and spinal cord.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Brain and spinal cord: opioids", "Nerve fibres: local anaesthetics", "Injury site: anti-inflammatories"]}
### Anti-inflammatories
When tissue is damaged, enzymes called **COX** make prostaglandins, chemicals that cause swelling and make nociceptors more sensitive. Ibuprofen, aspirin and naproxen block COX, so the nerves calm down. Aspirin's ancestor is salicin from willow bark, used for centuries; Bayer launched aspirin in 1899.
### Paracetamol
Paracetamol relieves pain and fever but has little anti-inflammatory effect. Surprisingly, how it works is still not fully understood; it seems to act mainly in the brain and spinal cord. Too much is dangerous to the liver, so it's important to follow the label.
### Opioids
Morphine, isolated from opium in the early 1800s, mimics the body's endorphins. Opioids are powerful for severe acute pain but can slow breathing and lead to dependence, and they generally work poorly for long-term pain.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ibuprofen", "b": "blocks COX at the injury"}, {"a": "Paracetamol", "b": "acts centrally, not fully known"}, {"a": "Morphine", "b": "copies endorphins"}, {"a": "Lidocaine", "b": "silences the nerve"}]}
!? Many over-the-counter painkillers sit in the World Health Organization's list of essential medicines, alongside morphine.
> Painkillers either calm the injury, silence the nerve or turn down the brain's pain signal.

# Placebo
A placebo has no active drug, yet it can produce real relief. The effect comes from **expectation**: believing relief is on its way changes what the brain does.
### The chemistry
In 1978 researchers found that placebo pain relief after dental surgery could be partly cancelled by naloxone, a drug that blocks opioid receptors. That showed the brain releases its own opioids when it expects relief. Brain scans have since shown placebos reducing activity in pain-processing areas.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Expect relief", "Brain releases opioids", "Pain signals dampened", "Feel better"]}
### What boosts it
• Injections tend to beat pills, and two pills beat one
• Branded or pricier-looking pills often work better than plain ones
• A warm, confident clinician strengthens it
• It can work even when people are told it's a placebo, at least for some conditions
### The dark twin
The **nocebo** effect is the reverse: expecting harm makes symptoms worse. In drug trials, people taking placebos often report the side effects listed on the leaflet.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Placebo helps", "items": ["pain", "nausea", "how you feel"]}, "r": {"t": "Placebo can't", "items": ["shrink tumours", "cure infections", "mend bones"]}}
!! Placebo means it's all in your head => The relief is real and has measurable brain chemistry behind it
> Expectation alone can switch on the brain's own painkillers.

# Chronic pain
Acute pain is an alarm about a current injury. **Chronic pain** lasts more than about three months, often after tissues have healed. Then the alarm itself becomes the problem.
### An oversensitive system
With repeated pain signals, nerve cells in the spinal cord and brain become more responsive, a process called **central sensitisation**. The volume knob gets stuck high.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hyperalgesia", "b": "painful things hurt more"}, {"a": "Allodynia", "b": "gentle touch hurts"}, {"a": "Spreading", "b": "pain beyond the first area"}]}
### How common
A large European survey in 2006 found roughly 1 in 5 adults had chronic pain. A Portuguese national study in 2012 found an even higher share, about a third of adults. Back pain, arthritis and headache are among the most common causes.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Pain", "Less movement", "Stiffness, poor sleep", "Worry and low mood"]}
### What helps
• Graded movement and exercise, building up slowly
• Learning how pain works, which itself can reduce fear and pain
• Psychological therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy
• Medicines chosen carefully; opioids usually help little long term
Treatment usually aims to improve daily life rather than remove all pain.
!? Since 2022 the WHO's disease classification (ICD-11) has listed chronic pain as a condition in its own right, not just a symptom.
> Chronic pain is often a nervous system stuck on high alert, and it is treated by retraining that system.

== bo-odd

# Why we yawn
A yawn lasts about six seconds: a wide gape, a deep breath in, stretched jaw muscles and a shorter breath out. Humans start yawning before birth, from about 11 weeks into pregnancy.
### Not about oxygen
The old idea was that yawning brings in extra oxygen. In the 1980s the psychologist Robert Provine tested it: people breathing air with extra oxygen or carbon dioxide yawned just as often.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Yawning tops up oxygen", "r": "Oxygen levels don't change it"}]}
### The cooling idea
One leading theory is that yawning cools the brain. A big gulp of air and a stretch that pumps blood through the skull may swap warm blood for cooler blood. Studies found people yawn less in very hot conditions and less when holding a cold pack to the forehead.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Brain warms slightly", "Deep yawn", "Cooler blood and air", "More alert"]}
### Catching yawns
Seeing, hearing or reading about a yawn triggers one in many adults. Children rarely catch yawns before about age four or five. Some studies suggest we catch yawns more easily from family and friends than from strangers, which hints at a link with social bonding.

# Hiccups
A hiccup is a reflex. The **diaphragm**, the dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, suddenly jerks down. Air rushes in, and about a thirtieth of a second later the vocal cords snap shut, making the "hic".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nerve irritated", "Diaphragm spasms", "Air rushes in", "Vocal cords snap shut", "Hic"]}
### Common triggers
• Eating or drinking quickly, or swallowing air
• Fizzy drinks, alcohol, or very hot or spicy food
• A sudden change in temperature
• Excitement or stress
### Why we have them
Unborn babies hiccup a lot, from the first trimester. Ideas include practising breathing muscles, or helping babies clear air from the stomach after feeding. One theory links hiccups to the gill-breathing reflexes of our distant ancestors. None is proven.
### Charles Osborne
The record belongs to Charles Osborne, an American farmer who started hiccuping in 1922 after a fall and continued until 1990, about a year before he died. He married twice and had eight children during that time.
ex: 68 years ≈ 36 million minutes | Estimated total ≈ 430 million hiccups | ≈ 12 hiccups a minute on average

### Do the cures work?
Holding your breath raises carbon dioxide, which tends to calm the diaphragm. Sipping cold water or swallowing sugar may stimulate the nerves involved. Evidence is weak, but they're harmless. Hiccups lasting more than two days need a doctor.
> A hiccup is a diaphragm spasm cut short by the vocal cords slamming shut.

# Your stomach lining
Stomach acid is mostly hydrochloric acid, with a pH of about 1.5 to 3.5. It kills most microbes in food and unfolds proteins so the enzyme **pepsin** can chop them up. Your stomach makes about 1.5 to 2 litres of gastric juice a day.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 14.0, "unit": "pH", "items": [{"l": "Stomach", "v": 2.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Lemon", "v": 2.5}, {"l": "Water", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Bleach", "v": 12.5}]}
### Why the stomach doesn't digest itself
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Acid and pepsin", "Mucus gel layer", "Bicarbonate buffer", "Lining cells, tightly sealed", "Good blood supply"]}
A thick mucus layer slows acid down, and bicarbonate trapped in it keeps the surface close to neutral. The lining cells are joined by tight seals. Even so, they take damage, so they're replaced every few days.
### When the defence fails
Stomach ulcers were long blamed on stress and spicy food. In the 1980s the Australian doctors Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed most are caused by a bacterium, **Helicobacter pylori**, which weakens the lining. Marshall drank a culture of it to prove the point and developed gastritis. They won the 2005 Nobel Prize.

!? Today most ulcers caused by H. pylori can be cured with a short course of antibiotics.
> The stomach survives its own acid by constantly rebuilding a slimy, alkaline shield.

# Fingerprints
Fingerprint ridges form between about 10 and 16 weeks of pregnancy. A deep layer of skin grows faster than the layers around it and buckles into ridges, a bit like a rug pushed against a wall. By about week 17 the pattern is fixed for life.
### Why twins differ
Genes set the broad type of pattern, so twins' prints can look similar. But the fine details depend on tiny local differences: the baby's position, pressure from the fluid and the blood flow in each finger. No two people have been found with identical prints.

### What they're for
• Ridges may help grip on wet or rough surfaces by channelling water away
• They seem to amplify vibrations as a finger slides, helping you feel fine textures
• On smooth dry glass they actually reduce contact slightly, so the grip idea is debated
### Fingerprints and crime
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1880", "l": "Faulds: prints for ID"}, {"y": "1892", "l": "Galton's book"}, {"y": "1892", "l": "Argentina: first case"}, {"y": "1901", "l": "Scotland Yard adopts"}]}
In 1892, in Argentina, police official Juan Vucetich's system helped solve a murder using a bloody fingerprint, the first case of its kind.
!? Koalas have fingerprints so similar to ours that they could, in theory, confuse a crime scene investigator.
> Fingerprints are random buckles in the skin made before birth, so even identical twins differ.

# Funny bone
The "funny bone" isn't a bone. At the inner side of your elbow, the **ulnar nerve** runs through a groove behind a bony bump on the upper arm bone. There it is covered by little more than skin and fat, with no muscle to cushion it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Elbow knocks a table", "Ulnar nerve squashed", "Nerve fires at random", "Tingling to little finger"]}
### Why the little finger?
The ulnar nerve carries sensation from the little finger and half of the ring finger. When it's hit, the brain assumes the signal came from where that nerve usually reports, so you feel it in the fingers, not the elbow.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ulnar nerve", "b": "little and half ring finger"}, {"a": "Median nerve", "b": "thumb side of the palm"}, {"a": "Radial nerve", "b": "back of the hand"}]}
### Where the name comes from
The likely origin is a pun: the upper arm bone is the **humerus**, which sounds like "humorous". The odd, electric feeling helps the joke along.
### When it isn't funny
Leaning on your elbows for long periods or keeping them bent tightly, such as holding a phone for hours, can irritate the nerve. Called cubital tunnel syndrome, it can cause numbness or weakness in the ring and little fingers.
!! The funny bone is a bone => It's a nerve, the ulnar nerve, lying almost unprotected
> Hitting your funny bone means hitting a nerve, and your brain feels it where that nerve normally reports from.

# Taller in the morning
Between your 24 movable vertebrae sit 23 **intervertebral discs**. Each has a tough outer ring and a gel-like centre that is mostly water. Together they make up about a quarter of the spine's length.
### Squeeze and recover
When you stand and sit, your body weight presses water out of the discs and they flatten slightly. Lying down at night takes the load off, and the discs soak water back up like a sponge.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Wake: discs full", "Day: weight squeezes", "Evening: shorter", "Night: discs refill"]}
ex: 23 discs | lose about 0.5 mm each | ≈ 1 cm shorter by evening
### Astronauts grow
In space, with no weight pressing down, the discs expand further. Astronauts can grow up to about 3% taller, around 5 cm, which can cause back pain. They shrink back on return to Earth.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "height gain, cm (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Overnight", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Months in orbit", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}]}
### Over a lifetime
The discs slowly lose water and height with age, which is one reason people get shorter as they get older, often a few centimetres by old age.
!? For accurate height tracking, doctors recommend measuring at the same time of day each time.
> Your spine's discs act like sponges, squeezed by the day and refilled by the night.

# Stomach rumbles
The word **borborygmi** comes from ancient Greek and was probably meant to sound like the noise itself. Most rumbles come from the small intestine as well as the stomach, as muscles squeeze gas and fluid along.
### The housekeeper wave
Between meals, a pattern of strong contractions called the **migrating motor complex** sweeps through the stomach and small intestine about every 90 to 120 minutes. It clears out leftover food and bacteria. With little food left to muffle the sound, this cleaning wave is often what you hear.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Stomach empties", "Hunger hormone rises", "Cleaning wave starts", "Gas and fluid squeezed", "Rumble"]}
### Hunger link
The hormone **ghrelin**, made mainly by the stomach, rises before meal times. It makes you feel hungry and is linked to the start of these cleaning waves, which is why rumbles and hunger often arrive together.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Empty gut", "items": ["more air pockets", "less to muffle", "louder, echoing"]}, "r": {"t": "Full gut", "items": ["food absorbs sound", "gentler mixing", "quieter gurgles"]}}
### What doctors listen for
Doctors use a stethoscope to listen to bowel sounds. Normal gurgles mean the gut is moving. Unusually high-pitched sounds, or long silence, can point to a blockage or a gut that has stopped working.
!! Rumbles mean your stomach is empty => They happen full or empty; you just hear them more when it's empty
> Stomach rumbles are the gut's cleaning waves, loudest when there's no food to soak up the sound.

# Brain freeze
Doctors call it a "cold-stimulus headache"; the tongue-twisting technical name is sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia. It usually peaks within seconds and fades in under a minute.
### What happens
When something very cold touches the roof of your mouth, blood vessels there narrow and then rapidly widen to rewarm the area. The sudden change is detected by branches of the **trigeminal nerve**, the main nerve that carries sensation from the face and forehead.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ice cream hits palate", "Vessels narrow", "Then rapidly widen", "Trigeminal nerve fires", "Forehead pain"]}
### Why the forehead?
It is referred pain. The nerve that senses your palate also serves the forehead, and the brain maps the signal there. A 2012 study also found a brief surge of blood flow in an artery at the front of the brain during brain freeze, which eased as the pain faded.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Press tongue up", "b": "warms the palate"}, {"a": "Sip warm water", "b": "same idea"}, {"a": "Eat slowly", "b": "prevents it in the first place"}]}
### Who gets it
Brain freeze is common in children and adults alike. People who get migraines seem to be more prone to it, which suggests the two share some pathways.
!? Brain freeze is one of the few headaches scientists can trigger on demand in a lab, which makes it useful for studying headaches in general.
> Brain freeze is the brain misplacing a cold alarm from your palate onto your forehead.

# Belly button
In the womb, the umbilical cord carries blood, food and oxygen from the placenta to the baby. After birth it's clamped and cut, and the short stump dries and falls off in about one to two weeks, leaving a scar: the navel.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cord feeds baby", "Clamped at birth", "Stump dries", "Falls off in 1–2 weeks", "Navel scar"]}
### Innies and outies
About 9 in 10 people have an "innie". Outies usually come from how the skin and scar tissue heal, or sometimes a small umbilical hernia, which is common in babies and often closes on its own.
!! The midwife's cut decides your belly button's shape => Shape depends on how the scar heals, not the cut
### A jungle of microbes
In 2012 the Belly Button Biodiversity project at North Carolina State University swabbed 60 navels. Across them they found over 2,000 types of bacteria, with dozens in each person on average. A handful of types were common to most people, while many were found in just one navel.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "60", "l": "navels swabbed (2012)"}, {"v": "2,000+", "l": "bacterial types in total"}, {"v": "dozens", "l": "in a typical navel"}]}
### Belly button fluff
A 2009 study by the chemist Georg Steinhauser found navel lint is mostly clothing fibres, plus dust and dead skin. Hair around the navel acts like a ratchet, guiding fibres inwards, which is why hairy bellies collect more.
> Your belly button is a scar from your first lifeline, now home to a private ecosystem.

# Goosebumps from music
**Frisson**, from French, means a sudden thrill. In music it's a wave of chills, tingling or goosebumps, often down the back of the neck and arms. Studies suggest roughly half of people or more get it, and some people never do.
### The reward chemistry
In 2011 researchers in Montreal used brain scans to show that music causing chills triggers **dopamine** release in the brain's reward system. Dopamine rose in one area during the build-up and in another at the moment of the chill itself. The same system responds to food and other rewards.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Music builds", "Brain predicts", "Surprise or peak", "Dopamine release", "Chills"]}
### What triggers it
• A sudden change in volume, such as a swell after a quiet passage
• A new voice or instrument entering
• An unexpected harmony or a note that resolves late
• Personal memories tied to the music
A fado singer's voice breaking into a crescendo is a classic example.
### Goosebumps are an old reflex
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Furry ancestors", "items": ["hair stands up", "traps warm air", "looks bigger"]}, "r": {"t": "Humans today", "items": ["tiny muscles still pull", "little hair to lift", "bumps on the skin"]}}
Goosebumps happen when tiny muscles at the base of each hair contract, controlled by the same nervous system that handles fight or flight. Music hijacks a reflex built for cold and fear.
!? People who score high on "openness to experience", a personality trait linked to curiosity and imagination, are more likely to get musical chills.
> Musical chills are the reward system and an ancient fur-raising reflex firing together at an emotional peak.
