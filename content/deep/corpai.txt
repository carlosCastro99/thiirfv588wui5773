# Four value levers
Most AI business cases map onto four levers. Naming the lever clarifies what to measure and who owns the result.

### Typical metrics per lever

### Mixing levers
Strong use cases often hit several at once: automated contract extraction cuts processing time (efficiency), reduces missed obligations (risk) and speeds up invoicing (cash flow).

Efficiency gains are easiest to measure; decision and growth gains are often larger but harder to prove. A balanced portfolio includes both.

# Productivity
Knowledge-work productivity gains come from removing low-value effort: searching, re-typing, reformatting, summarising, first drafts and routine answers.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Identify repetitive task", "AI drafts or extracts", "Human reviews", "Time freed for higher-value work"]}
### Where time goes
Studies of knowledge workers repeatedly find large shares of time spent searching for information, in email and on coordination. These are prime AI targets.

### From minutes to processes
Individual time savings are scattered; redesigning a whole process (e.g. accounts payable end to end) captures larger, measurable gains.

# Better decisions
Predictive models and AI-assisted analysis improve decisions that are made frequently and where small accuracy gains compound: pricing, inventory, credit, maintenance, logistics, hedging.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Demand forecasting", "Price and spread forecasting", "Credit and counterparty risk", "Predictive maintenance", "Route and freight optimisation"]}
### Why small improvements matter
In high-volume, thin-margin businesses like commodity trading, a slightly better forecast or freight decision applied to millions of tonnes can be worth millions.
ex: 5 million tonnes a year | × €0.50/t better execution | = €2.5 million a year
### Decision intelligence
Combine predictions with optimisation and human judgement: the model forecasts, an optimiser proposes actions, and experts decide with full context.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Data", "Forecast", "Optimisation proposes options", "Expert decides", "Outcome feeds back"]}
### Pitfalls
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Overfitting to the past", "b": "models miss regime changes"}, {"a": "Ignoring uncertainty", "b": "point forecasts hide risk"}, {"a": "No feedback loop", "b": "no learning from outcomes"}, {"a": "Black boxes", "b": "traders don't trust them"}]}
> Present forecasts with ranges and drivers; decision-makers trust what they understand.

# Competitive pressure
As AI lowers costs and speeds up processes, industry baselines shift. Customers come to expect faster responses; competitors operate with leaner cost structures.

### Sector dynamics
In commodity trading, information advantages are core. AI that processes market data, news, weather and logistics signals faster can sharpen decisions, but competitors access similar tools, so proprietary data and integration become differentiators.

### Measured approach
A prioritised roadmap, built on solid foundations and refreshed every six months, balances urgency with discipline.

Technology-first projects ("we need a chatbot") often struggle to find value. Problem-first projects start from a pain point with a measurable outcome and a committed owner.

# When to build
Build when AI is close to your competitive advantage, uses proprietary data, or must integrate deeply into core processes.

### Building on platforms
Building today rarely means training models. It means combining foundation models, retrieval, tools, guardrails and integrations on a platform.

> When an executive asks "why is this forecast different?", lineage provides the answer.

### AI-specific risks

### Controls

== corp-arch

# Layers of an AI platform
An enterprise AI platform is less a single product than a set of layers. Each layer solves one problem once, so individual teams don't reinvent it for every use case.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Applications: copilots, agents, apps", "Guardrails: filters, policies, validation", "Orchestration: RAG, agents, workflows", "Model access: gateway, catalogue", "Data layer: lakehouse, vector indexes", "Sources: ERP, CRM, documents"]}
### Cross-cutting concerns
Some things don't belong to one layer; they run through all of them.
• Identity and access: who can use which model, data and tool
• Security: secrets, network isolation, encryption
• Observability: logs, traces, cost and quality metrics
• Governance: inventory, risk classification, approvals
### Build vs buy per layer
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sources and data", "b": "usually existing systems"}, {"a": "Model access", "b": "buy (cloud APIs) or host open models"}, {"a": "Orchestration", "b": "frameworks plus in-house code"}, {"a": "Applications", "b": "mix of SaaS copilots and custom builds"}]}
### Why layers matter
Without a shared platform, every team picks its own model, vector database and logging. Five pilots later, the company has five security reviews, five bills and no reuse.
> Build the platform once, then let use cases plug in. The second and tenth use case should be much cheaper than the first.

# The AI gateway
An AI gateway is a proxy between applications and model providers. Every request passes through it, which makes it the natural place for control, measurement and flexibility.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["App sends request", "Gateway: auth and quota", "Filters and logging", "Route to model", "Response checked", "Back to app"]}
### What it does
• Authentication: only approved apps and users get access
• Routing: send each request to the right model or region, with fallbacks if a provider fails
• Quotas and budgets: stop one runaway script burning the monthly budget
• Logging and cost attribution per team and application
• Content filters: block sensitive data going out, harmful content coming back
### Swapping models centrally
Because applications call the gateway rather than a vendor directly, you can switch models (cheaper, newer, different provider) by changing configuration, not dozens of applications.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without gateway", "items": ["keys in every app", "no total cost view", "vendor lock-in"]}, "r": {"t": "With gateway", "items": ["one control point", "spend per team", "switch models centrally"]}}
### Caching
Gateways can cache identical or near-identical requests, cutting cost and latency for repeated questions.
!? Many gateways expose an OpenAI-compatible API, so tools written for one provider can be redirected to others with minimal changes.

# Model catalogue
A model catalogue is the approved menu of models, with guidance on when to use each. It prevents both chaos (everyone picks their own) and waste (everyone uses the biggest model for everything).
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Catalogue", "items": ["Large models: complex reasoning", "Small models: classification, extraction", "Embeddings: search and RAG", "Specialised: speech, vision, translation"]}
### Choosing a model
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hard reasoning, long analysis", "b": "large frontier model"}, {"a": "High-volume extraction", "b": "small, fast model"}, {"a": "Search and similarity", "b": "embedding model"}, {"a": "Sensitive data on premises", "b": "open-weights model self-hosted"}]}
### Documenting each entry
Each catalogue entry should record:
• Provider, version and hosting region
• Approved data classifications (public, internal, confidential)
• Cost per million tokens and typical latency
• Known strengths, weaknesses and evaluation results
• Retirement date, since providers deprecate model versions
### Cost differences
Prices between the smallest and largest models in a provider's lineup often differ by more than ten times per token, so routing simple work to small models is a major cost lever.
> Default to the smallest model that passes your evaluation, not the most famous one.

# Knowledge layer
The knowledge layer is shared retrieval infrastructure: it ingests approved document collections, chunks and embeds them, and serves permission-aware search to any AI application.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Connect sources", "Extract text", "Chunk", "Embed", "Index with permissions", "Serve search to apps"]}
### Why share it
Chunking, OCR, table handling and permission sync are hard to get right. Doing them once, well, beats ten teams doing them badly.
### Permission awareness
The index must store who can see each document and filter results at query time. Otherwise the assistant becomes a way to read documents you were never allowed to open.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Naive index", "items": ["everyone sees everything", "leaks"]}, "r": {"t": "Permission-aware", "items": ["results filtered per user", "mirrors source access"]}}
### Freshness
• Incremental sync so new and changed documents appear quickly
• Deletion propagation, so removed files disappear from answers
• Metadata (date, owner, document type) to filter and rank results
!! RAG is just plugging in a vector database => most effort goes into document parsing, permissions, freshness and evaluation

# Tools and integration
Agents become useful when they can act: look up an order, create a ticket, check a credit limit. That requires carefully designed access to core systems.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["ERP: orders, invoices", "CRM: accounts, contacts", "Ticketing: create, update", "Documents: search, read", "Email and calendar"]}
### Narrow APIs, not database access
Give agents specific operations ("get invoice status by number") rather than broad database credentials. Narrow tools are easier to secure, audit and test.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Agent gets read/write database login", "r": "Agent gets three narrow, logged API calls"}]}
### MCP servers
The Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced by Anthropic in November 2024 and since adopted widely, is a standard way to expose tools and data to AI applications. One MCP server for your ERP can serve many assistants.
### Design rules for tools
• Clear names and descriptions, since the model chooses tools by reading them
• Validate every input; never trust model-generated parameters blindly
• Separate read tools from write tools, and require approval for writes
• Return concise, structured results
> Treat every tool as a public API that a clever but unpredictable user will call.

# Guardrails
Guardrails are checks before and after the model. They catch what prompts alone can't guarantee: data leaks, harmful content, injected instructions and outputs that break business rules.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["User input", "Input checks", "Model", "Output checks", "Business rules", "Deliver or block"]}
### Input checks
• Detect sensitive data (card numbers, IDs, health data) and mask or block it
• Detect likely prompt injection or jailbreak attempts
• Enforce topic scope ("this assistant only answers HR policy questions")
### Output checks
• Toxicity and policy filters
• Grounding checks: is each claim supported by retrieved sources?
• Format validation: valid JSON, required fields present
• Business rules: discount not above 15%, no promises on delivery dates
### Layered defence
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Business rule validation", "Output filters", "Model's own safety training", "System prompt instructions", "Input filters"]}
No single layer is perfect; together they reduce risk to an acceptable level.
!? Deterministic checks (regular expressions, schema validation, numeric limits) are cheap and reliable; use them wherever possible before reaching for another AI model as a judge.

# Observability
You can't improve or defend what you can't see. Observability means capturing enough about each AI interaction to debug failures, track quality and control cost.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Prompts and responses", "Retrieved documents", "Tool calls and results", "Latency and errors", "Tokens and cost", "User feedback"]}
### Traces
A trace follows one request through every step: retrieval, model calls, tool calls, retries. When an agent does something odd, the trace shows exactly where it went wrong.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Request", "Retrieval (3 docs)", "Model call 1", "Tool call", "Model call 2", "Answer"]}
### Dashboards
• Usage: active users, requests per application
• Spend: per team, per model, trend versus budget
• Quality: thumbs up/down, correction and escalation rates
• Reliability: error rates, latency percentiles, provider outages
### Privacy controls
Logs of prompts can contain personal and confidential data. Apply retention limits, access restrictions and masking, and include logs in your GDPR records.
> Log enough to explain any answer after the fact, and protect those logs as carefully as the source data.

# Environments
AI applications deserve the same release discipline as other software: separate environments, version control and gates before production.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Development", "Test with eval sets", "Staging with real-like data", "Approval gate", "Production"]}
### What gets versioned
• Prompts and system instructions
• Model name and version
• Retrieval configuration and index version
• Tool definitions and guardrail rules
### Evaluation gates
Before promotion, run the change against a fixed evaluation set and compare with the current version. Quality, safety and cost must meet thresholds.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ad hoc", "items": ["edit prompt in production", "hope"]}, "r": {"t": "Disciplined", "items": ["versioned change", "eval run", "approved release"]}}
### Test data
Test environments need realistic data. Use anonymised or synthetic samples, never casually copy production personal data into development.
!! Prompt changes are too small to need testing => a one-line prompt edit can change behaviour across thousands of cases

# Cloud and region
Where models run and what the contract says about your data matter as much as which model you choose, especially in the EU.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Hosting choices", "items": ["Provider API: fastest start", "Cloud platform: regional, enterprise terms", "Self-hosted open model: full control"]}
### Data residency
Major cloud providers offer model hosting in EU regions and EU-only processing options, so prompts and outputs stay in the EU. Check where logging, abuse monitoring and support access happen too.
### Contract terms to check
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Training use", "b": "your data must not train provider models"}, {"a": "Retention", "b": "how long prompts and outputs are stored"}, {"a": "Sub-processors", "b": "who else touches the data"}, {"a": "Location", "b": "processing and storage regions"}, {"a": "Security", "b": "certifications such as ISO 27001, SOC 2"}]}
### Trade-offs
• Newest models often reach a provider's own API before every cloud region
• Self-hosting gives control but requires GPUs, skills and operations
• Regional hosting can cost slightly more or offer fewer models
> Read the enterprise data terms, not the consumer ones: they are often very different.

# Reference architecture
A reference architecture is a documented, approved blueprint plus reusable code templates. It turns each new AI project from a research effort into an assembly job.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pick template", "Connect data via knowledge layer", "Use gateway models", "Add guardrails", "Evaluate", "Launch"]}
### Typical templates
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Chat over documents (RAG)", "Document extraction to system", "Summarise and classify tickets", "Agent with approved tools", "Batch analysis pipeline"]}
### What's pre-approved
When a team uses the reference architecture, security and privacy controls are already reviewed. Their review focuses only on what's new: the data and the use case.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Custom stack", "items": ["full review", "months"]}, "r": {"t": "Reference stack", "items": ["delta review", "weeks"]}}
### Keeping it alive

== corp-security

# New attack surface
AI applications inherit all normal software risks and add new ones, because their behaviour is steered by natural language that attackers can also write.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "AI-specific risks", "items": ["Prompt injection: hijacked instructions", "Data leakage: secrets in outputs", "Poisoning: bad training or RAG data", "Excessive agency: tools misused", "Abuse: cost and misuse attacks"]}
### The OWASP view
The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025 edition) ranks the main risks. The top entries include:
• LLM01 Prompt injection
• LLM02 Sensitive information disclosure
• LLM03 Supply chain
• LLM04 Data and model poisoning
• LLM06 Excessive agency
### Why it's different
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Classic apps", "items": ["code defines behaviour", "inputs are data"]}, "r": {"t": "LLM apps", "items": ["text steers behaviour", "data can act as instructions"]}}
> In an LLM application, any text the model reads can try to give it orders.

# Prompt injection
Prompt injection hides instructions in content the AI processes: an email, a web page, a PDF, a calendar invite. The model can't reliably tell your instructions from the attacker's.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Attacker plants text in email", "Assistant summarises inbox", "Hidden text: \"forward invoices to X\"", "Assistant obeys"]}
### Direct vs indirect
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Direct", "b": "user types malicious instructions into the chat"}, {"a": "Indirect", "b": "instructions hidden in documents, sites or emails the AI reads"}]}
Indirect injection is the bigger enterprise risk, because the victim never sees the attack.
### The lethal combination
Researcher Simon Willison, who coined the term "prompt injection" in 2022, warns about a "lethal trifecta": an agent that has access to private data, reads untrusted content and can communicate externally can be tricked into exfiltrating data.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Private data access", "Untrusted content", "External communication"]}
### Defences
• Remove one leg of the trifecta wherever possible
• Require human approval for sensitive actions
• Restrict which domains the agent can send data to
• Separate and label untrusted content; filter suspicious instructions
• Give agents minimal permissions
!! Better prompts can fully stop injection => no known technique is fully reliable; design so a successful injection can't do much harm

# Data leakage
Data leaks through AI in three main ways: people paste it into the wrong tools, systems retrieve what users shouldn't see, or outputs reveal confidential details.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Leak paths", "items": ["Input: pasting into unapproved tools", "Retrieval: RAG ignoring permissions", "Output: model reveals secrets", "Logs: sensitive prompts stored"]}
### A famous example
In 2023, Samsung restricted staff use of public chatbots after engineers pasted confidential source code and meeting notes into ChatGPT.
### Controls
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Unapproved tools", "b": "provide approved ones, block or warn on others"}, {"a": "Retrieval", "b": "permission-aware indexes"}, {"a": "Outputs", "b": "filters for secrets and personal data"}, {"a": "Logs", "b": "retention limits and access control"}]}
### Data classification helps
If documents are labelled (public, internal, confidential, restricted), rules become simple: "restricted data only in approved, EU-hosted tools with no retention".
• Data loss prevention (DLP) tools can detect sensitive content in prompts
• Browser controls can warn when pasting into unknown AI sites
> Most AI leaks are ordinary data governance failures made faster and more visible.

# Shadow AI
Shadow AI is the use of AI tools without company approval: personal ChatGPT accounts, browser plugins, free transcription apps. Surveys consistently find it widespread.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ban only", "items": ["use goes underground", "no visibility"]}, "r": {"t": "Provide and guide", "items": ["approved tools", "clear rules", "visibility"]}}
### Why it happens
• Approved tools are missing, slow or worse than consumer apps
• Staff don't know the rules or the risks
• The productivity benefit is real and immediate
### Risks
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Confidential data in consumer tools", "Unknown data retention", "No audit trail", "Inconsistent quality", "Licence and IP issues"]}
### The better response
Provide a good enterprise assistant quickly, publish a one-page "what you can and can't do", and monitor network traffic to see which tools are in demand. Demand signals tell you what to approve next.
!? Many organisations found that usage of unapproved tools dropped sharply once a good approved assistant was rolled out.

# Least privilege for agents
An AI agent should have the minimum permissions needed for its task and nothing more, just like a new employee on day one.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Define task", "List needed actions", "Grant narrow scopes", "Add approvals for risky steps", "Log everything"]}
### Practical controls
• Scoped, short-lived tokens instead of shared admin accounts
• Read-only by default; write access only where essential
• Rate limits and spending caps per agent
• Allow-lists of systems, domains and recipients
• Human approval for payments, deletions, external emails
### Whose identity?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "On behalf of user", "b": "agent inherits the user's rights, never more"}, {"a": "Service identity", "b": "agent has its own narrow account"}, {"a": "Mixed", "b": "read as user, act via approved service"}]}
### Blast radius
Ask: if this agent is fully compromised by an injection, what's the worst it can do? Design so the answer is acceptable.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Over-privileged", "items": ["admin token", "any email", "any payment"]}, "r": {"t": "Least privilege", "items": ["one mailbox", "internal recipients", "payments need approval"]}}
> Assume the agent will eventually be tricked, and limit what a tricked agent can do.

# Supply chain
AI systems are built from third-party parts: models, weights, libraries, plugins, datasets and MCP servers. Each is a potential way in for attackers.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Model weights", "Python packages", "Plugins and MCP servers", "Datasets", "Hosted APIs"]}
### Real risks
• Malicious model files: some formats can run code when loaded, so prefer safe formats like safetensors
• Typosquatted packages with names similar to popular AI libraries
• Unvetted plugins or MCP servers with broad permissions
• Poisoned public datasets
### Controls
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sources", "b": "approved registries and vendors only"}, {"a": "Versions", "b": "pin and review updates"}, {"a": "Scanning", "b": "dependency and model file scanning"}, {"a": "Inventory", "b": "list every component (an AI bill of materials)"}]}
### Vendor due diligence
For hosted models and AI SaaS, review security certifications, data terms, sub-processors and incident history, as for any critical supplier.
!? An AI bill of materials (AI-BOM) extends the software bill of materials idea to list models, datasets and their versions.

# Personal data and GDPR
GDPR applies fully to AI. Any processing of personal data, whether training, fine-tuning, retrieval or prompts, needs a legal basis and must respect data protection principles.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Define purpose", "Choose legal basis", "Minimise data", "DPIA if high risk", "Inform people", "Secure and retain properly"]}
### Key principles applied to AI
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Purpose limitation", "b": "data collected for payroll isn't automatically usable for AI training"}, {"a": "Minimisation", "b": "only the personal data truly needed"}, {"a": "Accuracy", "b": "AI outputs about people must be correctable"}, {"a": "Storage limitation", "b": "delete prompts and logs when no longer needed"}]}
### DPIA
A data protection impact assessment is required when processing is likely to be high risk, which often applies to new AI uses involving employees, customers or profiling. The data protection officer (DPO) should be involved early, not at launch.
### Automated decisions
Article 22 GDPR gives people the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that have legal or similarly significant effects, such as rejecting a loan or job application, except under specific conditions with safeguards.
• Meaningful human review, not rubber-stamping
• Right to contest and obtain an explanation
> Involve the DPO at the design stage: privacy fixes are cheap early and expensive late.

# Red teaming
Red teaming means attacking your own AI system before real attackers or unlucky users do. It finds failures that normal testing misses.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Plan attacks", "Attack", "Record failures", "Fix", "Retest"]}
### What to try
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Prompt injection", "Jailbreaks", "Data extraction", "Harmful content", "Tool misuse", "Cost abuse"]}
### Who does it
• Security specialists who know injection techniques
• Domain experts who know what a harmful answer looks like
• Ordinary users who misuse things in unexpected ways
• Automated tools that generate thousands of attack variants
### Make it repeatable
Turn every successful attack into a test case in your evaluation set, so a future prompt or model change can't quietly reopen the hole.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "One-off pentest", "items": ["snapshot", "forgotten"]}, "r": {"t": "Continuous red teaming", "items": ["regression tests", "every release"]}}
!? Frontier AI labs run large red-teaming exercises with external experts before releasing major models, and publish summaries in system cards.

# Incident response
AI incidents will happen: a chatbot gives wrong policy advice, an agent emails the wrong customer, a document leaks via search. Preparation decides whether they are minor or major.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Detect", "Contain: switch off feature", "Investigate logs", "Fix and test", "Notify if required", "Learn"]}
### AI-specific incident types
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Harmful or false outputs", "Personal data exposure", "Agent wrong actions", "Injection attacks", "Runaway costs"]}
### Kill switches
Every AI feature should have a way to disable it instantly via feature flag, without a code deployment. Agents should be stoppable mid-task.
### Legal clocks
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "GDPR breach", "b": "notify the authority within 72 hours when required"}, {"a": "EU AI Act", "b": "serious incidents with high-risk systems must be reported"}, {"a": "NIS2", "b": "significant incidents reported quickly for covered entities"}]}
### A lesson in liability
In 2024 a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to honour a refund policy its website chatbot had invented. The airline argued the chatbot was responsible for its own words; the tribunal disagreed.
> Your company owns what its AI says and does. Plan incidents accordingly.

# Security frameworks
You don't need to invent AI security from scratch. Established frameworks provide checklists and structure, and they map onto existing security programmes.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "OWASP Top 10 for LLMs", "b": "most common LLM app vulnerabilities"}, {"a": "NIST AI RMF", "b": "risk framework: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage"}, {"a": "ISO/IEC 42001", "b": "certifiable AI management system (2023)"}, {"a": "ISO 27001", "b": "information security baseline"}]}
### NIST AI RMF
Published in January 2023 by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, the AI Risk Management Framework organises work into four functions. A generative AI profile followed in 2024.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Govern", "Map", "Measure", "Manage"]}
### ISO/IEC 42001
The first international standard for AI management systems, published in December 2023. Like ISO 27001 for security, organisations can be certified against it.
### MITRE ATLAS
A knowledge base of real-world attack techniques against AI systems, modelled on the well-known MITRE ATT&CK framework.
• Use OWASP for developers' checklists
• Use NIST or ISO 42001 for the management system
• Map AI controls into existing ISO 27001 processes
> Extend your existing security programme to AI rather than building a separate one.

== corp-governance

# Why governance
AI governance is the set of rules, roles and processes that keep AI use safe, legal and aligned with company values. Done well, it speeds adoption by removing uncertainty.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "No governance", "items": ["fear", "shadow AI", "surprises"]}, "r": {"t": "Good governance", "items": ["clear rules", "faster approvals", "trust"]}}
### What it covers
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Approved tools and data", "Use-case review", "Roles and accountability", "Monitoring and audit", "Training", "Vendor management"]}
### The enabling role
Teams often stall because nobody knows whether something is allowed. A clear policy with pre-approved patterns lets most work start immediately.
### Signs of bad governance
• Every request goes to the same overloaded committee
• Reviews take months, regardless of risk
• Rules exist on paper but nobody follows them
• Nobody knows which AI systems are actually running
> Governance should be a paved road, not a toll booth.

# AI policy
An AI policy is the short, readable rulebook for employees. Long legal documents go unread; the best policies fit on a page or two with examples.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Approved tools", "Allowed data per tool", "Human review duties", "Disclosure rules", "Prohibited uses", "How to propose new uses"]}
### Example rules
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Approved assistant", "b": "internal and confidential data allowed"}, {"a": "Public chatbots", "b": "public information only"}, {"a": "Customer-facing outputs", "b": "human review before sending"}, {"a": "Decisions about people", "b": "never solely by AI"}]}
### Make it usable
• Use traffic-light examples: green, amber, red
• Link to the request form for new tools
• Name a contact for questions
• Review it every six to twelve months, as tools change fast
### Prohibited uses
Typical lists include uploading restricted data to unapproved tools, using AI to monitor employees' emotions, and generating content that impersonates real people.
!? Policies that explain why a rule exists are followed far better than bare prohibitions.

# Inventory of AI systems
An AI inventory is a register of every AI system and use case in the company. You can't govern, secure or comply for systems you don't know about.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Purpose and owner", "Data used", "Model and vendor", "Users affected", "Risk level", "Controls and reviews"]}
### Why it's essential
The EU AI Act assigns obligations based on each system's risk category and on whether you are the provider or deployer. Without an inventory, you can't even start classifying.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Discover systems", "Record in register", "Classify risk", "Assign controls", "Review periodically"]}
### Finding hidden AI
• AI features switched on inside existing SaaS tools
• Vendor products marketed as "smart" or "intelligent"
• Spreadsheets and scripts calling AI APIs
• Pilots that quietly became production
### Keep it alive
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Static list", "items": ["outdated in months"]}, "r": {"t": "Living register", "items": ["linked to procurement and release processes"]}}
> If procurement and IT change processes feed the register automatically, it stays current.

# Risk-based review
Not every AI use needs the same scrutiny. A risk-based process sends low-risk uses through a quick check and reserves deep review for systems that can really hurt people or the business.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Use case submitted", "Triage questionnaire", "Low risk: self-approve", "Medium: light review", "High: full review board"]}
### Triage questions
• Does it make or influence decisions about people?
• Does it use personal or confidential data?
• Is the output customer-facing or acted on automatically?
• Could errors cause financial, legal or safety harm?
• Is it a high-risk category under the EU AI Act?
### Tiers
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Low", "b": "internal drafting with approved tools, self-assessment"}, {"a": "Medium", "b": "customer-facing with human review, light review"}, {"a": "High", "b": "decisions about people or critical operations, full review and monitoring"}]}
### Speed targets
Set service levels: low-risk in days, medium in two weeks, high in a defined, published timeline. Predictability matters as much as speed.
!! Every AI use needs a committee approval => this creates bottlenecks and drives shadow AI; most uses are low risk

# The EU AI Act for companies
The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) entered into force in August 2024 and applies in stages. Most companies are "deployers": they use AI systems built by others, which carries lighter but real duties.

# AI literacy duty
Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires providers and deployers to take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff dealing with AI systems. It has applied since 2 February 2025.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2 Feb 2025", "l": "AI literacy duty applies"}, {"v": "Art. 4", "l": "of the EU AI Act"}, {"v": "All", "l": "risk levels covered, not just high risk"}]}
### What "sufficient" means
The Act doesn't prescribe a course. Literacy should fit people's roles, technical knowledge and the context in which AI is used.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "All staff", "b": "what AI is, limits, policy, data rules"}, {"a": "Power users", "b": "prompting, verification, hallucinations"}, {"a": "Builders", "b": "evaluation, security, bias"}, {"a": "Leaders", "b": "strategy, risk, regulation"}]}
### Practical programme
• Short mandatory basics for everyone using AI tools
• Role-based modules for developers, HR, legal, managers
• Documented attendance and materials as evidence
• Refreshers as tools and rules change
### Beyond compliance
Literacy is also the biggest driver of value: people who understand AI's strengths and limits use it more and more safely.
> Treat AI literacy as a productivity programme that also satisfies a legal duty.

# Model risk management
Banks have managed "model risk" for over a decade: the risk that decisions based on models are wrong. Those practices transfer well to AI and LLMs.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Develop and document", "Independent validation", "Approve", "Monitor performance", "Periodic review"]}
### Origins
The US Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 guidance (2011) defined model risk management for banks. In the UK, the PRA's SS1/23 principles took effect in 2024 and explicitly include AI models.
### Core practices
• An inventory of models with risk ratings
• Documentation of purpose, data, assumptions and limits
• Independent validation before use
• Ongoing performance monitoring and periodic revalidation
### Adapting to LLMs
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Classic models", "items": ["fixed formula", "numeric accuracy tests"]}, "r": {"t": "LLMs", "items": ["open-ended outputs", "vendor model changes", "evaluation sets and human review"]}}
• Vendor models can change under you: pin versions and re-test on updates
• Validation uses evaluation sets and red teaming rather than only statistical tests
> If your company already has model risk management, extend it; don't build a parallel AI process.

The goal is to let most AI use proceed quickly while focusing scrutiny where it matters. Good governance is measured by both safety and speed.

# Fairness
An AI system is unfair when it systematically disadvantages people based on protected characteristics such as sex, ethnicity, age or disability, or on proxies for them.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Identify affected groups", "Define fairness metric", "Test outcomes per group", "Investigate gaps", "Mitigate", "Monitor"]}
### How bias enters
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Biased historical data", "Unrepresentative samples", "Proxy variables", "Biased labels", "Feedback loops"]}
### Real cases
• Amazon scrapped an experimental CV-screening tool, reported in 2018, after it learned to penalise CVs mentioning "women's"
• In the Netherlands, a benefits fraud system that used nationality as a risk factor contributed to the childcare benefits scandal; the government resigned in 2021
### Proxies
Removing the "gender" column isn't enough. Postcode, first name, career gaps or hobbies can stand in for protected attributes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fairness through blindness", "items": ["drop sensitive columns", "proxies remain"]}, "r": {"t": "Fairness through testing", "items": ["measure outcomes per group", "fix gaps"]}}
### No single definition
Different fairness metrics (equal approval rates, equal error rates, equal accuracy) can mathematically conflict. Choosing one is a business and ethical decision, not only a technical one.
> Test outcomes across groups; don't assume fairness because sensitive data was excluded.

# Transparency
Transparency has two audiences: people affected by AI, who should know it's involved and why it decided as it did, and internal reviewers, who need to understand how the system works.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Transparency", "items": ["Disclosure: \"you're talking to AI\"", "Explanation: main reasons for a decision", "Documentation: how it works and its limits"]}
### Legal duties
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "EU AI Act", "b": "people must be told when interacting with a chatbot"}, {"a": "AI-generated content", "b": "deepfakes and certain synthetic content must be labelled"}, {"a": "GDPR", "b": "meaningful information about the logic of automated decisions"}]}
### Explanations that help
A good explanation names the main factors and what the person could change: "the application was declined mainly due to short credit history and high existing debt".
• Avoid technical jargon
• Focus on actionable factors
• Offer a route to human review
### Internal transparency
Document data sources, intended use, known limits and evaluation results, so reviewers, auditors and future maintainers can understand the system.
!? Research shows people trust AI recommendations more appropriately, neither too much nor too little, when they understand its typical error patterns.

# Accountability
Accountability means a specific person or team answers for each AI system's behaviour and outcomes. AI can make decisions, but it can't be responsible for them.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"The algorithm decided\"", "r": "\"The credit team owns this model and its decisions\""}]}
### Who is accountable
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Business owner", "b": "outcomes and acceptable use"}, {"a": "Technical owner", "b": "performance and maintenance"}, {"a": "Control functions", "b": "oversight of compliance"}, {"a": "Executives", "b": "overall risk appetite"}]}
### Mechanisms
• Named owner in the AI inventory
• Sign-off before launch and after major changes
• Audit trails of decisions and changes
• Clear escalation paths when something goes wrong
### Courts agree
The Air Canada chatbot case (2024) and many regulatory decisions confirm that organisations are responsible for their AI systems' outputs.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Incident", "Owner investigates", "Fix", "Report to governance", "Lessons shared"]}
> Every AI system needs a human name next to it.

# Human oversight
Human oversight means people can understand, monitor and intervene in AI systems. The right level depends on the stakes.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Human-in-the-loop: approves each action", "Human-on-the-loop: monitors and can intervene", "Human-in-command: sets goals and audits"]}
### Choosing the level
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rejecting a loan", "b": "human-in-the-loop or meaningful review"}, {"a": "Routing support tickets", "b": "human-on-the-loop"}, {"a": "Spam filtering", "b": "periodic audit"}, {"a": "Drafting internal summaries", "b": "user reviews own output"}]}
### Automation bias
People tend to over-trust automated recommendations, especially when tired or busy. A human who clicks "approve" on everything provides no real oversight.
• Show confidence and the evidence behind each recommendation
• Track how often reviewers disagree; zero disagreement is a warning sign
• Give reviewers time and authority to override
• Occasionally insert test cases to check attention
### Legal requirement
The EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed for effective human oversight, and deployers to assign it to people with competence, training and authority.
!! A human in the loop makes it safe => only if the human has time, information and power to disagree

# Reliability
A reliable AI system performs consistently within its intended scope, knows its limits and fails safely when it meets something outside them.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Define scope", "Test within scope", "Test edge cases", "Detect uncertainty", "Escalate instead of guessing"]}
### Scope matters
An invoice extractor tested on Portuguese and Spanish suppliers may fail on Chinese invoices. Document the tested scope and detect inputs outside it.
### Graceful failure
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Brittle", "items": ["always answers", "confidently wrong"]}, "r": {"t": "Reliable", "items": ["flags uncertainty", "routes to human"]}}
• Confidence thresholds below which outputs go to review
• "I don't know" answers when sources don't contain the answer
• Fallback to rules or humans when the AI service is down
### Robustness tests
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Typos and odd formats", "Unusual but valid inputs", "Adversarial inputs", "Load and latency spikes", "Provider outages"]}
> Reliability is knowing when not to answer.

# Environmental impact
AI runs in data centres that consume electricity and water. The footprint per query is small, but scale makes the total significant.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~415 TWh", "l": "data centres' electricity in 2024 (IEA)"}, {"v": "~1.5%", "l": "of global electricity use"}, {"v": "~945 TWh", "l": "IEA projection for 2030"}]}
### Per query
Google reported in 2025 that a median text prompt to its Gemini assistant used about 0.24 Wh of electricity, roughly nine seconds of TV, and about 0.26 mL of water. Large reasoning tasks, images and video use much more.
### Practical levers
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Use smaller models", "Cache repeated answers", "Avoid unnecessary long outputs", "Batch non-urgent jobs", "Choose clean-energy regions"]}
### Training vs use
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Training", "items": ["huge one-off energy", "done by the provider"]}, "r": {"t": "Inference", "items": ["small per query", "dominates at scale"]}}

# Model and system cards
Model cards and system cards are short documents describing what an AI system is for, how it was built, how well it performs and where it shouldn't be used.

### For internal systems
A one-to-two-page card per AI application helps:
• Users understand what to trust it with
• Reviewers check risks quickly
• Auditors and regulators see evidence
• Future teams maintain it safely

### Scoring methods
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Exact match", "b": "extraction fields, classifications"}, {"a": "Rules", "b": "format, length, forbidden content"}, {"a": "AI judge", "b": "helpfulness, faithfulness, tone"}, {"a": "Human review", "b": "sample of high-stakes outputs"}]}

In production you rarely have the correct answer for every output, so you monitor proxies that reveal quality problems early.

# Data drift
Data drift happens when real-world inputs differ from what the model was built and tested on. Performance can degrade silently because nothing technically breaks.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Launch", "l": "model fits data well"}, {"y": "Month 6", "l": "new supplier formats"}, {"y": "Month 9", "l": "accuracy slipping"}, {"y": "Month 12", "l": "problem noticed late"}]}
### Types of drift
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Data drift", "b": "inputs change (new products, markets)"}, {"a": "Concept drift", "b": "the relationship changes (what predicts default)"}, {"a": "Upstream change", "b": "a source system changes a field's meaning"}]}
### Famous example
In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic broke many demand forecasting and fraud models overnight because buying behaviour changed radically.
### Detection
• Compare input distributions against training data
• Watch prediction distributions for shifts
• Track accuracy where true outcomes arrive later
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Monitor inputs", "Detect shift", "Alert", "Evaluate impact", "Retrain or adjust"]}
!? In 2021 Zillow closed its home-buying business after its pricing models failed to keep up with a rapidly changing housing market, at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars.

# Rollbacks
Things will go wrong after a release. The ability to return quickly to the last good version turns a potential crisis into a minor incident.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["New release", "Problem detected", "Flip feature flag or roll back", "Investigate calmly", "Fix and re-release"]}
### Make rollback easy
• Keep the previous prompt, model and index versions deployable
• Use feature flags to switch AI features off instantly
• Canary releases: give the new version to 5% of users first
• Automate rollback when error metrics cross thresholds
### Release strategies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Canary", "b": "small share of traffic first"}, {"a": "Shadow mode", "b": "new version runs silently alongside the old"}, {"a": "A/B test", "b": "compare versions on real outcomes"}, {"a": "Blue-green", "b": "switch all traffic between two environments"}]}
### Data considerations
Rolling back a model is easy; rolling back actions an agent took is not. Agents with write access need extra caution and staged rollouts.
> If you can't roll back in minutes, you're not ready to release.

== corp-scale

# Pilot purgatory
"Pilot purgatory" describes organisations with dozens of promising AI experiments and almost nothing running at scale. Demos impress; production doesn't follow.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No business owner", "No integration plan", "Value not measured", "Risk questions unresolved", "No budget to run it"]}
### The numbers
Gartner predicted in 2024 that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs or unclear business value.
### Why demos mislead
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Demo", "items": ["clean sample data", "expert user", "happy path"]}, "r": {"t": "Production", "items": ["messy real data", "busy users", "edge cases", "security reviews"]}}
### Escaping purgatory
• Fewer pilots, chosen for value and feasibility
• Each pilot has a sponsor committed to scale if it works
• Production requirements (security, integration) addressed from week one
• Clear go/no-go decision at the end
> A pilot without a path to production is a demo with a budget.

# Design for production from day one
A pilot should test whether the solution works in reality, not whether AI can work in principle. That means real data, real users and the target architecture.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Throwaway pilot", "items": ["sample data", "personal API key", "separate app"]}, "r": {"t": "Production-minded pilot", "items": ["real data", "platform gateway", "inside the workflow"]}}
### What to include early
• Real documents, with their scans, typos and odd formats
• Actual users doing real work, not only enthusiasts
• Security and privacy review of the data used
• The gateway, logging and evaluation you'll use later
### What can wait
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Full automation", "b": "start with human review"}, {"a": "All variants", "b": "start with the top document types"}, {"a": "Perfect UI", "b": "simple but integrated"}, {"a": "Every region", "b": "one team first"}]}
### The rebuild trap
Pilots built on shortcuts often need a complete rebuild for production, which costs months and kills momentum.
!? Good pilots answer three questions at once: does it work, will people use it, and can we run it safely?

# Define success upfront
Agreeing success criteria before starting prevents moving goalposts and endless "promising" pilots. It also forces clarity on what the solution is actually for.

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Invoice extraction", "b": "95% of fields correct, 50% less handling time"}, {"a": "Support assistant", "b": "30% of tickets resolved without escalation"}, {"a": "Contract review", "b": "all key clauses found, review time halved"}]}

# Handle exceptions
Every AI system meets cases it can't handle well. Designing a good path for them is what makes automation safe and trustworthy.

• Make correcting faster than doing it from scratch

Typically, a few common case types make up most volume. Automate those well and route the long tail to people.

# Technology is the easy part
AI projects fail more often because people don't use them than because the technology doesn't work. Change management is not a side task; it decides the outcome.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tech-only project", "items": ["tool launched", "low use", "no value"]}, "r": {"t": "Change-led project", "items": ["people involved", "process adapted", "value realised"]}}
### Why people resist
• Fear of job loss or reduced status
• Distrust of AI accuracy
• Habit and time pressure: the old way feels faster
• Unclear benefit for them personally
### The 10-20-70 rule
Boston Consulting Group's rule of thumb: about 10% of the effort in AI transformation is algorithms, 20% technology and data, and 70% people and processes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "where AI effort should go, % (BCG's 10-20-70 rule)", "items": [{"l": "Algorithms", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Tech and data", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "People, process", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}]}

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Communication", "Training", "Process redesign", "Role changes", "Incentives", "Feedback"]}

# Address fears openly
People worry about job security, surveillance and looking incompetent with new tools. Ignoring these fears doesn't make them go away; it makes rumours fill the gap.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Will I lose my job?", "Am I being monitored?", "Will I look slow?", "Will my skills still matter?"]}
### Honest communication
• Explain the purpose: growth, quality, workload or cost, and be truthful
• Describe how roles will change, not just that they will
• Commit to reskilling and say how
• Clarify what data is and isn't collected about employees

# Hands-on training

# Redesign the process
Adding AI to an unchanged process delivers small gains. Rethinking the process around what AI does well unlocks much larger ones.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bolt on", "items": ["same steps", "AI helps one step", "small saving"]}, "r": {"t": "Redesign", "items": ["fewer handovers", "new roles", "big saving"]}}

@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Before: receive", "type", "check", "approve", "post"]}

# ADKAR in brief
ADKAR is a widely used change management model developed by Jeff Hiatt of Prosci. It describes five outcomes individuals need for a change to succeed.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Reinforcement: make it stick", "Ability: able to do it in practice", "Knowledge: know how", "Desire: want to take part", "Awareness: understand why"]}
### Applied to AI adoption
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Awareness", "b": "why AI, what changes, what doesn't"}, {"a": "Desire", "b": "personal benefits, fears addressed"}, {"a": "Knowledge", "b": "training, prompt libraries"}, {"a": "Ability", "b": "practice, coaching, time"}, {"a": "Reinforcement", "b": "recognition, metrics, leaders' example"}]}
### Diagnosing barriers
Find the first element that's missing. Training (Knowledge) won't help someone who doesn't see why (Awareness) or doesn't want to (Desire).
### Other models
• Kotter's 8 steps: urgency, coalition, vision, communication, empowerment, quick wins, build on change, anchor in culture
• Lewin's unfreeze, change, refreeze
> Fix the earliest missing step first.
