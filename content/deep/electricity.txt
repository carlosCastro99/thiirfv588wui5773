== el-basics

# Electricity is moving charge
Electric current is a flow of electric charge. In metal wires, the charges that move are electrons, which are loosely bound to their atoms and can drift through the metal lattice.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Battery pushes electrons", "Electrons drift through wire", "Push travels almost instantly", "Lamp lights at once"]}
### Slow electrons, fast signal
Each electron drifts surprisingly slowly: in a household wire carrying a few amps, its average drift is well under a millimetre per second. But the "push" travels along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, because the wire is already full of electrons, like a hose already full of water.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~0.1 mm/s", "l": "typical electron drift speed"}, {"v": "~2/3 of light speed", "l": "how fast the signal travels in a cable"}, {"v": "6.24 × 10^18", "l": "electrons per second in 1 amp"}]}
### Conventional current
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Conventional current", "items": ["drawn from + to −", "used on all diagrams"]}, "r": {"t": "Electron flow", "items": ["actually − to +", "electrons are negative"]}}
Benjamin Franklin labelled charges "positive" and "negative" in the 1740s, before electrons were discovered in 1897. His guess about direction stuck.
> Current is charge on the move; the push is fast even when the charges are slow.

# The water analogy
Electricity is invisible, so a water system makes a handy mental model. It isn't perfect, but it captures the key relationships.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Voltage", "b": "water pressure"}, {"a": "Current", "b": "flow rate"}, {"a": "Resistance", "b": "narrow pipe"}, {"a": "Battery", "b": "pump"}, {"a": "Switch", "b": "valve"}, {"a": "Wire", "b": "wide pipe"}]}
### What it explains well
• More pressure (voltage) pushes more flow (current)
• A narrower pipe (more resistance) reduces flow
• Flow is the same everywhere in a single pipe loop, just as current is the same everywhere in a series circuit
### Where it breaks down
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No leaks from open ends", "Wires don't empty", "Magnetic effects", "Alternating current"]}
An open switch doesn't spill electricity on the floor, and AC has no simple water equivalent.
### Units
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "volt (V)", "b": "pressure"}, {"a": "amp (A)", "b": "flow"}, {"a": "ohm (Ω)", "b": "restriction"}, {"a": "watt (W)", "b": "rate of work"}]}
> Voltage pushes, current flows, resistance restricts.

# Ohm's law
Georg Ohm published his law in 1827: in many materials, current is proportional to voltage. The ratio between them is the resistance.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "V = I × R", "items": [{"s": "V", "m": "voltage in volts"}, {"s": "I", "m": "current in amps"}, {"s": "R", "m": "resistance in ohms"}]}
### The triangle
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "V", "b": "I × R"}, {"a": "I", "b": "V ÷ R"}, {"a": "R", "b": "V ÷ I"}]}
### Worked examples
ex: A 230 V kettle element of 24 Ω | I = 230 ÷ 24 | ≈ 9.6 A
ex: A LED resistor: 5 V supply, 3 V LED, 20 mA wanted | R = (5 − 3) ÷ 0.02 | = 100 Ω
### Where it doesn't apply
Ohm's law holds for metals and resistors at constant temperature. Diodes, LEDs and transistors are "non-ohmic": their current doesn't rise in proportion to voltage. A lamp filament's resistance also rises as it heats up.
> Know any two of V, I and R, and you can find the third.

# Try it: dim the bulb
The simulator shows how voltage and resistance together set the current, and how the current turns into power in the bulb.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "P = V × I = I² × R = V² ÷ R", "items": [{"s": "P", "m": "power in watts"}, {"s": "V", "m": "volts"}, {"s": "I", "m": "amps"}, {"s": "R", "m": "ohms"}]}
### What to try
• Double the voltage with the same resistance: current doubles and power quadruples
• Double the resistance with the same voltage: current and power halve
• Find the setting where the bulb glows brightly without "burning out"
### Why power rises so fast
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "power in a 10 Ω bulb, W", "items": [{"l": "5 V", "v": 2.5}, {"l": "10 V", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "15 V", "v": 22.5}, {"l": "20 V", "v": 40.0, "hi": true}]}
Power grows with the square of voltage, which is why a small overvoltage can quickly burn out a component.
### Dimmers
Real dimmers don't add a resistor (that would waste energy as heat). They switch the power on and off very fast, or control LED drivers electronically.
> Power rises with the square of voltage or current.

# It's the current that hurts
The danger of electric shock depends on the current flowing through the body, its path and how long it lasts. Voltage matters because it drives that current through your body's resistance.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "effect of current through the body, mA (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Tingle", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Can't let go", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "RCD trips", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Fibrillation", "v": 75.0, "hi": true}]}
### Your body's resistance
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dry skin", "b": "thousands of ohms or more"}, {"a": "Wet or broken skin", "b": "can drop below 1,000 Ω"}, {"a": "Inside the body", "b": "roughly 500–1,000 Ω"}]}
ex: 230 V across wet hands, about 1,000 Ω | I = 230 ÷ 1,000 | ≈ 230 mA: potentially lethal
### Path and time
• Hand to hand or hand to foot crosses the chest and heart
• Longer exposure greatly increases harm, which is why speed of disconnection matters
• Alternating current at 50 Hz is especially good at triggering heart fibrillation
### RCD protection
A 30 mA RCD must trip within 300 milliseconds at its rated current, and much faster at higher leakage. It limits both current and time.
!! Low voltage means safe => around 50 V AC can be dangerous in wet conditions; that's why bathrooms and pools use even lower "safety extra-low voltage"

# Conductors and insulators
Materials differ enormously in how easily current flows through them. The difference comes from how tightly electrons are bound to atoms.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Conductors", "items": ["copper", "aluminium", "silver", "gold", "free electrons"]}, "r": {"t": "Insulators", "items": ["plastic", "rubber", "glass", "dry wood", "bound electrons"]}}
### Best conductors
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "resistivity, nΩ·m (lower is better)", "items": [{"l": "Silver", "v": 15.9, "hi": true}, {"l": "Copper", "v": 16.8}, {"l": "Gold", "v": 24.4}, {"l": "Aluminium", "v": 26.5}, {"l": "Iron", "v": 97.0}]}
### Why copper?
Silver conducts slightly better but costs far more. Copper is the best balance of conductivity, cost, flexibility and corrosion resistance. Aluminium is lighter and cheaper, so overhead power lines use it.
### In between
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Semiconductors: silicon", "Water with salts: conducts", "Human body: conducts", "Carbon: graphite conducts"]}
### Superconductors
Some materials, cooled to very low temperatures, conduct with zero resistance. MRI scanners use superconducting coils cooled by liquid helium.
> Pure water is a poor conductor; the dissolved salts in real water make it dangerous.

# Resistance of a wire
A wire's resistance depends on its material, length and cross-section. Longer wires resist more; thicker wires resist less.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "R = ρ × L ÷ A", "items": [{"s": "ρ", "m": "resistivity of the material"}, {"s": "L", "m": "length"}, {"s": "A", "m": "cross-sectional area"}]}
### Example
ex: 50 m of 2.5 mm² copper (25 m there and back) | R = 1.68×10⁻⁸ × 50 ÷ 2.5×10⁻⁶ | ≈ 0.34 Ω
At 16 A, that's a voltage drop of about 5.4 V, around 2% of 230 V.
### Why it matters
• Voltage drop: long runs deliver less voltage at the far end
• Heat: current through resistance produces heat (P = I²R)
• Wiring rules limit voltage drop, typically to a few percent
### Common sizes in homes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1.5 mm²", "b": "lighting circuits"}, {"a": "2.5 mm²", "b": "general sockets"}, {"a": "4–6 mm²", "b": "ovens, hobs, water heaters"}, {"a": "10 mm² and up", "b": "main supply and EV chargers"}]}
### Warm extension leads
A thin, coiled extension lead powering a heater can overheat: high current, small cross-section, and a coil that traps the heat.
> Long run or big load? Use a thicker cable.

# Short circuits
A short circuit creates a path with almost no resistance between conductors at different voltages. By Ohm's law, the current becomes enormous.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Insulation damaged", "Live touches neutral", "Resistance tiny", "Current thousands of amps", "Breaker trips in milliseconds"]}
### How big?
ex: 230 V across 0.1 Ω of wiring | I = 230 ÷ 0.1 | = 2,300 A
Home breakers are rated to safely interrupt fault currents of several thousand amps; the "6000" printed on many breakers means 6 kA.
### Causes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Damaged insulation", "Nails through cables", "Water ingress", "Loose connections", "Faulty appliances"]}
### Earth faults
A live wire touching a metal appliance case is an earth fault. With proper earthing, current flows to earth and the breaker or RCD trips, instead of the case becoming live.
> Fuses and breakers exist to cut a short circuit before the cable becomes a heater.

# Open and closed circuits
Current only flows around a complete loop from the source and back. Break the loop anywhere and the current stops everywhere in that loop.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Closed circuit", "items": ["complete loop", "current flows", "lamp on"]}, "r": {"t": "Open circuit", "items": ["gap in loop", "no current", "lamp off"]}}
### Ways a circuit opens
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Switch turned off", "Blown fuse", "Tripped breaker", "Broken wire", "Loose terminal"]}
### Troubleshooting
When something doesn't work, check the loop step by step: supply, protection, switch, wiring, then the device.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Is there power?", "Breaker or fuse OK?", "Switch working?", "Connections tight?", "Device faulty?"]}
### Safety note
An open switch on the neutral side would leave a lamp holder live even when "off". That's why switches must break the live conductor.
> No loop, no current.

# Series vs. parallel
Components can be connected one after another (series) or side by side (parallel). Homes use parallel wiring, but both arrangements appear in electronics.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Series", "items": ["one loop", "same current", "voltage shared", "one break stops all"]}, "r": {"t": "Parallel", "items": ["separate paths", "full voltage each", "independent"]}}
### Resistances
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Series", "b": "R total = R1 + R2 + …"}, {"a": "Parallel", "b": "1/R total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …"}]}
ex: Two 100 Ω resistors | Series: 200 Ω | Parallel: 50 Ω
### Christmas lights
Old strings wired in series went completely dark when one bulb failed. Modern strings use shunts or parallel sections so one failure doesn't kill the rest.
### Batteries
• In series, voltages add: two 1.5 V cells give 3 V
• In parallel, capacity adds at the same voltage
> Series shares voltage; parallel shares current.

# Your house is wired in parallel
Every socket and light in your home is connected in parallel between live and neutral, so each gets the full 230 V and works independently.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Supply", "Meter", "Main switch", "Breakers", "Circuits", "Sockets and lights in parallel"]}
### Colours of the wires
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Brown (or black or grey)", "b": "live (phase)"}, {"a": "Blue", "b": "neutral"}, {"a": "Green-yellow", "b": "protective earth"}]}
### Total current adds
ex: Kettle 9.6 A + toaster 4 A + microwave 5 A | = 18.6 A on the circuit | A 16 A breaker may trip
### Ring and radial circuits
Portugal and most of Europe use radial circuits: each runs out from the board to its sockets. The UK commonly uses ring circuits, which loop back to the board, with fused plugs.
> Parallel wiring lets each appliance work alone, but all share the supply.

# Power
Power is the rate at which energy is converted, measured in watts. For electrical devices, it's voltage times current.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "P = V × I", "items": [{"s": "P", "m": "power in watts"}, {"s": "V", "m": "voltage"}, {"s": "I", "m": "current"}]}
### Typical appliances
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "power, W", "items": [{"l": "LED bulb", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Laptop", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "TV", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Microwave", "v": 1000.0}, {"l": "Kettle", "v": 2200.0, "hi": true}]}
### Current drawn at 230 V
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "LED bulb 8 W", "b": "0.03 A"}, {"a": "Laptop 60 W", "b": "0.26 A"}, {"a": "Kettle 2,200 W", "b": "9.6 A"}, {"a": "Oven 3,500 W", "b": "15 A"}]}
### Heat appliances are hungry
Anything that heats (kettle, oven, heater, iron, hair dryer, water heater) draws large currents. Electronics draw little.
### Watts vs volt-amps
For motors and some electronics, voltage and current aren't perfectly in step, so "apparent power" (VA) can be higher than real power (W). Contracted power in Portugal is in kVA for this reason.
> Heating is power-hungry; electronics sip.

# Breakers vs. RCDs
A home consumer unit has two kinds of protection that do different jobs: breakers protect cables, RCDs protect people.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Breaker (MCB)", "items": ["too much current", "protects cables from overheating"]}, "r": {"t": "RCD", "items": ["current leaking to earth", "protects people from shock"]}}
### How a breaker works
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thermal trip", "b": "a bimetal strip bends with sustained overload"}, {"a": "Magnetic trip", "b": "a coil reacts instantly to short circuits"}]}
### How an RCD works
It compares current on live and neutral. Normally they're equal. If 30 mA goes missing, it must be leaking somewhere, possibly through a person, so the RCD trips within a fraction of a second.
### The test button

### In Portugal
Portuguese rules require 30 mA residual current protection for socket circuits in homes. RCBOs combine breaker and RCD in one device.
> Breakers save cables; RCDs save lives.

# Direct current
Direct current (DC) flows in one direction at a steady voltage. It's what batteries, solar panels and USB chargers provide, and what all electronics use internally.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "DC", "items": ["one direction", "steady voltage", "batteries", "electronics"]}, "r": {"t": "AC", "items": ["alternating direction", "wave", "mains", "grid"]}}
### Sources of DC
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Batteries", "Solar panels", "Fuel cells", "USB chargers", "Car electrical systems"]}
### Common DC voltages
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1.5 V", "b": "AA battery"}, {"a": "3.7 V", "b": "phone battery"}, {"a": "5 V", "b": "USB"}, {"a": "12 V", "b": "car battery"}, {"a": "400–800 V", "b": "electric car battery"}]}
### Inside electronics
Chips need precise, steady DC, often 1–5 V. Power supplies convert mains AC to clean DC for them.
> Electronics run on DC; the grid runs on AC.

# Alternating current
In alternating current (AC), the voltage rises and falls in a smooth wave, reversing direction many times a second.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "50 Hz", "l": "European mains frequency"}, {"v": "20 ms", "l": "duration of one cycle"}, {"v": "100", "l": "reversals per second"}]}
### One cycle
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Voltage rises to +325 V", "Falls through zero", "Reaches −325 V", "Returns to zero", "Repeats 50 times a second"]}
### Why lights don't flicker visibly
Incandescent filaments stay hot between peaks. LED lamps need a good driver to avoid flicker at 100 Hz, which some people can perceive.
### Hertz
The unit honours Heinrich Hertz, who demonstrated radio waves in the 1880s. One hertz means one cycle per second.
> AC is a wave; 50 Hz means 50 full cycles every second.

# Why 230 V isn't the peak
Mains voltage is quoted as an RMS (root mean square) value: the DC voltage that would produce the same heating effect. The actual peaks are higher.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "V peak = V RMS × √2", "items": [{"s": "V RMS", "m": "230 V"}, {"s": "√2", "m": "about 1.414"}, {"s": "V peak", "m": "about 325 V"}]}
### The numbers
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "RMS", "b": "230 V"}, {"a": "Peak", "b": "about 325 V"}, {"a": "Peak to peak", "b": "about 650 V"}]}
### Why RMS?
RMS lets you use the same formulas as for DC. A 2,300 W heater on 230 V AC draws 10 A RMS and produces the same heat as on 230 V DC.
### Practical consequences
• Insulation and components must withstand the peaks, not just 230 V
• Multimeters usually display RMS values
• "True RMS" meters measure correctly even when the waveform isn't a perfect sine
!? Europe harmonised mains at 230 V in the late 1980s, bridging older 220 V and 240 V systems; tolerances allow roughly 207–253 V.

# The war of the currents
In the 1880s and 1890s, Thomas Edison and George Westinghouse fought over which kind of current would power the world.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1882", "l": "Edison's DC Pearl Street station"}, {"y": "1888", "l": "Tesla's AC motor patents"}, {"y": "1893", "l": "AC lights Chicago World's Fair"}, {"y": "1896", "l": "Niagara Falls AC power"}]}
### Edison's DC
Edison's DC systems worked well but had to be near customers: low-voltage DC couldn't travel far without big losses, so power stations were needed every mile or so.
### Westinghouse and Tesla's AC
Westinghouse bought Nikola Tesla's AC motor patents. With transformers, AC could be sent at high voltage over long distances and stepped down for homes.
### The fight
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Edison campaigned on AC dangers", "Public demonstrations", "Electric chair controversy", "Price wars"]}
### The outcome
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "DC (Edison)", "items": ["short distances", "many stations"]}, "r": {"t": "AC (Westinghouse)", "items": ["long distances", "transformers", "won the grid"]}}
!? Edison's company later merged into General Electric, which itself went on to build AC systems.

# Why AC for the grid?
Power losses in cables depend on the square of the current. Sending the same power at higher voltage means lower current and much smaller losses.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Loss = I² × R", "items": [{"s": "I", "m": "current in the line"}, {"s": "R", "m": "resistance of the line"}]}

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1 kV", "b": "losses × 10,000"}, {"a": "10 kV", "b": "losses × 100"}, {"a": "100 kV", "b": "losses × 1"}]}

### Transformers
A transformer uses two coils on an iron core. A changing current in one induces a voltage in the other, stepped up or down by the ratio of turns. It only works with changing current, which is why AC won.

# Magnetic fields
A magnet produces a magnetic field around it. Opposite poles attract and like poles repel. Field lines run from north to south outside the magnet.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Opposite poles", "items": ["N–S attract"]}, "r": {"t": "Like poles", "items": ["N–N or S–S repel"]}}
### Field lines
Iron filings sprinkled near a magnet line up along the field, showing curved lines that bunch together where the field is strongest, near the poles.
### Units
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Earth's field", "b": "about 25–65 microtesla"}, {"a": "Fridge magnet", "b": "about 5 millitesla"}, {"a": "Neodymium magnet", "b": "about 1 tesla"}, {"a": "MRI scanner", "b": "1.5–3 tesla"}]}
### Where magnetism comes from
Magnetism comes from moving charges and from the "spin" of electrons. In iron, nickel and cobalt, electron spins line up in regions called domains.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Unmagnetised: domains random", "Strong field applied", "Domains align", "Permanent magnet"]}
!? Cutting a magnet in half doesn't separate north and south: you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles.

# Earth is a magnet
Earth's magnetic field is generated by moving molten iron in the outer core, acting like a giant dynamo. A compass needle lines up with this field.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Liquid iron moves in outer core", "Electric currents", "Magnetic field", "Compass needle aligns"]}
### Magnetic vs geographic north
The magnetic north pole wanders. During the 20th century it drifted across northern Canada, and since the 1990s it has moved quickly towards Siberia, at tens of kilometres a year.
### Declination

### Protection
The magnetic field deflects much of the solar wind and cosmic radiation, helping protect the atmosphere and life.
> Earth's core is a dynamo; your compass is reading it.

# Current makes a field
In 1820, Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a compass needle moved when placed near a wire carrying current. It was the first proof that electricity and magnetism are linked.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1820", "l": "Ørsted's discovery"}, {"y": "1820", "l": "Ampère's laws"}, {"y": "1831", "l": "Faraday's induction"}, {"y": "1865", "l": "Maxwell unifies both"}]}
### The right-hand grip rule
Point your right thumb along the current; your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field circling the wire.
### Field strength
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "More current", "b": "stronger field"}, {"a": "Closer to wire", "b": "stronger field"}, {"a": "Coiled wire", "b": "fields add up"}]}
### Electromagnetism
James Clerk Maxwell later showed that electricity, magnetism and light are all aspects of one electromagnetic field, described by four equations.
> Every current creates a magnetic field.

# Electromagnets
Coil a wire and the fields from each turn add up. Put an iron core inside and the field becomes hundreds or thousands of times stronger. Turn off the current, and the magnet turns off.

### Strength

# Induction
In 1831, Michael Faraday discovered that a changing magnetic field induces a voltage in a nearby conductor. It's the principle behind generators, transformers and induction hobs.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Magnet moves into coil", "Field through coil changes", "Voltage induced", "Current flows"]}
### Faraday's law
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Induced voltage ∝ rate of change of magnetic flux", "items": [{"s": "Faster change", "m": "higher voltage"}, {"s": "More turns", "m": "higher voltage"}]}
### Key point: change
A stationary magnet next to a coil induces nothing. Only changing fields induce voltage, which is why transformers need AC.
### Applications
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Generators", "Transformers", "Induction hobs", "Wireless charging", "Metal detectors", "Electric guitar pickups"]}
### Lenz's law
The induced current opposes the change that caused it. Drop a magnet through a copper tube and it falls slowly, braked by its own induced currents.
> Changing magnetism makes electricity.

# Induction hobs

# The motor effect
A wire carrying current inside a magnetic field feels a force at right angles to both. Bend the wire into a loop on an axle and the forces turn it.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = B × I × L", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "force in newtons"}, {"s": "B", "m": "magnetic field in tesla"}, {"s": "I", "m": "current"}, {"s": "L", "m": "length of wire in the field"}]}
### From force to rotation
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Current in loop", "One side pushed up, other side down", "Torque", "Loop turns"]}
### Direction
Fleming's left-hand rule: thumb for motion, first finger for field, second finger for current, all at right angles.
### History
Michael Faraday demonstrated the first electric motor in 1821: a wire rotating around a magnet in a cup of mercury.
### Stronger motors
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Stronger magnets", "More current", "More turns", "Longer conductors in field"]}
> Current plus magnetic field equals force.

# DC motors
Simple DC motors use a commutator, a split ring that reverses the current in the coil every half turn, so it keeps spinning the same way.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Brushed DC", "items": ["commutator and brushes", "simple", "brushes wear"]}, "r": {"t": "Brushless DC", "items": ["electronic switching", "long life", "efficient"]}}

The speed of a DC motor rises with voltage. Electronic controllers vary the average voltage by switching very fast (pulse-width modulation).

# Induction motors
The AC induction motor is the workhorse of industry. It has no brushes and a simple, tough rotor, and it starts directly from the mains.

# Transformers
A transformer changes AC voltage using two coils wound on an iron core. The ratio of turns sets the ratio of voltages.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Vs / Vp = Ns / Np", "items": [{"s": "Vp, Vs", "m": "primary and secondary voltage"}, {"s": "Np, Ns", "m": "number of turns"}]}
### Example
ex: Primary 10,000 V, 1,000 turns | Secondary 23 turns | ≈ 230 V
### Energy conserved
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Step up", "items": ["higher voltage", "lower current"]}, "r": {"t": "Step down", "items": ["lower voltage", "higher current"]}}
Power in ≈ power out: big transformers are over 99% efficient.
### Where you see them
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Pole-mounted transformers", "Neighbourhood substations", "Phone chargers (tiny ones)", "Welding machines"]}
### The hum
The 100 Hz hum near transformers comes from the iron core changing size slightly with each magnetic cycle, an effect called magnetostriction.
> Transformers trade voltage for current, almost without loss.

# Why high voltage?
Line losses depend on the square of the current. Raising the voltage lowers the current for the same power, slashing losses.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Loss = I² × R", "items": [{"s": "I", "m": "current"}, {"s": "R", "m": "line resistance"}]}
### Doubling voltage
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative losses for the same power, %", "items": [{"l": "200 kV", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "400 kV", "v": 25.0, "hi": true}]}
Doubling voltage halves the current and cuts losses to a quarter.
### Trade-offs
• Higher voltages need taller towers and bigger insulators
• Wider clearances to the ground and buildings
• More expensive equipment at substations
### Typical choices
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Long-distance", "b": "400 kV or more"}, {"a": "Regional", "b": "60–150 kV"}, {"a": "Towns", "b": "10–30 kV"}, {"a": "Streets", "b": "400/230 V"}]}
> High voltage carries power far; transformers make it safe at home.

# Balancing supply and demand
Electricity must be produced at the same moment it's consumed. Grid operators balance supply and demand continuously, second by second.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Forecast demand", "Schedule generation", "Monitor frequency", "Adjust reserves", "Forecast again"]}
### Tools for balance
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Flexible power plants", "Hydro and pumped storage", "Batteries", "Imports and exports", "Demand response"]}
### Reserves
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Primary", "b": "automatic response in seconds"}, {"a": "Secondary", "b": "within minutes"}, {"a": "Tertiary", "b": "replaces earlier reserves over longer periods"}]}
### Forecasting
Operators forecast demand using weather, calendars and past patterns, and forecast wind and solar output from weather models. Errors are covered by reserves.
### Demand response
Large consumers, and increasingly households with smart devices, can reduce or shift use when the grid is stressed, often for payment.
> The grid is a balancing act that never stops.

# The daily demand curve
Electricity demand follows daily rhythms. Solar power has changed when electricity is cheapest, shifting cheap hours to midday.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Night", "l": "low demand"}, {"y": "Morning", "l": "rise"}, {"y": "Midday", "l": "solar peak"}, {"y": "Evening", "l": "demand peak"}, {"y": "Late night", "l": "falls"}]}
### The duck curve
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without solar", "items": ["midday demand met by plants"]}, "r": {"t": "With lots of solar", "items": ["midday net demand dips", "steep evening ramp"]}}
The shape of net demand (demand minus solar) looks like a duck, first described in California around 2013.
### What it means for prices
• Midday prices can fall very low, even negative, on sunny days
• Evening peaks become more expensive
• Batteries charge at midday and discharge in the evening
### For homes

# The April 2025 blackout
On 28 April 2025, shortly after 12:30 in Spain (11:30 in Portugal), a cascade of disconnections left almost all of mainland Spain and Portugal without power, in one of Europe's largest blackouts in decades.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "11:33", "l": "grid collapses in Iberia"}, {"y": "Afternoon", "l": "restoration begins"}, {"y": "Night", "l": "most supply restored"}, {"y": "Next morning", "l": "full service"}]}
### What happened
Investigations pointed to voltage control problems: rising voltages caused generators to disconnect in sequence, and the grid lost synchronism with France within seconds.
### Black start
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plants with black-start ability start without grid", "Energise lines", "Reconnect other plants", "Reconnect customers step by step"]}
In Portugal, hydro and gas plants able to start without external power were used to rebuild the grid.
### Effects
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Trains and metros stopped", "Traffic lights off", "Mobile networks failed", "Hospitals on generators", "Shops closed"]}
> A modern society runs on electricity; restoring it from zero takes hours.

# How a battery works
A battery converts chemical energy into electrical energy. Two electrodes with different chemical potential are separated by an electrolyte that lets ions move but not electrons.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chemical reaction at anode releases electrons", "Electrons flow through your circuit", "Ions move through electrolyte", "Electrons rejoin at cathode"]}
### In a lithium-ion cell
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Discharging", "b": "lithium ions move from anode (graphite) to cathode"}, {"a": "Charging", "b": "charger pushes them back to the anode"}, {"a": "Separator", "b": "thin porous film keeping electrodes apart"}]}
### History
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1800", "l": "Volta's pile"}, {"y": "1859", "l": "lead-acid battery"}, {"y": "1991", "l": "Sony sells first Li-ion"}, {"y": "2019", "l": "Nobel Prize for Li-ion"}]}
### Primary vs secondary
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Primary", "items": ["single use", "alkaline AA"]}, "r": {"t": "Secondary", "items": ["rechargeable", "Li-ion", "lead-acid", "NiMH"]}}
> A battery is a controlled chemical reaction that pushes electrons through your circuit.

# Capacity: Ah and Wh
Battery capacity is quoted in amp-hours (Ah) or milliamp-hours (mAh), but energy in watt-hours (Wh) is the fair way to compare batteries of different voltages.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Wh = Ah × V", "items": [{"s": "Ah", "m": "charge capacity"}, {"s": "V", "m": "nominal voltage"}, {"s": "Wh", "m": "energy stored"}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Phone 5,000 mAh at 3.85 V", "b": "about 19 Wh"}, {"a": "Laptop", "b": "50–100 Wh"}, {"a": "E-bike", "b": "400–700 Wh"}, {"a": "Electric car", "b": "40–100 kWh"}]}
### Power banks
A 10,000 mAh power bank stores about 37 Wh at 3.7 V. After conversion losses, it can deliver roughly 60–70% of that to a phone.
### Airline rules
Airlines generally allow power banks up to 100 Wh in cabin baggage without approval, and between 100 and 160 Wh with airline approval. They're banned from checked bags.
> Compare batteries in watt-hours, not milliamp-hours.

# Battery chemistries
Different battery chemistries trade energy density, cost, safety and lifespan.

### Common types
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lead-acid", "b": "cheap, heavy, car starter batteries"}, {"a": "NiMH", "b": "older hybrids, AA rechargeables"}, {"a": "LFP", "b": "safe, long-life, storage and many EVs"}, {"a": "NMC", "b": "high energy, premium EVs, laptops"}]}

Lithium iron phosphate cells contain no cobalt or nickel, are cheaper and tolerate more cycles. They now power a large share of EVs, especially in China, and most home batteries.

# C-rate
The C-rate expresses charge or discharge current relative to capacity. It shows how hard a battery is being worked.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Current = C-rate × capacity", "items": [{"s": "1C", "m": "full in about 1 hour"}, {"s": "0.5C", "m": "2 hours"}, {"s": "2C", "m": "30 minutes"}]}
### Examples
ex: 3,000 mAh battery at 1C | = 3 A | about 1 hour to charge or discharge
ex: 75 kWh EV charging at 150 kW | = 2C | 10–80% in roughly 20–30 minutes
### Why it matters
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low C-rate", "items": ["cool", "gentle", "long life"]}, "r": {"t": "High C-rate", "items": ["heat", "stress", "faster ageing"]}}
### Charging curve
Fast charging slows down above about 80%, because the cell's voltage rises and the charger reduces current to protect it. That's why EV charging stops are often 10–80%.
> High C-rates are fast but hard on batteries.

# State of charge and cycles
Lithium-ion batteries age faster when kept at very high or very low charge for long periods, especially when warm.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 100.0, "unit": "% charge", "items": [{"l": "Stressful low", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Sweet spot start", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Sweet spot end", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Stressful high", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}]}
### Practical advice
• For daily use, charging to about 80% extends life
• Charge to 100% before long trips, then drive soon after
• Avoid leaving devices fully charged in hot cars or sun
• Store long-term at around 40–60%
### Phone features
Many phones offer "optimised charging", pausing at 80% overnight and finishing just before you wake.
### LFP is different
LFP batteries tolerate 100% charge better, and some manufacturers recommend charging them fully regularly so the battery management system can calibrate.
> Middle charge, moderate temperature: that's where lithium is happiest.

# Why batteries age
Every charge and discharge causes tiny, irreversible chemical changes that slowly reduce capacity and increase internal resistance.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Electrolyte breakdown", "Protective layer growth", "Lithium plating", "Cathode cracking", "Heat"]}
### Main stress factors
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative ageing effect (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Heat", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "High charge", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Fast charging", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Deep discharge", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Time alone", "v": 3.0}]}
### Calendar vs cycle ageing
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Calendar ageing", "items": ["happens even unused", "worse when hot and full"]}, "r": {"t": "Cycle ageing", "items": ["from use", "worse with deep cycles and fast charging"]}}
### End of life
A battery is usually considered "worn out" at 70–80% of original capacity. EV batteries at that point can find a second life in stationary storage.
> Heat is a battery's worst enemy.

# Cycle life
Cycle life is the number of full charge-discharge cycles before capacity falls to a threshold, often 80%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical cycles to 80% capacity (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Lead-acid", "v": 500.0}, {"l": "NMC", "v": 1500.0}, {"l": "LFP", "v": 4000.0, "hi": true}]}
### What it means for EVs
ex: 1,500 cycles × 400 km per cycle | = 600,000 km | well beyond most cars' lifetime
### Partial cycles
Two charges from 50% to 100% count roughly as one full cycle. Shallow cycles are gentler than deep ones.
### Real-world data
Studies of real EV fleets show many batteries keep around 90% of capacity after 100,000–200,000 km, better than many early predictions.
> For home storage cycling every day, LFP's long life is a big advantage.

# Battery management systems
A battery management system (BMS) monitors and protects every cell in a lithium pack. Large lithium packs should never run without one.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Monitor cell voltages", "Measure temperatures", "Balance cells", "Limit current", "Disconnect on faults", "Estimate charge"]}
### Cell balancing
Cells in a pack drift apart slightly. The BMS balances them so no cell is overcharged or over-discharged while others are still mid-range.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Measure every cell", "Find highest", "Bleed or redistribute charge", "All cells aligned"]}
### State of charge
The BMS estimates remaining charge from voltage, current and temperature. That's the percentage on your phone or EV dashboard.
### Protection
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Overcharge", "b": "stop charging"}, {"a": "Over-discharge", "b": "cut load"}, {"a": "Over-temperature", "b": "reduce power or stop"}, {"a": "Short circuit", "b": "disconnect instantly"}]}
> The BMS is the battery's brain and bodyguard.

# Thermal runaway
Thermal runaway is a self-heating chain reaction in a lithium cell. Once started, it releases heat and flammable gases and can spread to neighbouring cells.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Damage, overcharge or overheating", "Internal short", "Heat", "Electrolyte decomposes", "More heat", "Fire and gas", "Spreads to next cell"]}
### Causes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Physical damage", "Manufacturing defects", "Overcharging", "External heat", "Poor quality chargers"]}
### Why fires are hard
Burning cells produce their own oxygen from the cathode, so smothering doesn't work well. EV fires need large amounts of water for cooling and can reignite hours or days later.
### LFP is safer
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "NMC", "items": ["runaway at lower temperatures", "more energetic"]}, "r": {"t": "LFP", "items": ["more stable chemistry", "runaway much harder to trigger"]}}
### Warning signs
• Swelling or bulging
• Unusual heat
• Hissing or sweet smell
> A swollen battery is a warning: stop using it and dispose of it safely.

Most EVs use around 400 V packs. Some newer cars use 800 V architectures, allowing faster charging with lower current and thinner cables.

> Series for voltage, parallel for capacity.

# Orientation and tilt
Panel orientation and tilt determine how much sunlight they capture over the year.

### The optimum
In Portugal, south-facing panels tilted at roughly 30–35° collect the most energy over the year, close to the latitude minus a few degrees.
### East–west layouts
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "South", "items": ["maximum total energy", "peak at noon"]}, "r": {"t": "East–west", "items": ["less total", "morning and evening production", "matches household use"]}}
### Flat roofs
Panels on flat roofs are usually tilted on frames, or laid flatter in east–west rows to fit more panels on the roof.
> South at 30–35° maximises energy; east–west better matches daily use.

# Shade matters
Shading even a small part of a solar panel can reduce output disproportionately, because cells in series all carry the same current.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Leaf shades one cell", "Cell can't pass full current", "Whole string limited", "Bypass diode skips section", "Partial loss"]}
### Bypass diodes
Panels contain bypass diodes, typically three per panel, that route current around shaded sections, limiting the loss to part of the panel.
### Optimisers and microinverters
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "String inverter only", "items": ["shade on one panel affects the string"]}, "r": {"t": "Optimisers or microinverters", "items": ["each panel works independently"]}}
### Hot spots
A shaded cell forced to carry current can heat up and be damaged. Persistent partial shading, such as from a chimney, should be avoided in design.
> Check for chimneys, trees and antennas before deciding where panels go.

# Heat lowers output
Solar cells work better when cool. Silicon panels lose about 0.3–0.4% of output for every °C above 25 °C.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Loss ≈ temperature coefficient × (T cell − 25 °C)", "items": [{"s": "Coefficient", "m": "about −0.35%/°C"}, {"s": "T cell", "m": "often 20–35 °C above air temperature"}]}
### Example
ex: Cell at 60 °C | 35 °C above STC × 0.35% | ≈ 12% less output
### Cooling helps
• Mounting with air gaps behind panels
• Panels on light-coloured roofs
• Breezy locations
### Best months
In Portugal, spring often gives excellent output: strong sun, long days and cooler temperatures than midsummer.
!? Floating solar farms on reservoirs, such as Alqueva's, benefit from water cooling, which slightly improves efficiency.

== el-3phase

# Three waves, offset
Three-phase power uses three alternating voltages of the same frequency, each shifted by a third of a cycle (120°). Together they deliver power smoothly and efficiently.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["L1 peaks", "120° later: L2 peaks", "120° later: L3 peaks", "back to L1"]}
### Why smooth matters
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Single-phase", "items": ["power pulses 100 times a second", "drops to zero"]}, "r": {"t": "Three-phase", "items": ["total power almost constant", "never zero"]}}
Constant power means motors run smoothly with less vibration.
### Balanced loads
When the three phases carry equal loads, their currents cancel in the neutral, which then carries almost nothing.
### Invention
Polyphase AC systems were developed in the late 1880s by Nikola Tesla, Galileo Ferraris and Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky, who built the first three-phase transmission line in Germany in 1891.
> Three overlapping waves deliver smooth, continuous power.

# 230 V and 400 V
A three-phase supply provides two voltages: 230 V between any phase and neutral, and 400 V between any two phases.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "V line = √3 × V phase", "items": [{"s": "V phase", "m": "230 V"}, {"s": "√3", "m": "about 1.732"}, {"s": "V line", "m": "about 400 V"}]}
### Using both
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Phase to neutral", "b": "230 V: sockets, lights"}, {"a": "Phase to phase", "b": "400 V: three-phase motors, big heaters"}, {"a": "Three phases together", "b": "EV chargers, heat pumps"}]}
### Why √3?
Because the waves are 120° apart, the difference between two phases is √3 times the voltage of one phase, not double.
### At home
Even in a three-phase home, normal sockets still provide 230 V. Only special outlets or appliances use 400 V.
> 230 V to neutral, 400 V between phases.

# Three-phase power
Three-phase systems carry about three times the power of a single phase using the same current per conductor, with only modest extra copper.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "P = √3 × V line × I × cos φ", "items": [{"s": "V line", "m": "400 V"}, {"s": "I", "m": "current per phase"}, {"s": "cos φ", "m": "power factor"}]}
### Examples
ex: 16 A three-phase at 400 V | P = 1.732 × 400 × 16 | ≈ 11 kW
ex: 32 A three-phase at 400 V | P ≈ 22 kW
### Comparison
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "max power at 32 A per conductor, kW", "items": [{"l": "Single-phase", "v": 7.4}, {"l": "Three-phase", "v": 22.0, "hi": true}]}
### Copper efficiency
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Three single-phase circuits", "items": ["six conductors"]}, "r": {"t": "One three-phase circuit", "items": ["three or four conductors", "same power"]}}
> Same current per wire, three times the power.

# Why industry loves it
Three-phase power is the standard for generation, transmission and industrial loads worldwide.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Self-starting motors", "Smaller motors", "Less copper", "Smoother power", "Efficient transmission"]}
### Motors
Three-phase currents create a rotating magnetic field naturally. Induction motors start and run without extra starting devices, and are smaller than single-phase motors of the same power.
### Transmission
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Single-phase", "items": ["two conductors", "pulsating power"]}, "r": {"t": "Three-phase", "items": ["three conductors", "constant power", "less copper per kW"]}}
### Why not more phases?
Six or twelve phases are possible, but three is the best compromise between smoothness and the number of conductors.
> Three phases: the sweet spot of power engineering.

# Three-phase at home
Many Portuguese homes have single-phase supply, but larger homes and those with heavy loads can opt for three-phase.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Single-phase", "items": ["simpler", "up to about 10.35 kVA", "enough for most flats"]}, "r": {"t": "Three-phase", "items": ["higher contracted power", "11–22 kW EV charging", "big heat pumps"]}}
### When it makes sense
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Fast EV charging", "Large heat pumps", "Workshops with machines", "Large houses", "Big solar systems"]}
### Costs
• Installation changes and possibly a new meter
• Fixed charges depend on contracted power, not the number of phases
### Distributing circuits
Circuits are spread across the three phases so each carries a similar load.
!? Some EVs can only charge at 7.4 kW on single phase but 11 kW on three-phase, cutting home charging time significantly.

# Balancing the phases
Loads should be spread evenly across the three phases. Unbalance wastes capacity and puts extra current on the neutral.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "example current per phase, A", "items": [{"l": "L1 (overloaded)", "v": 28.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "L2", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "L3", "v": 6.0}]}
### Problems with unbalance
• One phase trips while others have spare capacity
• Neutral carries the imbalance current
• Three-phase motors run hotter and less efficiently
### Planning
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["List circuits and loads", "Assign to phases", "Check peak per phase", "Adjust", "Verify with clamp meter"]}
### Harmonics
Some electronic loads produce harmonic currents that add up in the neutral instead of cancelling, so neutrals in office buildings may carry high currents even when phases are balanced.
> Share the load and every phase does its fair part.

# Phase rotation
The order in which the three phases peak (L1, L2, L3) is called phase rotation or sequence. It determines which way a three-phase motor spins.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Motor runs backwards after installation", "r": "Swap any two phases"}]}
### Why it matters
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Pumps", "Fans", "Conveyors", "Compressors", "Lifts"]}
A pump running backwards may move little water and can be damaged; a conveyor may run the wrong way.
### Checking
Electricians use a phase rotation tester before connecting motors.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Connect tester to L1, L2, L3", "Indicator shows clockwise or anticlockwise", "Correct if needed"]}
> Swap two phases, reverse the motor.

# Star and delta
Three-phase windings can be connected in two ways, each giving a different voltage across each winding.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Star (Y)", "items": ["one end of each winding joined", "230 V per winding on 400 V supply", "lower current"]}, "r": {"t": "Delta (Δ)", "items": ["windings in a triangle", "400 V per winding", "full power"]}}
### Star-delta starting
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Start in star: lower voltage per winding", "Starting current about a third", "Motor reaches speed", "Switch to delta: full power"]}
### Why reduce starting current
Large motors draw five to seven times their rated current when starting, causing voltage dips. Star-delta starters, soft starters and drives reduce this.
### Transformers too
Distribution transformers often use delta on the high-voltage side and star on the low-voltage side, providing a neutral for 230 V loads.
> Star to start gently, delta to run at full power.

# Grids are three-phase worldwide
From generator to street transformer, power travels as three-phase. Single-phase homes are connected to one of the three phases.

== el-pf

# Real and apparent power
When voltage and current don't rise and fall together, volts times amps (apparent power) exceeds the power doing useful work (real power).
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Real power (W) = V × I × cos φ", "items": [{"s": "V × I", "m": "apparent power in VA"}, {"s": "cos φ", "m": "power factor"}, {"s": "φ", "m": "angle between voltage and current"}]}
### Example
ex: Motor draws 10 A at 230 V | Apparent power = 2,300 VA | With power factor 0.8: real power = 1,840 W
### What's happening
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Resistive load", "items": ["voltage and current in step", "PF = 1"]}, "r": {"t": "Inductive load", "items": ["current lags voltage", "PF < 1"]}}
### Why it costs
The extra current still flows through cables and transformers, causing losses and taking capacity, even though it does no useful work.
> Power factor measures how usefully current is used.

# The beer analogy
A glass of beer helps visualise power factor: liquid, foam and the whole glass.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Whole glass: apparent power (VA)", "Foam: reactive power (var)", "Beer: real power (W)"]}
### Reading the analogy
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Real power", "b": "the beer: does the work"}, {"a": "Reactive power", "b": "the foam: takes space, no work"}, {"a": "Apparent power", "b": "the full glass: what the system must carry"}]}
### Less foam is better
A high power factor means mostly beer: the same glass delivers more useful power.
### Where it falls short
Reactive power isn't truly wasted: it sustains magnetic fields that motors and transformers need. It just flows back and forth without being consumed.
> Pay for the beer, but the glass must hold the foam too.

# What causes it
Low power factor comes from loads that store energy in magnetic or electric fields, or that draw current in pulses.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Displacement", "items": ["motors, transformers", "current lags voltage"]}, "r": {"t": "Distortion", "items": ["cheap electronics", "current in pulses", "harmonics"]}}
### Inductive loads
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Induction motors", "Transformers", "Fluorescent ballasts", "Welding machines"]}
Coils store energy in magnetic fields during part of each cycle and release it later, so their current lags the voltage.
### Electronics
Simple power supplies charge a capacitor only near the voltage peak, drawing short spikes of current. This distortion also lowers power factor.
### Capacitive loads
Long underground cables and some LED drivers draw leading current, partly offsetting inductive loads.
> Coils make current lag; cheap electronics make it spiky.

# Why it matters
A low power factor means more current for the same useful work, increasing losses and requiring bigger equipment.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "current needed for 10 kW at 400 V three-phase, A", "items": [{"l": "PF 1.0", "v": 14.4, "hi": true}, {"l": "PF 0.9", "v": 16.0}, {"l": "PF 0.8", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "PF 0.6", "v": 24.0}]}
### Consequences
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Bigger cables", "Higher losses", "Larger transformers", "Voltage drop", "Penalty charges"]}
### Utility charges
In Portugal, larger commercial and industrial customers are charged for reactive energy when their power factor falls below set thresholds, encouraging correction.
### Grid perspective
Reactive power also affects voltage levels across the grid. Operators manage it carefully, and inverters at solar farms help regulate it.
> Poor power factor makes everything carry more current than necessary.

# Correction with capacitors
Capacitors draw current that leads the voltage, cancelling the lagging current of motors. Factories install capacitor banks to keep the power factor close to 1.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Motor current lags", "Capacitor current leads", "They cancel", "Supply sees mostly real power"]}
### Types of correction
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fixed", "b": "one capacitor per motor"}, {"a": "Automatic banks", "b": "switch capacitors in steps as load changes"}, {"a": "Active filters", "b": "electronics that correct dynamically"}]}
### Typical target
Many installations aim for a power factor of 0.95 or above, avoiding penalties and freeing capacity.
### Caution
Over-correction makes the power factor leading, which can cause overvoltage. Capacitors can also resonate with harmonics, so design matters.
> Capacitors supply the reactive power locally so the grid doesn't have to.

# Units: W, VA, var
Three related units describe the components of AC power.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "W (watt)", "b": "real power: does work"}, {"a": "var", "b": "reactive power: oscillates"}, {"a": "VA (volt-ampere)", "b": "apparent power: what wiring carries"}, {"a": "Wh / kWh", "b": "energy over time"}]}
### Why VA ratings
UPS units, generators and transformers are rated in VA or kVA because their windings and electronics must handle the total current, whatever the power factor.
ex: UPS rated 1,000 VA, power factor 0.6 | Maximum real power = 600 W
### Generators
Generators are usually rated in kVA at a power factor of 0.8. A 10 kVA generator delivers about 8 kW of real power.
> Watts do the work; VA sizes the equipment.

# The power triangle
Real power and reactive power are at right angles, like two sides of a right-angled triangle. Apparent power is the hypotenuse.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "VA² = W² + var²", "items": [{"s": "W", "m": "real power"}, {"s": "var", "m": "reactive power"}, {"s": "VA", "m": "apparent power"}, {"s": "", "m": "cos φ = W ÷ VA"}]}
### Example
ex: Real power 8 kW, reactive 6 kvar | Apparent = √(64 + 36) = 10 kVA | Power factor = 0.8
### Using it
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Known W and PF", "b": "find VA = W ÷ PF"}, {"a": "Known VA and PF", "b": "find W = VA × PF"}, {"a": "Correction needed", "b": "reduce var"}]}
### Angle
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 90.0, "unit": "degrees", "items": [{"l": "PF 1.0", "v": 0.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "PF 0.9", "v": 26.0}, {"l": "PF 0.8", "v": 37.0}, {"l": "PF 0.5", "v": 60.0}]}
> The smaller the angle, the closer to 1 the power factor.

# Your home bill
Portuguese households pay only for active energy (kWh), not reactive energy. But contracted power is in kVA, which reflects current capacity.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Households", "items": ["pay kWh", "contracted power in kVA", "no reactive charges"]}, "r": {"t": "Businesses", "items": ["pay kWh", "reactive energy charges above thresholds"]}}
### Why kVA for contracted power
The limit protects cables and network capacity, which depend on current, not on how useful that current is. kVA captures current directly.
ex: 6.9 kVA | ÷ 230 V | = 30 A
### At home
Most household loads are resistive or have power factor correction, so kW and kVA are similar. Old motors and cheap electronics lower it slightly.
> At home you pay for watts; your limit is in volt-amps.

# Power factor of devices
Different appliances have very different power factors, depending on how they draw current.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical power factor (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Kettle, heater", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Laptop with PFC", "v": 0.95}, {"l": "Motor full load", "v": 0.85}, {"l": "Motor light load", "v": 0.4}, {"l": "Cheap LED bulb", "v": 0.5}]}
### Lightly loaded motors
A motor running at a fraction of its rated load still needs its magnetising current, so its power factor drops sharply. Oversized motors waste capacity.
### Small electronics
Small LED bulbs and phone chargers often have low power factors, but their currents are tiny, so the impact is small.
### Measuring
Plug-in energy monitors often display power factor, a fun way to explore appliances at home.
> Resistive loads are perfect; motors and cheap electronics are not.

# PFC in chargers
Power factor correction (PFC) circuits make electronic power supplies draw current that follows the voltage wave smoothly, instead of in spikes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["AC input", "PFC stage shapes current", "DC bus", "Converter", "Device"]}
### Why required
EU rules on harmonic currents (standard EN 61000-3-2) set limits for equipment above about 75 W, effectively requiring PFC in larger power supplies.
### Benefits
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Less current for same power", "Fewer harmonics", "Less heat in wiring", "Better use of grid capacity"]}
### Active vs passive
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Passive PFC", "items": ["inductors", "simple", "heavier"]}, "r": {"t": "Active PFC", "items": ["electronics", "power factor above 0.95", "compact"]}}
> Good chargers draw current as smoothly as a heater.

The car's electronics, pumps and battery management run during charging. At low power, these fixed losses form a larger share.

# Time-of-use tariffs
Time-of-use tariffs charge different prices at different times of day. Cheaper off-peak hours reward shifting consumption.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simple", "items": ["same price all day"]}, "r": {"t": "Bi-horário", "items": ["cheaper off-peak (vazio), mostly at night and weekends", "Tri-horário: peak, shoulder and off-peak"]}}

### Typical off-peak window
In the daily cycle, off-peak typically runs overnight (for example 22:00–08:00). Exact times depend on the cycle and season.
### Dynamic tariffs
Some suppliers offer prices that follow the wholesale market hour by hour (indexed tariffs), which can be very cheap at midday on sunny days or at night.

# Resistive heating
Resistive heaters turn electricity into heat as current flows through a resistance. Every kilowatt-hour of electricity becomes a kilowatt-hour of heat: 100% efficient, but not the cheapest way to heat.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Current through element", "Resistance", "Electrical energy becomes heat", "Room, water or food warms"]}
### Where it's used
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Kettles", "Ovens", "Water heaters", "Portable heaters", "Hair dryers", "Irons"]}
### 100% efficient but expensive
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Resistive heater", "items": ["1 kWh in, 1 kWh heat"]}, "r": {"t": "Heat pump", "items": ["1 kWh in, 3–4 kWh heat"]}}
A heater converts all its electricity to heat, but a heat pump moves extra heat from outside, delivering several times more heat for the same electricity.
### When resistive makes sense
• Small, occasional needs: a bathroom heater for a few minutes
• Very well-insulated homes with tiny heating demand
• Kettles and cooking, where heat is needed fast and locally
> Converting electricity to heat is easy; moving heat is smarter.

# Heat pumps: 3 to 4 times better
A heat pump uses electricity to move heat from outside into the home, like a fridge working in reverse. It delivers far more heat than the electricity it uses.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "heat delivered per kWh of electricity, kWh", "items": [{"l": "Resistive heater", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Heat pump, cold day", "v": 2.5}, {"l": "Heat pump, average", "v": 3.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Heat pump, mild day", "v": 4.5}]}
### How it works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Refrigerant absorbs heat from outside air", "Compressor raises its temperature", "Releases heat indoors", "Expands and cools", "Repeats"]}
### COP and SCOP
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "COP", "b": "efficiency at one moment"}, {"a": "SCOP", "b": "average over a heating season"}, {"a": "Higher", "b": "better"}, {"a": "Typical SCOP", "b": "3–4 in mild climates"}]}
### Portugal's advantage
Mild winters make heat pumps especially efficient in Portugal: the warmer the outside air, the less work the compressor must do.
> A heat pump is a heat mover, not a heat maker.

# Air-to-air vs air-to-water
Heat pumps come in two main types, depending on how they deliver heat indoors.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Air-to-air", "items": ["split air conditioners", "heat and cool air", "quick", "cheaper"]}, "r": {"t": "Air-to-water", "items": ["heat water for radiators, underfloor heating, hot taps"]}}

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Flat without radiators", "b": "air-to-air splits"}, {"a": "House with radiators", "b": "air-to-water"}, {"a": "Underfloor heating", "b": "air-to-water, very efficient"}, {"a": "Hot water only", "b": "heat pump water heater"}]}

Underfloor systems need water at only 30–40 °C, which lets heat pumps work at their most efficient.

# Induction vs ceramic vs gas
Cooking methods differ greatly in how much energy reaches the food.

### Why induction wins
Induction heats the pan base directly, wasting less heat into the air. Ceramic hobs heat the glass first, then the pan; gas flames lose much heat around the pan.
### Speed and control
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Induction", "items": ["very fast", "instant control", "cool surface"]}, "r": {"t": "Gas", "items": ["instant control", "open flame", "heats the kitchen"]}}

Gas cooking releases nitrogen dioxide and other pollutants indoors. Good ventilation or a cooker hood vented outside reduces exposure.
### Switching cost

> Induction puts the heat where the food is.

# Static charge
Static electricity is charge that builds up on a surface and stays there. Rubbing materials together transfers electrons from one to the other.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rub two materials", "Electrons move from one to the other", "One negative, one positive", "Charge stays until discharged"]}
### The triboelectric series
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tend to gain positive charge", "items": ["hair", "wool", "glass", "nylon"]}, "r": {"t": "Tend to gain negative charge", "items": ["rubber", "PVC", "polyester", "Teflon"]}}
### Everyday examples
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Balloon on hair", "Clothes in a dryer", "Plastic film clinging", "Sparks from a jumper"]}
### Why dry weather
Humid air helps charge leak away. In dry winter air or air-conditioned buildings, charge builds up more easily.
> Static is electricity standing still, waiting to jump.

# Why you get shocks
Walking across carpet in dry weather can charge your body to thousands of volts. Touching metal discharges it in a quick spark.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "3,000 V", "l": "roughly where you start to feel a spark"}, {"v": "Up to 35,000 V", "l": "body charge walking on carpet in very dry air"}, {"v": "Microseconds", "l": "duration of discharge"}]}
### Why it doesn't hurt you
The charge is tiny and flows for microseconds, so the energy is very small, even though the voltage is high.
### Reducing shocks
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Humidify dry rooms", "Wear natural fibres", "Touch metal with a key first", "Anti-static mats"]}
### Cars
Sliding across a car seat builds charge. Touching the metal door frame as you get out discharges you gradually.
> High voltage, tiny energy: that's why static stings but doesn't harm.

# ESD and electronics
Electrostatic discharge (ESD) can damage electronic components with voltages too small for you to feel.

ESD can cause immediate failure or latent damage that shortens a component's life, causing mysterious failures later.

# Safe in a car
A metal car is a good place to shelter from lightning, thanks to the Faraday cage effect, not the rubber tyres.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Rubber tyres insulate the car", "r": "The metal body carries current around the occupants"}]}
### How it works
Current flows over the outer metal surface and jumps to the ground. The inside stays largely protected.
### Exceptions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Convertibles", "Fibreglass or plastic bodies", "Bicycles and motorbikes", "Open vehicles"]}
### Planes too
Aircraft are struck by lightning roughly once a year on average. Their metal or conductive skins carry the current around the cabin.
> Tyres are a few centimetres; lightning has crossed kilometres of air.

# Outdoor safety
Lightning can strike several kilometres from the storm. If you hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck.

### If caught outside
• Avoid open fields, ridges and water
• Don't shelter under lone trees
• Keep away from metal fences and poles

### First aid

> When thunder roars, go indoors.

# Three dangers
Electricity can harm people and property in three main ways.

### Arc flash
An arc flash is an explosive release of energy when current jumps through air. It can reach thousands of degrees and cause severe burns even without contact.
### Fire
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Overloaded circuits", "Loose connections", "Damaged cables", "Faulty appliances", "Misused extensions"]}
### Protection
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Shock", "b": "RCDs, insulation, earthing"}, {"a": "Fire", "b": "correct cables, breakers, inspections"}, {"a": "Burns", "b": "isolation and protective equipment"}]}

# Current path matters
The path current takes through the body determines how dangerous it is. Paths across the chest risk the heart.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Most dangerous", "items": ["hand to hand", "hand to foot", "across the chest"]}, "r": {"t": "Less dangerous", "items": ["within one hand or limb"]}}

### Time
Even moderate currents become dangerous when they last longer. Fast disconnection by RCDs is crucial.
> Wet hands and a path across the chest are the deadliest combination.

# Loose connections
Loose connections are a leading cause of electrical fires. A small contact area has higher resistance, which heats up and makes things worse.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Loose terminal", "Higher resistance", "Heat", "Oxidation", "Even higher resistance", "More heat"]}
### Why it's dangerous
The heat is concentrated at one point, often hidden in a socket box or switch. A breaker doesn't trip, because current is normal.
### Warning signs
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Warm socket", "Discoloured plastic", "Flickering lights", "Crackling", "Burning smell"]}

• Correct tightening torque for terminals
• Ferrules on flexible conductors
• Periodic inspection with thermal cameras in larger installations
> Fires often start where cables connect, not in the cables themselves.

# Warning signs

# If someone is being shocked
Helping a person being electrocuted can make you the next victim. Break the circuit first.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Don't touch them", "Switch off power", "Or separate with dry non-conductive object", "Call 112", "Start CPR if needed"]}

# Overhead lines
Overhead power lines carry dangerous voltages, and high voltage can arc across air without direct contact.

# Why a home battery?
A home battery stores electricity for later. With solar panels, it saves midday surplus for the evening; with time-of-use tariffs, it shifts cheap energy to expensive hours.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Midday solar surplus", "Battery charges", "Evening demand", "Battery supplies home", "Less grid import"]}

Interest in home batteries with backup capability rose in Portugal and Spain after the April 2025 blackout.

# Sizing a battery
Size a battery to cover your evening and night consumption, not your whole day. Bigger isn't always better.
