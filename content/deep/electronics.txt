# Reading a resistor
Through-hole resistors are marked with coloured bands instead of printed numbers. Once you know the code, you can read any value in seconds.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Black", "b": "0"}, {"a": "Brown", "b": "1"}, {"a": "Red", "b": "2"}, {"a": "Orange", "b": "3"}, {"a": "Yellow", "b": "4"}, {"a": "Green", "b": "5"}]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Blue", "b": "6"}, {"a": "Violet", "b": "7"}, {"a": "Grey", "b": "8"}, {"a": "White", "b": "9"}, {"a": "Gold", "b": "×0.1 or ±5%"}, {"a": "Silver", "b": "×0.01 or ±10%"}]}
### Reading four bands
ex: Yellow, violet, red, gold | 4, 7, × 100 | = 4,700 Ω (4.7 kΩ) ±5%
ex: Brown, black, orange, gold | 1, 0, × 1,000 | = 10 kΩ ±5%
### Five bands
Precision resistors use three digit bands, a multiplier and a tolerance band (often brown, ±1%).
### Which end first?
The tolerance band (gold or silver) is usually set slightly apart and goes on the right. If in doubt, measure with a multimeter.
### Surface-mount codes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "103", "b": "10 × 10³ = 10 kΩ"}, {"a": "472", "b": "4.7 kΩ"}, {"a": "4R7", "b": "4.7 Ω"}, {"a": "01C (EIA-96)", "b": "10 kΩ, 1%"}]}
> Two digits, a multiplier and a tolerance: that's the whole code.

# Every LED needs a resistor
An LED has an almost fixed forward voltage. Above it, current rises steeply, so without a limit it burns out. A series resistor sets the current.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "R = (V supply − V forward) ÷ I", "items": [{"s": "V supply", "m": "e.g. 3.3 or 5 V"}, {"s": "V forward", "m": "LED's voltage"}, {"s": "I", "m": "desired current"}]}
### Typical forward voltages
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. forward voltage, V", "items": [{"l": "Red", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Yellow", "v": 2.1}, {"l": "Green", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Blue", "v": 3.1}, {"l": "White", "v": 3.1, "hi": true}]}
### Worked examples
ex: Red LED on 5 V at 10 mA | (5 − 2) ÷ 0.01 | = 300 Ω, use 330 Ω
ex: White LED on 3.3 V at 5 mA | (3.3 − 3.0) ÷ 0.005 | = 60 Ω, use 68 Ω
### How much current?
Modern LEDs are bright at 2–5 mA. Using less current saves power and keeps microcontroller pins within limits.
!! Bigger resistor means dimmer and safer => yes, but too big and a white LED on 3.3 V may barely light; check the forward voltage

# Try it: the voltage divider
Two resistors in series divide a voltage in proportion to their values. It's one of the most common circuits in electronics.

# Standard resistor values
Resistors come in standard series of preferred values, spaced roughly evenly on a logarithmic scale, so each value covers its tolerance range.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["E12: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27", "E12: 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82", "E24: twice as many values, 5%", "E96: 1% precision"]}
### Why these numbers?
Each value is about 21% higher than the previous one in the E12 series, matching ±10% tolerance so the ranges overlap.
### Picking a value
ex: Calculation gives 290 Ω | Nearest E12 values: 270 or 330 Ω | Choose 330 Ω for an LED (slightly less current)
### Combining
If an exact value is needed, combine two standard resistors in series or parallel, or use E96 precision parts.
> Calculate, then round to the nearest standard value.

# Resistor power rating
Resistors convert electrical power into heat. Each has a maximum power rating beyond which it overheats.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "P = I² × R = V² ÷ R", "items": [{"s": "P", "m": "power in watts"}, {"s": "I", "m": "current"}, {"s": "R", "m": "resistance"}]}
### Common ratings
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Through-hole", "b": "¼ W (most common), ½ W, 1 W and up"}, {"a": "SMD 0603", "b": "about 0.1 W"}, {"a": "SMD 1206", "b": "about 0.25 W"}, {"a": "Power resistors", "b": "5–50 W with heatsinks"}]}
### Example
ex: 100 Ω resistor with 50 mA | P = 0.05² × 100 | = 0.25 W: use at least ½ W
### Safety margin
Choose a rating at least double the calculated power. A resistor at its full rating can get hot enough to burn fingers or discolour the board.
> Check the watts, not just the ohms.

# Series and parallel resistors
Combining resistors creates new values. Series adds them; parallel reduces the total.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Series", "items": ["R = R1 + R2", "total larger"]}, "r": {"t": "Parallel", "items": ["1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2", "total smaller than smallest"]}}
### Quick rules
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Two equal in parallel", "b": "half the value"}, {"a": "Three equal in parallel", "b": "a third"}, {"a": "Big and small in parallel", "b": "close to the small one"}]}
### Examples
ex: 10 kΩ + 4.7 kΩ in series | = 14.7 kΩ
ex: 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ | = 5 kΩ
ex: 1 kΩ ∥ 1 MΩ | ≈ 999 Ω
### Product over sum
For two resistors in parallel: R = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2).
> Series adds up; parallel always gives less than the smallest.

# RC time constant
When a resistor charges a capacitor, the voltage rises quickly at first, then slows as the capacitor fills. The time constant τ = R × C describes the pace.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "τ = R × C", "items": [{"s": "τ", "m": "seconds"}, {"s": "R", "m": "ohms"}, {"s": "C", "m": "farads"}]}
### Charging steps
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "charge reached, %", "items": [{"l": "1 τ", "v": 63.0}, {"l": "2 τ", "v": 86.0}, {"l": "3 τ", "v": 95.0}, {"l": "5 τ", "v": 99.0, "hi": true}]}
### Example
ex: 10 kΩ and 100 µF | τ = 10,000 × 0.0001 | = 1 second; full in about 5 s
### Uses
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Delays and timers", "Debouncing buttons", "Low-pass filters", "Power-on reset circuits"]}
### Discharge too
Discharging follows the same curve in reverse: after one time constant, 37% of the voltage remains.
> One τ: 63%; five τ: done.

# Digital vs. analog
Signals are either digital (discrete states, usually 0 and 1) or analogue (continuously variable). Microcontrollers are digital but have tools to handle analogue.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Digital", "items": ["0 or 1", "noise tolerant", "easy to process"]}, "r": {"t": "Analogue", "items": ["any value in a range", "real-world quantities"]}}
### Converting
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "ADC", "b": "analogue to digital: read sensors"}, {"a": "DAC", "b": "digital to analogue: true analogue output"}, {"a": "PWM", "b": "fake analogue by fast switching"}]}
### Logic levels
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 3.3, "unit": "V", "items": [{"l": "Low", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "Undefined", "v": 1.6}, {"l": "High", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}]}
A voltage near the middle is ambiguous: avoid signals that sit there.
### Noise immunity
Digital signals survive noise well: a little noise on a 3.3 V "1" is still clearly a 1. That's why digital systems are so reliable.
> The world is analogue; computers are digital; converters bridge the gap.

# PWM: dimming by blinking
Pulse-width modulation switches a pin fully on and off rapidly. The load responds to the average, so changing the on-time controls brightness or speed.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Average V = duty cycle × V supply", "items": [{"s": "Duty cycle", "m": "fraction of time on"}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average output on 12 V, V", "items": [{"l": "25%", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "50%", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "75%", "v": 9.0}, {"l": "100%", "v": 12.0, "hi": true}]}
### Frequency choices
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "LEDs", "b": "above about 1 kHz to avoid visible flicker"}, {"a": "Motors", "b": "about 20 kHz to be inaudible"}, {"a": "Servos", "b": "50 Hz with 1–2 ms pulses"}]}
### Why efficient
A switch fully on has little voltage drop, and fully off has no current, so little power is wasted either way.
### On the ESP32
The LEDC peripheral provides many PWM channels with adjustable frequency and resolution, often used for LED dimming and motors.
> PWM fakes an analogue level by switching faster than the load can notice.

# Pull-up resistors
An unconnected input pin "floats", picking up noise and reading random values. A pull-up or pull-down resistor gives it a defined default state.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pull-up", "items": ["pin to 3.3 V", "reads 1", "button pulls to 0"]}, "r": {"t": "Pull-down", "items": ["pin to ground", "reads 0", "button pulls to 1"]}}
### Typical values
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "External", "b": "4.7–10 kΩ"}, {"a": "ESP32 internal", "b": "roughly 45 kΩ"}, {"a": "I²C buses", "b": "2.2–10 kΩ"}]}
### Why not a wire?
A direct wire to 3.3 V would short circuit when the button connects the pin to ground. The resistor limits that current to a fraction of a milliamp.
### In code
ex: pinMode(4, INPUT_PULLUP) | Button between pin 4 and ground | Pressed reads LOW
> Never leave an input floating.

Using delay() freezes the program. Checking elapsed time with millis() lets the program do several things at once.

# A one-way valve
A diode lets current flow in one direction (anode to cathode) and blocks it in the other. It's the electronic equivalent of a check valve.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Forward bias", "items": ["anode more positive", "conducts"]}, "r": {"t": "Reverse bias", "items": ["cathode more positive", "blocks"]}}

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Band on body", "b": "cathode"}, {"a": "Symbol bar", "b": "cathode"}, {"a": "Arrow", "b": "direction of conventional current"}]}
### Inside
A diode is a junction between p-type and n-type semiconductor. The junction creates a barrier that current can cross easily only one way.
### Common diodes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["1N4148: small signal", "1N4007: rectifier, 1 A", "1N5819: Schottky, 1 A", "Zener diodes", "LEDs"]}
> The band marks the end where current exits.

# Forward voltage
A conducting diode drops a roughly fixed voltage, its forward voltage (Vf). The type determines how much.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical forward voltage, V", "items": [{"l": "Schottky", "v": 0.3}, {"l": "Silicon", "v": 0.7}, {"l": "Red LED", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Blue/white LED", "v": 3.1, "hi": true}]}
### Why it matters
ex: Silicon diode with 2 A | 0.7 V × 2 A | = 1.4 W of heat
ex: Schottky with 2 A | 0.3 V × 2 A | = 0.6 W
### Schottky trade-offs
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Schottky", "items": ["low drop", "fast", "higher leakage", "lower voltage ratings"]}, "r": {"t": "Silicon", "items": ["higher drop", "low leakage", "high voltage ratings"]}}
### Temperature
Forward voltage falls about 2 mV for each °C rise, which is used in simple temperature sensors.
> Every diode costs a little voltage; Schottkys cost less.

# Flyback diodes
Coils such as relays, solenoids and motors store energy in their magnetic field. When switched off, they produce a voltage spike that can destroy the transistor driving them.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Relay coil driven with no diode", "r": "Diode across coil, cathode to positive"}]}
### What happens
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Transistor switches off", "Coil current must continue", "Voltage spikes to tens or hundreds of volts", "Without diode: transistor damaged", "With diode: current circulates safely"]}
### Choosing the diode
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["1N4007 for relays", "Fast or Schottky diodes for PWM motors", "Rated for coil current"]}
### Side effect
The diode slows the relay's release slightly. For fast release, a Zener in series with the diode is sometimes used.
> Every switched coil needs a flyback diode.

### Common values
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["3.3 V", "5.1 V", "9.1 V", "12 V", "15 V"]}

# Why you need one
A microcontroller pin can supply only a few milliamps. Motors, relays and LED strips need far more. A transistor lets a small current or voltage control a much larger one.

# BJT: current controls current
In a bipolar junction transistor (BJT), a small base current controls a larger collector current.

# MOSFET: voltage controls current
A MOSFET's gate is insulated, so it's controlled by voltage with almost no steady gate current. When fully on, it behaves like a very small resistor.

# Low-side switching
The simplest MOSFET circuit places an N-channel MOSFET between the load and ground. The microcontroller drives the gate.

# Gate resistor and pull-down
Two resistors make a MOSFET switch reliable: one limits gate current, the other keeps it off when the pin is floating.

== tr-opamps

# What an op-amp is
An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a chip that amplifies the difference between its two inputs by a huge factor, often 100,000 or more.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "V out = A × (V+ − V−)", "items": [{"s": "A", "m": "open-loop gain, very large"}, {"s": "V+", "m": "non-inverting input"}, {"s": "V−", "m": "inverting input"}]}
### Pins
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "V+ input", "b": "non-inverting"}, {"a": "V− input", "b": "inverting"}, {"a": "Output", "b": "amplified difference"}, {"a": "Power", "b": "positive and negative or ground rails"}]}
### The name
The term "operational amplifier" comes from 1940s analogue computers, where they performed mathematical operations like addition, integration and differentiation.
### Common parts
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["LM358", "TL072", "MCP6002", "OPA2333", "μA741"]}
> An op-amp is a difference amplifier with enormous gain.

# Feedback tames it
Raw open-loop gain is too large and unpredictable to use directly. Negative feedback, connecting the output back to the inverting input, sets a precise gain.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Output too high", "Feedback raises V−", "Difference shrinks", "Output corrected", "Stable balance"]}
### Why it works
The gain then depends only on resistor values, not on the op-amp's variable internal gain. Resistors are precise and stable.
### Benefits
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Precise gain", "Lower distortion", "Wider bandwidth", "Stable operation"]}
### Positive feedback
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Negative feedback", "items": ["stable amplifiers"]}, "r": {"t": "Positive feedback", "items": ["oscillators and Schmitt triggers"]}}
> Feedback trades excess gain for precision.

# Non-inverting amplifier
The non-inverting amplifier boosts a signal without inverting it. The input goes to the + input; two resistors set the gain.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Gain = 1 + Rf ÷ Rg", "items": [{"s": "Rf", "m": "feedback resistor"}, {"s": "Rg", "m": "resistor to ground"}]}
### Example
ex: Sensor gives 0–300 mV, ADC wants 0–3 V | Gain 10 | Rf = 9 kΩ, Rg = 1 kΩ
### Inverting amplifier
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Non-inverting", "items": ["gain ≥ 1", "high input impedance"]}, "r": {"t": "Inverting", "items": ["gain = −Rf ÷ Rin", "output flipped"]}}
### High input impedance
The non-inverting input draws almost no current, so it doesn't load delicate sensors.
> Two resistors set the gain precisely.

# The voltage follower
Connect the output directly to the inverting input and the gain is exactly 1. The output copies the input, but with much more driving ability.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Weak source", "Op-amp follower", "Same voltage", "Strong output for loads"]}
### Why it's useful
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Buffer voltage dividers", "Isolate sensors from loads", "Drive ADC inputs", "Reference voltages"]}
### Example
A voltage divider with large resistors can't drive a load without its voltage dropping. A follower after it holds the voltage steady.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without buffer", "items": ["output sags with load"]}, "r": {"t": "With buffer", "items": ["output steady"]}}
> A gain of 1 can be the most useful gain of all.

# Comparators
Without feedback, an op-amp acts as a comparator: its output swings fully high or low depending on which input is larger.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sensor voltage", "Compared with reference", "Higher: output high", "Lower: output low", "Switch something"]}
### Uses
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Thermostats", "Light-activated switches", "Battery low alarms", "Zero-crossing detection"]}
### Hysteresis
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plain comparator", "items": ["chatters near threshold"]}, "r": {"t": "With hysteresis (Schmitt trigger)", "items": ["two thresholds", "clean switching"]}}
A little positive feedback creates two thresholds, preventing rapid on-off chatter.
### Dedicated comparators
Chips like the LM393 are designed for comparison: faster, with open-collector outputs suited to logic.
> A comparator answers one question: which is bigger?

# Two golden rules
With negative feedback, op-amp circuits can be analysed with two simple rules, popularised by Horowitz and Hill's classic book "The Art of Electronics".
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No current flows into the inputs", "The output makes both inputs equal"]}
### Applying them
ex: Non-inverting amp, input 0.2 V, Rf 9 kΩ, Rg 1 kΩ | V− must equal 0.2 V | Output = 0.2 × 10 = 2 V
### When they fail
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Output at supply rail", "b": "can't make inputs equal"}, {"a": "No feedback", "b": "rules don't apply"}, {"a": "Very high frequencies", "b": "gain runs out"}]}
### Why they work
The enormous open-loop gain means only a microscopic difference between inputs is needed for any realistic output.
> Two rules solve most op-amp circuits.

# Why regulate?
Electronic chips need a stable supply voltage. Batteries sag as they discharge, adapters vary and loads change suddenly. A regulator holds the output steady.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Varying input", "Regulator", "Steady output", "Happy chips"]}
### How much inputs vary
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Li-ion cell", "b": "4.2 V full to about 3.0 V empty"}, {"a": "USB 2.0", "b": "4.75–5.25 V by specification"}, {"a": "Car \"12 V\"", "b": "11–14.5 V, with spikes"}, {"a": "Solar panel", "b": "varies with light"}]}
### What chips tolerate
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["ESP32: about 3.0–3.6 V", "Most 3.3 V logic: ±10%", "Sensors: often tight limits", "Analogue circuits: sensitive to noise"]}
### Two families
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Linear regulators", "items": ["simple", "quiet", "waste the voltage difference as heat"]}, "r": {"t": "Switching regulators", "items": ["efficient", "small", "some electrical noise"]}}
> Clean, stable power is the foundation of reliable electronics.

# Linear regulators
A linear regulator acts like a self-adjusting resistor, dropping the extra voltage and converting it to heat.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Power wasted = (V in − V out) × I", "items": [{"s": "V in", "m": "input"}, {"s": "V out", "m": "output"}, {"s": "I", "m": "load current"}]}
### Efficiency
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Efficiency ≈ V out ÷ V in", "items": [{"s": "5 V → 3.3 V", "m": "66%"}, {"s": "12 V → 3.3 V", "m": "27.5%"}]}
### Example
ex: 12 V to 5 V at 0.5 A with LM7805 | (12 − 5) × 0.5 | = 3.5 W of heat: needs a heatsink
### Common parts
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "LM7805", "b": "5 V, needs about 2 V headroom"}, {"a": "AMS1117-3.3", "b": "common on ESP32 boards, about 1 V headroom"}, {"a": "LM317", "b": "adjustable"}]}
### Advantages
Linear regulators are cheap, simple and produce very little electrical noise, ideal for small currents and sensitive circuits.
> Linear regulators turn excess voltage into heat.

# LDO regulators
A low-dropout (LDO) regulator keeps regulating even when the input is only slightly above the output, often by just 0.1–0.3 V.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Standard linear", "items": ["needs 1–2 V headroom"]}, "r": {"t": "LDO", "items": ["needs 0.1–0.3 V headroom"]}}
### Why it matters for batteries
ex: Li-ion cell 3.7 V nominal, 3.4 V near empty | Regulator to 3.3 V | Only an LDO can keep working until the battery is nearly flat
### Quiescent current
The current an LDO uses itself matters for battery projects. Some draw milliamps; good ones draw a few microamps.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "AMS1117", "b": "about 5 mA quiescent"}, {"a": "MCP1700", "b": "about 1.6 µA"}, {"a": "HT7333", "b": "a few µA"}]}
### Limits
LDOs are still linear: efficiency is low when the input is much higher than the output.
> For battery projects, choose an LDO with tiny quiescent current.

# Buck converters
A buck converter steps voltage down efficiently by switching rapidly and storing energy in an inductor.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Switch on: inductor charges", "Switch off: inductor releases", "Capacitor smooths", "Lower voltage out"]}
### Efficiency
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "efficiency 24 V to 3.3 V at 1 A, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Linear", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "Buck converter", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}]}
### Duty cycle
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "V out ≈ duty cycle × V in", "items": [{"s": "Duty cycle", "m": "fraction of time the switch is on"}]}
### Current out vs in
Because power is conserved (minus losses), a buck converter delivers more current at the output than it draws from the input.
ex: 24 V in, 3.3 V out at 2 A (6.6 W) | Input ≈ 6.6 ÷ 0.9 ÷ 24 | ≈ 0.31 A
### Modules
Cheap buck modules (such as those based on MP1584 or LM2596) make it easy to power 3.3 V or 5 V circuits from 12–24 V supplies.
> Buck converters step down voltage with little heat.

# Boost converters
A boost converter steps voltage up, for example from a 3.7 V battery to 5 V for USB.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "I in ≈ I out × V out ÷ (V in × efficiency)", "items": [{"s": "Higher output voltage", "m": "more input current"}]}
### Example
ex: 5 V at 1 A from a 3.7 V battery, 90% efficient | I in ≈ 1 × 5 ÷ (3.7 × 0.9) | ≈ 1.5 A from the battery
### Uses
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Power banks", "LED drivers", "Single AA-powered gadgets", "Solar chargers"]}
### Buck-boost
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Buck", "b": "output lower than input"}, {"a": "Boost", "b": "output higher than input"}, {"a": "Buck-boost", "b": "either, as battery voltage crosses the output"}]}
### Limits
Boosting large ratios draws heavy input currents and reduces efficiency.
> Boosting voltage costs extra current from the source.

# Efficiency compared
For a large voltage drop, switching regulators win easily. For small drops and noise-sensitive circuits, an LDO can be the better choice.

# Brown-outs
A brown-out is a dip in supply voltage below what a chip needs. It can cause resets, crashes or corrupted data.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "ESP32 resets when Wi-Fi connects", "r": "Stronger supply and a large capacitor near the board"}]}
### Why ESP32s brown out
Wi-Fi transmission causes current spikes of several hundred milliamps. A weak USB cable, thin wires or a small regulator can't keep up, so the voltage dips.
### Fixes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Short, thick power wires", "Capacitor 470–1,000 µF near the board", "Better regulator", "Quality USB cable"]}

The ESP32 has a built-in brown-out detector that resets the chip safely when voltage falls too low, rather than letting it misbehave.
> Random resets are usually power problems, not code problems.

# Ultrasonic quirks

### Temperature compensation
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Speed ≈ 331.3 + 0.606 × T (m/s)", "items": [{"s": "T", "m": "air temperature in °C"}]}

# Why buses?
Microcontrollers talk to sensors, displays and memory through serial buses that send bits one after another over a few wires.

Standard buses let parts from different manufacturers work together, so a BME280 from one company talks to an ESP32 from another.

# UART
UART (universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter) uses one line to send and one to receive, with no shared clock.

# SPI
SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) uses separate lines for clock, data in, data out and a chip-select for each device. It's fast and simple.

# The trade-off triangle
Every wireless technology balances range, data rate and power consumption. Improving one usually costs another.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "High data rate", "items": ["Wi-Fi", "short range or high power"]}, "r": {"t": "Long range, low power", "items": ["LoRa", "tiny data rates"]}}
### The triangle
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Wi-Fi", "b": "fast, medium range, power-hungry"}, {"a": "BLE", "b": "slow, short range, very low power"}, {"a": "LoRa", "b": "very slow, long range, low power"}, {"a": "Cellular", "b": "medium speed, long range, moderate power plus fees"}]}
### Physics
Sending more bits per second requires more signal relative to noise. At long distances, signals are weak, so only low data rates get through reliably.
> Pick two: range, speed or low power.

# Wi-Fi
Wi-Fi offers high data rates and direct internet access, but uses significant power, best for mains-powered devices.

### Battery devices
Wi-Fi on batteries is possible with deep sleep: wake, connect, send, sleep. Connecting takes time and energy, so readings every few minutes work better than every second.

# Average current is what counts
Battery life depends on average current, not peak current. Devices that sleep most of the time can last for years.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Battery life (h) = capacity (mAh) ÷ average current (mA)", "items": [{"s": "Capacity", "m": "from datasheet"}, {"s": "Average", "m": "weighted by time"}]}
### Example
ex: 2,000 mAh battery, average 0.1 mA | 2,000 ÷ 0.1 | = 20,000 h ≈ 2.3 years
### The duty cycle
A device awake 1 second every 10 minutes sleeps 99.8% of the time. If it sleeps poorly, sleep current dominates the battery drain.
### Real-world derating
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Self-discharge", "Temperature", "Battery ageing", "Regulator efficiency"]}
Plan for 70–80% of the theoretical life.
> Sleep current usually decides battery life.

# Average current
The average current is each state's current weighted by the fraction of time spent in it.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "I avg = Σ (I state × t state) ÷ t total", "items": [{"s": "I state", "m": "current in each mode"}, {"s": "t state", "m": "time in each mode"}]}
### Worked example
ex: Awake 2 s at 120 mA, asleep 598 s at 0.02 mA | (240 + 12) ÷ 600 | ≈ 0.42 mA average
### Where energy goes
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "charge per cycle, mA·s", "items": [{"l": "Awake", "v": 240.0}, {"l": "Asleep", "v": 12.0, "hi": true}]}
### Improving it
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Wake less often", "Shorten awake time", "Lower sleep current", "Lower active current"]}
> Weight each state by how long it lasts.

# Decoupling capacitors
Every chip needs a small capacitor right next to its power pins. When the chip suddenly draws current, the capacitor supplies it instantly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chip switches", "Sudden current demand", "Nearby capacitor delivers", "Supply rail stays stable"]}
### Typical values
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Standard", "b": "100 nF ceramic per power pin"}, {"a": "Added", "b": "1–10 µF nearby"}, {"a": "Radios and processors", "b": "several values for different frequencies"}]}
### Placement
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Capacitor at the board edge", "r": "As close as possible to the power pin"}]}
Long traces add inductance, which blocks the fast currents the capacitor is meant to supply.
### Symptoms without decoupling
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Random resets", "Communication errors", "Noisy ADC readings", "Radio problems"]}
> One 100 nF capacitor per chip, as close as possible.

# Bulk capacitance
Besides small decoupling capacitors, boards need larger bulk capacitors where power enters and near heavy loads.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Decoupling", "items": ["100 nF", "fast transients", "at each chip"]}, "r": {"t": "Bulk", "items": ["10–1,000 µF", "slower dips", "at power entry and motors"]}}
### Where to put bulk capacitors
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Power input", "Near motor drivers", "Near radios", "Near LED strip connections"]}
### ESP32 example
ex: Wi-Fi transmit bursts | Several hundred mA for milliseconds | 100–470 µF near the module prevents brownouts
### LED strips
Adding a large capacitor (around 1,000 µF) across the supply at the start of an LED strip protects the first LEDs from inrush spikes.
> Bulk capacitors handle the big gulps; decoupling handles the sips.

# Ground loops
A ground loop occurs when two parts of a system connect to ground at different points with slightly different voltages. Current flows through the ground wiring, adding noise.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Audio hum from two grounded devices", "r": "Single ground reference or isolation"}]}
### Causes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Multiple earth connections", "Long ground cables", "Shielded cables grounded at both ends", "Different power outlets"]}
### Symptoms
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Audio", "b": "50 Hz hum"}, {"a": "Sensors", "b": "wandering readings"}, {"a": "Communication", "b": "errors"}, {"a": "Video", "b": "rolling bars"}]}
### Fixes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ground at one point", "Isolated interfaces", "Differential signals", "Ground loop isolators"]}
> Ground should be one reference, not many competing ones.

# Star grounding
Star grounding brings ground connections from different circuit sections back to one point, so noisy currents don't flow through sensitive ground paths.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ground point", "items": ["Motor ground: separate wire", "Power ground: separate wire", "Digital ground: separate wire", "Analogue sensor ground: separate wire"]}
### Why it helps
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Daisy-chained ground", "items": ["motor currents pass through sensor ground", "noise"]}, "r": {"t": "Star ground", "items": ["each returns separately", "sensor ground quiet"]}}
### On PCBs
Modern PCBs often use a solid ground plane instead, which gives low impedance everywhere. Careful placement keeps noisy and sensitive circuits apart.
### For hobby builds
Run separate ground wires from the motor driver and the sensors back to the power supply's ground terminal.
> Keep noisy currents out of quiet grounds.

# Long sensor cables
Long wires act as antennas, picking up interference from motors, mains wiring and radio. Signals degrade with distance.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Twisted pairs", "Shielded cable", "Lower impedance", "Differential signals", "Digital sensors"]}
### Twisted pairs
Twisting signal and ground together means interference affects both equally, and much of it cancels out.
### Better options for distance
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Analogue voltage", "b": "poor over long distances"}, {"a": "4–20 mA current loop", "b": "industrial standard, very robust"}, {"a": "RS-485", "b": "digital, hundreds of metres"}, {"a": "Wireless", "b": "no cable at all"}]}
### Protection
Long outdoor cables can also pick up surges from nearby lightning. Add TVS diodes and series resistors at the microcontroller.
> Long cables need robust signals.

# Pull-ups and floating inputs
An unconnected input floats, picking up noise and reading random values. It can even cause extra current draw and oscillation inside the chip.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Unused input left unconnected", "r": "Pull-up, pull-down or set as output"}]}
### Effects of floating inputs
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Random readings", "Phantom button presses", "Increased power consumption", "Unpredictable wake-ups"]}
### Long button wires
Buttons on long cables need stronger pull-ups (lower resistance, such as 4.7 kΩ) and maybe a small capacitor, to resist interference.
### Unused pins
In low-power designs, configure unused pins with pull-ups or as outputs to prevent leakage currents.
> Every input needs a defined level.

# Switching noise

# LED brightness and current
LED brightness depends on current, not voltage. LEDs must be driven with controlled current.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Voltage source", "Resistor or constant-current driver", "Controlled current", "Predictable brightness"]}
### Why not voltage
A tiny change in voltage near an LED's forward voltage causes a big change in current. Without control, current runs away and the LED fails.
### Driver options
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Resistor", "b": "simple, fine for indicators"}, {"a": "Constant-current driver", "b": "efficient, for power LEDs"}, {"a": "PWM", "b": "dimming without colour shift"}]}
### Brightness perception
Our eyes perceive brightness logarithmically. Gamma correction makes PWM dimming look smooth to the eye.
> Control the current, and the LED behaves.

@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Power at one end", "Voltage drops along strip", "Far end dimmer and yellowish", "Inject power every 1–2 m"]}

# Level shifting the data
WS2812B strips running at 5 V expect a data "high" of at least about 3.5 V. An ESP32's 3.3 V output may be borderline, causing flicker or wrong colours.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "ESP32 data pin straight to 5 V strip, flickering", "r": "Level shifter such as 74AHCT125"}]}
### Options
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["74AHCT125 or 74HCT245 buffer", "Sacrificial first LED at lower voltage", "Short data wire", "Series resistor"]}
### Series resistor
A resistor of about 300–500 Ω at the start of the data line reduces reflections on longer data wires.
### Why it sometimes works anyway
Many strips accept 3.3 V signals at room temperature with short wires, which is why the problem appears inconsistently.
> Flickering strips usually need a level shifter.
