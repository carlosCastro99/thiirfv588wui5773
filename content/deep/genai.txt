# From recognising to creating
Discriminative models learn to tell things apart: they map an input to a label or number. Generative models learn what the data itself looks like, so they can produce new examples that resemble it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Discriminative", "items": ["\"is this a cat?\"", "outputs a label or score"]}, "r": {"t": "Generative", "items": ["\"draw a cat\"", "outputs new content"]}}
### Generative isn't new
Statistical text generators, Markov chains and early neural generators existed for decades. What changed around 2020–2022 was scale and quality: outputs became good enough to be genuinely useful.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1948", "l": "Shannon's word-chain text"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "GANs generate faces"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "Transformer"}, {"y": "2020", "l": "GPT-3"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "ChatGPT and image generators"}]}
### What "new" means
Generated content is novel in combination, not copied wholesale (though models can sometimes reproduce memorised passages). It reflects patterns in training data, including its styles and biases.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Text", "b": "emails, reports, code"}, {"a": "Images", "b": "illustrations, product mock-ups"}, {"a": "Audio", "b": "voices, music"}, {"a": "Structured data", "b": "tables, JSON from documents"}]}
### The productivity angle
Generative AI is especially valuable where creating a first version is slow but checking it is fast: drafting, summarising, translating, coding.
> Rule of thumb: use GenAI where verification is cheaper than creation.

# One idea underneath
Generative models learn a probability distribution over data. A language model learns P(next token | previous tokens); an image model learns which arrangements of pixels are likely. Generating means sampling from that distribution.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Learn the distribution from examples", "Start from a prompt or noise", "Sample step by step", "New content"]}
### Autoregressive vs diffusion
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Autoregressive", "items": ["one token at a time", "left to right", "used for text"]}, "r": {"t": "Diffusion", "items": ["start from noise", "refine the whole image", "used for images and video"]}}
### Why sampling gives variety
Because outputs are sampled, the same prompt can produce different results. Settings like temperature control how adventurous the sampling is.
### Conditioning
Prompts "condition" the distribution: P(image | "a red bicycle in Porto at sunset"). The better the conditioning information, the more targeted the result.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Prompt narrows the distribution", "Model samples from what's likely given the prompt", "Output matches the request"]}
!? Claude Shannon generated surprisingly English-like gibberish in 1948 just by picking each word based on the previous one, a primitive language model.

# Foundation models
The term "foundation model" was popularised by Stanford researchers in 2021 to describe large models trained on broad data that serve as a base for many applications.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Adapting a foundation model", "items": ["Prompting: no training", "Retrieval: add your documents", "Fine-tuning: adjust weights", "Tools and agents: connect to systems"]}
### Economics
Training a foundation model costs a fortune once; adapting it is cheap. That shifts most organisations from building models to applying them well.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical cost to get a working solution (illustrative, €)", "items": [{"l": "Prompting", "v": 1000.0}, {"l": "RAG system", "v": 50000.0}, {"l": "Fine-tuning", "v": 100000.0}, {"l": "Training from scratch", "v": 50000000.0, "t": "50000000", "hi": true}]}
### Homogenisation risk
When many products rely on a few foundation models, a flaw or bias in one can spread everywhere. Diversity of models and careful evaluation reduce this risk.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Benefit", "items": ["one model, many uses", "fast to build"]}, "r": {"t": "Risk", "items": ["shared flaws", "dependence on few providers"]}}
> For most companies, the strategic question is not "should we train a model?" but "how do we use and govern the best ones?"

# Main families
Generative AI has several model families, often mixed in modern systems.

• **GANs** (generative adversarial networks): a generator and a discriminator compete; powered the first realistic face generators (2014–2019)
• **VAEs** (variational autoencoders): compress to a latent space and decode; used inside many image systems

Boundaries are blurring: Transformers are used inside diffusion models, and multimodal LLMs generate images and audio directly.

# What it's not
Generative models produce plausible output, not verified truth. Understanding their limits prevents costly mistakes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Not a database", "items": ["may misremember facts"]}, "r": {"t": "Not a calculator", "items": ["arithmetic can slip without tools"]}}
### Key limitations
• **Hallucinations**: confident but wrong statements
• **Knowledge cutoff**: no awareness of recent events without tools
• **No private knowledge**: doesn't know your files unless provided
• **Inconsistency**: same question, different answers
• **Sensitivity to wording**: small prompt changes can change results

# Next-token prediction
During pre-training, the model sees text and repeatedly predicts the next token. The loss is cross-entropy: how surprised the model was by the actual next token. Gradients adjust billions of weights to reduce surprise.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Text sample", "Hide the next token", "Model predicts probabilities", "Compare with actual token", "Adjust weights"]}
### Why so much emerges
Predicting text written by experts requires modelling their knowledge and reasoning. To predict the next line of a proof, a translation or a program, the model must learn some of the underlying structure.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Grammar", "b": "needed for any prediction"}, {"a": "Facts", "b": "needed for factual text"}, {"a": "Reasoning", "b": "needed for maths and arguments"}, {"a": "Style", "b": "needed to match voices and genres"}]}
### Emergent abilities
As models scaled, abilities like multi-step arithmetic or following complex instructions seemed to appear suddenly. Later research argued many gains are smoother than they look, depending on how they're measured.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Emergence view", "items": ["abilities appear abruptly with scale"]}, "r": {"t": "Measurement view", "items": ["smooth improvement, sharp-looking metrics"]}}
> Ilya Sutskever argued that predicting the next token well requires understanding the reality that produced the text.

# Tokens
Tokens are the units models read and write. A tokeniser converts text into integer IDs from a fixed vocabulary; the model converts IDs back into text when generating.
ex: "Lisboa é bonita" | → ["Lis", "boa", " é", " bon", "ita"] | → 5 tokens (illustrative)
### Why Portuguese costs more
Tokenisers are trained mostly on English-heavy data, so English words are often single tokens while Portuguese words split into pieces. The same content can need noticeably more tokens, raising cost and using more context.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "tokens for the same text (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "English", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Portuguese", "v": 125.0}, {"l": "Greek", "v": 180.0}, {"l": "Burmese", "v": 400.0, "hi": true}]}
### Practical consequences
• API pricing: separate prices per million input and output tokens
• Output tokens usually cost several times more than input tokens
• Context limits are counted in tokens, including the model's own answer
• Some quirks, like counting letters, stem from tokenisation
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1,000 tokens", "b": "750 English words"}, {"a": "1 page", "b": "500–700 tokens"}, {"a": "1 book", "b": "100,000+ tokens"}, {"a": "1 hour of meeting transcript", "b": "10,000 tokens"}]}
!? Newer tokenisers with larger vocabularies reduce the "token tax" on non-English languages.

# Scaling laws
Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) found that model loss decreases as a power law in parameters, data and compute, across many orders of magnitude. Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, 2022, "Chinchilla") refined the optimal balance: scale data and parameters roughly equally.

### Chinchilla's insight
Many earlier models were too large for their data. Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) outperformed the much larger Gopher (280B) trained with the same compute.

# Before Transformers
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) process sequences step by step, carrying a hidden state forward. LSTMs (1997) added gates to remember longer, enabling the first strong neural translation systems around 2014–2016.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "RNN/LSTM", "items": ["sequential", "hidden state memory", "hard to parallelise"]}, "r": {"t": "Transformer", "items": ["all tokens at once", "attention", "highly parallel"]}}
### Problems
• **Sequential processing**: can't use GPU parallelism fully, so training is slow
• **Long-range memory**: information fades over long sequences
• **Bottleneck**: early translation models squeezed a whole sentence into one vector
### Attention before Transformers
In 2014 Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio added attention to RNN translation, letting the decoder look back at all input words. The Transformer kept attention and dropped recurrence entirely.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1997", "l": "LSTM"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "attention for translation"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Google neural translation"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "Transformer"}]}
!? LSTMs powered smartphone keyboards, Google Translate and voice assistants in the mid-2010s before Transformers took over.

# Attention
Self-attention computes, for each token, a weighted mix of information from all tokens in the context. Weights reflect relevance, learned during training.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Each token asks: who's relevant to me?", "Scores against every other token", "Softmax into weights", "Weighted mix of information"]}
### Example
In "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big", attention helps "it" connect to "trophy"; change "big" to "small" and "it" refers to "suitcase".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "\"it\" + \"too big\"", "b": "trophy"}, {"a": "\"it\" + \"too small\"", "b": "suitcase"}, {"a": "\"bank\" + \"rates\"", "b": "financial bank"}, {"a": "\"bank\" + \"river\"", "b": "riverbank"}]}
### Why it's powerful
Attention creates direct connections between any two positions, however far apart, so long-range dependencies are easy to learn.
### Visualising attention
Attention weights can be plotted as heatmaps showing which words attend to which, though interpreting them as explanations needs care.
> Attention lets context reshape every word's meaning, which is why the same word can mean different things in different sentences.

# Query, key, value
Each token's vector is multiplied by three learned matrices to produce a query, key and value. Attention scores are dot products between one token's query and every token's key, scaled and normalised; the output is a weighted sum of values.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d) · V", "items": [{"s": "Q", "m": "queries"}, {"s": "K", "m": "keys"}, {"s": "V", "m": "values"}, {"s": "d", "m": "vector size"}]}
### A library analogy
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Query", "b": "your search request"}, {"a": "Key", "b": "book catalogue labels"}, {"a": "Value", "b": "the books' contents"}, {"a": "Attention", "b": "read most from the best-matching books"}]}
### Why scale by √d?
Without scaling, dot products grow large in high dimensions, pushing softmax into extreme values with tiny gradients. Dividing by √d keeps training stable.
### Causal masking
In language models generating text, each token can only attend to earlier tokens, not future ones: a mask hides the future so the model can't cheat during training.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Encoder, e.g. BERT", "items": ["attends both directions"]}, "r": {"t": "Decoder, e.g. GPT", "items": ["attends only to earlier tokens"]}}
!? This single formula, a few matrix multiplications and a softmax, is the core computation behind almost every modern AI model.

# Multi-head attention
Instead of one attention computation, Transformers run several heads in parallel, each with its own query, key and value projections. Their outputs are concatenated and combined.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Heads learn different patterns", "items": ["Previous-token heads", "Syntax heads: subject–verb links", "Coreference heads: pronouns to names", "Induction heads: copy earlier patterns"]}
### Induction heads
Interpretability research found "induction heads": circuits that spot a repeated pattern ("A B … A") and predict B next. They're thought to underpin in-context learning, learning from examples in the prompt.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["See \"Harry Potter\" earlier", "Later see \"Harry\"", "Induction head predicts \"Potter\""]}
### How many heads?
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "attention heads per layer (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Original Transformer", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "GPT-3", "v": 96.0}, {"l": "Large modern models", "v": 128.0, "hi": true}]}
### Efficiency variants
Grouped-query and multi-query attention share keys and values across heads, shrinking the KV cache and speeding up inference with little quality loss.
> Many heads let the model attend to several kinds of relationships at once.

# Layers stack up
Each Transformer block has a multi-head attention sub-layer and a feed-forward sub-layer, each wrapped with residual connections and normalisation. Models stack dozens to over a hundred blocks.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Output: predict next token", "Block N: attention + feed-forward", "…", "Block 1: attention + feed-forward", "Token embeddings + positions"]}
### The residual stream
Researchers describe the residual connections as a "stream" of information flowing through the model, which each layer reads from and writes to, gradually refining representations.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Embedding", "Each layer adds updates to the stream", "Final representation", "Next-token prediction"]}
### Feed-forward layers as memory
The feed-forward layers hold much of the model's stored knowledge, acting somewhat like key–value memories that recall facts associated with patterns.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Attention", "b": "moves information between tokens"}, {"a": "Feed-forward", "b": "processes information within each token"}, {"a": "Residuals", "b": "keep information flowing"}, {"a": "Normalisation", "b": "keeps values stable"}]}
### Depth
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "layers (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Original Transformer", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "GPT-2 XL", "v": 48.0}, {"l": "GPT-3", "v": 96.0}, {"l": "Large modern models", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}]}
!? Researchers have edited specific facts in models (e.g. changing where a landmark is located) by modifying feed-forward weights in particular layers.

# Positional information
Attention treats input as a set, so without position information "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical. Positional encodings inject order.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "No positions", "items": ["bag of words", "order lost"]}, "r": {"t": "With positions", "items": ["order matters", "meaning preserved"]}}
### Approaches
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sinusoidal", "b": "original 2017 fixed wave patterns"}, {"a": "Learned", "b": "trained position vectors"}, {"a": "RoPE", "b": "rotates vectors by position, widely used"}, {"a": "ALiBi", "b": "biases attention by distance"}]}
### Extending context
Rotary embeddings (RoPE) and related tricks allowed models to be extended from a few thousand tokens to hundreds of thousands, sometimes with extra training on long documents.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "context length, thousand tokens (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Original Transformer", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "GPT-3", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "GPT-4", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Long-context models", "v": 1000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Lost in the middle
Even with long context, models may use information at the beginning and end better than information buried in the middle; newer training methods reduce this.
> Order matters in language, and positional encoding is how Transformers know it.

# Why it scaled
Transformers process all tokens of a training sequence in parallel, so GPUs can be kept busy. Combined with a simple, uniform architecture, this made scaling to billions of parameters and trillions of tokens practical.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Parallel processing", "Full GPU use", "Train on far more data", "Better models", "More investment"]}
### Simplicity helps
The same block repeated many times is easy to optimise, distribute across machines and scale up. Engineering tools and hardware co-evolved around it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "RNN training", "items": ["step-by-step", "GPU underused"]}, "r": {"t": "Transformer training", "items": ["all at once", "GPU saturated"]}}
### Transfer across domains
Because Transformers make few assumptions about data, the same architecture works for text, image patches, audio frames and protein sequences.
### Hardware co-design
Modern AI chips include tensor cores and memory systems optimised for Transformer workloads, reinforcing their dominance.
!? The original Transformer base model trained in about 12 hours on 8 GPUs; frontier models today use tens of thousands of GPUs for months.

# Attention's cost
Standard attention computes a score for every pair of tokens: for n tokens, n² scores. Doubling context quadruples attention compute and memory.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative attention cost", "items": [{"l": "1k tokens", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "2k tokens", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "4k tokens", "v": 16.0}, {"l": "8k tokens", "v": 64.0, "hi": true}]}
### Engineering solutions
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "FlashAttention", "b": "reorders computation to save memory, exact results"}, {"a": "Sparse attention", "b": "attend to selected tokens"}, {"a": "Sliding windows", "b": "attend locally"}, {"a": "Linear and state-space models", "b": "alternatives that scale linearly"}]}
### The KV cache
During generation, models cache keys and values of previous tokens so they aren't recomputed. For long contexts and many users, this cache dominates memory.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without cache", "items": ["recompute everything each token"]}, "r": {"t": "With cache", "items": ["reuse past work", "memory grows with context"]}}
### Alternatives
State-space models like Mamba (2023) and hybrid architectures process long sequences more efficiently, and some production models combine them with attention layers.
> Long context is possible today mostly thanks to clever engineering around attention's quadratic cost.

# Generating text
Generation is a loop. The model processes the prompt (the "prefill" phase, fast and parallel), then generates tokens one by one (the "decode" phase, sequential).
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Process prompt", "Predict next token", "Sample and append", "Repeat until done"]}
### Prefill vs decode
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Prefill", "items": ["whole prompt at once", "compute-bound", "fast per token"]}, "r": {"t": "Decode", "items": ["one token at a time", "memory-bound", "slower per token"]}}
### Speeding it up
• **KV caching**: reuse earlier computations
• **Batching**: serve many users together
• **Speculative decoding**: a small model drafts tokens, the big model verifies several at once
• **Quantisation**: smaller weights, faster memory reads
### Stopping
Generation stops at a special end token, a maximum length, or a stop sequence chosen by the developer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "output speed, tokens/second (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Large model, cloud", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Small model, laptop", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Optimised small model, cloud", "v": 300.0, "hi": true}]}
!? This is why you see answers appear word by word: the model is generating them one token at a time, and the app streams each as it arrives.

# One architecture, many uses
Since 2017 the Transformer has spread from translation to almost every AI field, making it one of the most versatile architectures ever designed.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Text", "b": "LLMs like GPT, Claude, Gemini"}, {"a": "Images", "b": "Vision Transformers, diffusion Transformers"}, {"a": "Audio", "b": "Whisper speech recognition"}, {"a": "Biology", "b": "AlphaFold, protein language models"}]}
### Vision Transformers
ViT (2020) splits an image into patches, like 16×16 pixels, treats each as a token and applies a standard Transformer. With enough data, it matches or beats CNNs.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Image", "Cut into patches", "Embed each patch as a token", "Transformer", "Classification"]}
### Beyond
• Time-series forecasting models
• Weather prediction
• Robotics control (vision-language-action models)
• Recommender systems over user histories
### Universal interface
Treating everything as sequences of tokens lets one model mix modalities, the basis of multimodal AI.
> When the same tool works everywhere, progress in one field quickly transfers to others.

# Context is everything

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Who you are and your role", "Who the output is for", "Why you need it", "Relevant facts and data", "Constraints and preferences"]}
### Context engineering

@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["System instructions: role, rules", "Retrieved knowledge: documents, data", "Conversation history", "User request"]}
### Too much context
Irrelevant material can distract the model and cost tokens. Give what's relevant, structured clearly.
!? Many "AI is useless" experiences come from one-line prompts that would also confuse a human colleague.

# Be specific about the task
Specific instructions reduce guesswork. Specify the output's audience, purpose, length, format and tone.

# Give examples

# Paste the source

# Step-by-step reasoning

@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Problem", "Break into steps", "Solve each step", "Combine", "Answer"]}

== gen-images

# Starting from noise
Diffusion models are trained on a forward process that adds Gaussian noise to images over many steps until only noise remains. A neural network learns the reverse: predicting and removing noise at each step.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clean image", "Add noise step by step", "Pure noise", "Model learns to reverse each step"]}
### Generation
To generate, start with random noise and apply the learned denoising repeatedly, typically 20–50 steps (fewer with newer fast samplers), gradually revealing an image.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Step 50: final image", "Step 30: details emerge", "Step 10: rough shapes", "Step 0: random noise"]}
### History
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2015", "l": "diffusion idea proposed"}, {"y": "2020", "l": "DDPM makes it practical"}, {"y": "2021", "l": "diffusion beats GANs on image quality"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion"}]}
### Why diffusion won
Diffusion models train stably (unlike GANs, which were notoriously finicky) and produce diverse, high-quality images.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "GANs", "items": ["fast generation", "unstable training", "less diversity"]}, "r": {"t": "Diffusion", "items": ["stable training", "high quality", "slower generation"]}}
!? The random starting noise acts like a seed: the same seed and prompt reproduce the same image in many tools.

# Guided by text
A text encoder converts the prompt into embeddings. During each denoising step, these embeddings guide the model through cross-attention, steering the image towards the description.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Prompt", "Text encoder", "Embeddings", "Guide each denoising step", "Matching image"]}
### Classifier-free guidance
The model predicts noise both with and without the prompt and amplifies the difference. A "guidance scale" controls how strictly the image follows the text.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low guidance", "items": ["more varied", "may drift from prompt"]}, "r": {"t": "High guidance", "items": ["follows prompt closely", "can look oversaturated"]}}
### Better text understanding
Newer systems use large language models as text encoders or rewrite prompts automatically, improving understanding of complex descriptions, spatial relations and text in images.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "CLIP encoder", "b": "image-text alignment"}, {"a": "T5 or LLM encoders", "b": "richer language"}, {"a": "Prompt rewriting", "b": "expands short requests"}, {"a": "Multimodal LLMs", "b": "generate images directly"}]}
### Negative prompts
Many tools accept negative prompts listing things to avoid ("blurry, extra fingers"), steering generation away from them.
> Image models translate language into visual structure through these learned connections.

# Latent diffusion
Running diffusion on full-resolution pixels is expensive. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) first compresses images with an autoencoder into a smaller latent representation, runs diffusion there, then decodes back to pixels.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Prompt", "Diffusion in small latent space", "Decoder", "Full-resolution image"]}
### Efficiency
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "values processed per image (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Pixel space 512×512×3", "v": 786432.0}, {"l": "Latent 64×64×4", "v": 16384.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it mattered
Latent diffusion made high-quality generation feasible on consumer GPUs. Stable Diffusion's open release in August 2022 let anyone run and customise models locally, sparking a huge ecosystem.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Autoencoder", "b": "compresses and decompresses images"}, {"a": "Diffusion model", "b": "generates in latent space"}, {"a": "Text encoder", "b": "understands the prompt"}, {"a": "Fine-tuning", "b": "custom styles and subjects"}]}
### Diffusion Transformers
Newer models replace the U-Net denoiser with Transformers ("DiT"), scaling better and powering leading image and video models.
!? Stable Diffusion's first version could generate a 512×512 image on a gaming GPU with under 10 GB of memory in seconds.

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Camera makers adding signing in hardware", "AI image generators labelling outputs", "News organisations", "Social platforms displaying labels"]}

== gen-embed

# Meaning as coordinates
Embedding models, usually Transformer encoders, read a piece of text and output a fixed-length vector capturing its meaning. Similar meanings land close together, even with different words.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "\"invoice overdue\"", "b": "close to \"late payment\""}, {"a": "\"wheat shipment delayed\"", "b": "close to \"grain cargo late\""}, {"a": "\"annual leave\"", "b": "close to \"holiday request\""}, {"a": "\"Lisbon\"", "b": "close to \"Porto\", \"Madrid\""}]}
### Dimensions
Embedding vectors typically have 384 to 3,072 dimensions. Each dimension isn't individually meaningful; meaning emerges from the overall pattern.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "dimensions (typical models)", "items": [{"l": "Small model", "v": 384.0}, {"l": "Medium", "v": 768.0}, {"l": "Large", "v": 1536.0}, {"l": "Very large", "v": 3072.0, "hi": true}]}
### Training embeddings
Embedding models are trained with contrastive learning: pull matching pairs (question and answer, title and article) together and push unrelated ones apart.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pairs of related texts", "Train to bring them close", "Push unrelated texts apart", "Meaningful vector space"]}
> Embeddings are the bridge between human language and fast mathematical search.

# Similarity
Cosine similarity measures the angle between vectors, ignoring their length. Values near 1 mean very similar meaning; near 0, unrelated.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 1.0, "unit": "cosine similarity", "items": [{"l": "Unrelated", "v": 0.1}, {"l": "Same topic", "v": 0.6}, {"l": "Paraphrase", "v": 0.9, "hi": true}]}
### Other measures
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Cosine similarity", "b": "angle, most common for text"}, {"a": "Dot product", "b": "angle and length"}, {"a": "Euclidean distance", "b": "straight-line distance"}, {"a": "Normalised vectors", "b": "cosine and dot product equivalent"}]}
### Thresholds are model-specific
A "good" similarity score differs between embedding models. Calibrate thresholds on your own examples rather than using a universal cut-off.
### Example uses
• Finding the passages most relevant to a question
• Detecting near-duplicate tickets or contracts
• Matching supplier names with different spellings
!? Two sentences in different languages with the same meaning can score higher similarity than two sentences in the same language on different topics, in multilingual models.

# Keyword vs semantic search
Keyword search (like BM25) ranks documents by matching terms, weighting rare words more. Semantic search ranks by embedding similarity. Each catches what the other misses.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Keyword", "items": ["exact terms", "codes and names", "transparent"]}, "r": {"t": "Semantic", "items": ["meaning", "synonyms", "paraphrases"]}}
### Where keywords win
Exact identifiers such as contract numbers, part numbers, error codes, chemical names and people's names: embeddings may blur them with similar-looking text.
### Hybrid search
Hybrid systems run both searches and merge results, often with reciprocal rank fusion, then re-rank. This is the standard approach for enterprise retrieval.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Query", "Keyword search + vector search", "Merge results", "Re-rank", "Top passages"]}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "retrieval quality (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Keyword only", "v": 65.0}, {"l": "Semantic only", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Hybrid", "v": 78.0}, {"l": "Hybrid + re-rank", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}]}
> If users search for codes and IDs as well as concepts, you need hybrid search.

# Vector databases
Vector databases index embeddings for fast approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search across millions or billions of vectors, typically in milliseconds.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "pgvector", "b": "vectors inside PostgreSQL"}, {"a": "Azure AI Search", "b": "managed hybrid search"}, {"a": "Pinecone, Qdrant, Weaviate", "b": "dedicated vector databases"}, {"a": "Elasticsearch, OpenSearch", "b": "keyword plus vectors"}]}
### How ANN works
Exact search compares the query with every vector, too slow at scale. Indexes like HNSW (a navigable graph) find near-best matches by hopping through neighbours, trading a tiny bit of accuracy for huge speed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Query vector", "Enter graph at top layer", "Hop towards closer neighbours", "Return nearest candidates"]}
### Choosing
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Scale of data", "Need for hybrid keyword search", "Metadata filtering, e.g. by department", "Security and access control", "Operational simplicity"]}
### Access control
Enterprise search must respect permissions: users should only retrieve documents they're allowed to see. Store access metadata with each chunk and filter at query time.
!? For many companies, adding vectors to an existing database like PostgreSQL is simpler than running a separate vector database.

# Chunking
Chunking splits documents into retrievable pieces. Retrieval quality depends heavily on chunk size and boundaries.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too large", "items": ["mixed topics", "diluted meaning", "wastes context"]}, "r": {"t": "Too small", "items": ["lost context", "fragments", "more noise"]}}
### Strategies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fixed size", "b": "e.g. 500 tokens with overlap"}, {"a": "Structural", "b": "by headings, sections, clauses"}, {"a": "Semantic", "b": "split where topics change"}, {"a": "Hierarchical", "b": "small chunks linked to parent sections"}]}
### Adding context
Attaching a document title, section heading or a short generated summary to each chunk ("contextual retrieval") helps match queries correctly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Document", "Split by sections", "Add title and heading to each chunk", "Embed", "Index"]}
### Tables and PDFs
Tables, scanned PDFs and slides need special handling: extract tables as structured text, run OCR, and keep captions with figures.
> Chunking is unglamorous but often the biggest lever on retrieval quality.

# Other uses
Embeddings are a general tool for comparing meaning, well beyond search.

### Recommendations
Embed products, articles or courses and recommend items near what a user engaged with.
### Classification with few labels
Train a simple classifier on embeddings with a few hundred labelled examples, often matching more complex models at far lower cost.

Multilingual embedding models are trained on parallel and multilingual data so that equivalent meanings in different languages map to nearby vectors.

### Benefits
• One index for documents in many languages
• Users search in their own language
• No need to translate entire corpora first

### Industrial example
Search years of inspection photos for "corrosion on pipe joints" without anyone having tagged them.

### Multimodal embeddings
Newer models embed text, images, audio and video in one space, enabling cross-media search, like finding the video moment matching a sentence.

# Re-ranking
First-stage retrieval (vector or keyword) is fast but approximate. A re-ranker, usually a cross-encoder model that reads the query and each candidate together, scores relevance more precisely.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Query", "Fast retrieval: top 50", "Re-ranker scores each", "Top 5 to the LLM"]}
### Why two stages
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bi-encoder retrieval", "items": ["embeds separately", "fast", "less precise"]}, "r": {"t": "Cross-encoder re-ranking", "items": ["reads together", "slower", "more precise"]}}
### Benefits
Re-ranking often noticeably improves the relevance of the top results, which matters because LLMs answer from only a handful of passages.
### LLMs as re-rankers
Language models can also re-rank or filter candidates, judging relevance with reasoning, at higher cost.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relevant passage in top 5, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Vector only", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "+ keyword hybrid", "v": 78.0}, {"l": "+ re-ranker", "v": 88.0, "hi": true}]}
> If your RAG system retrieves "nearly right" passages, add a re-ranker before changing anything else.

# Keep embeddings fresh
Embeddings are snapshots of documents at indexing time. When documents change, are added or deleted, the index must update, or answers become stale.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Document changes", "Detect change", "Re-chunk and re-embed", "Update index", "Remove deleted items"]}
### Pipelines
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Scheduled re-indexing", "Change-triggered updates", "Deletion handling", "Versioning of documents", "Monitoring index freshness"]}
### Model consistency
Queries and documents must use the same embedding model and version. Upgrading the model means re-embedding the whole corpus, which can be costly for large collections.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Same model", "items": ["comparable vectors"]}, "r": {"t": "Mixed models", "items": ["meaningless similarity"]}}
### Governance
Track which documents are indexed, their source, owner and access rights. Remove documents promptly when they're withdrawn or must be deleted under data protection rules.
!? Stale indexes are a common cause of chatbots confidently citing outdated policies.

== gen-rag

# The idea
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), named in a 2020 Facebook AI paper, combines a search step with generation. Instead of answering from memory, the model answers from retrieved passages placed in its context.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["User question", "Retrieve relevant passages", "Add them to the prompt", "Model answers from them", "Cite sources"]}
### Why it works
Models are good at reading and synthesising text in front of them, and much less reliable at recalling specific facts from training. RAG plays to their strength.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without RAG", "items": ["memory only", "may hallucinate", "no private data"]}, "r": {"t": "With RAG", "items": ["grounded in documents", "current", "citable"]}}
### Typical enterprise uses
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Policy and HR assistants", "IT and technical support", "Contract and legal Q&A", "Product and engineering documentation", "Customer service knowledge"]}
### Agentic RAG
Newer systems let the model decide when and what to search, run several searches, read results and search again, handling questions that need multiple steps.
!? RAG is probably the most common architecture behind enterprise "chat with your documents" tools.

# Why not just fine-tune?
Fine-tuning changes model weights through training; RAG changes what the model sees at question time. They solve different problems.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fine-tuning", "items": ["style", "format", "behaviour", "costly to update"]}, "r": {"t": "RAG", "items": ["facts", "documents", "updates instantly", "cites sources"]}}
### Facts in weights are fragile
Fine-tuning on documents doesn't reliably make a model recall exact facts, and it can increase hallucination of plausible-sounding details. Updating facts means retraining.
### Combining both
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "RAG", "b": "what to say: facts and sources"}, {"a": "Fine-tuning", "b": "how to say it: format, tone, domain style"}, {"a": "Prompting", "b": "task instructions"}, {"a": "Tools", "b": "live data and actions"}]}
### Decision guide
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Choose", "items": ["Knowledge changes often? RAG", "Need citations? RAG", "Need consistent format or tone? Fine-tune or prompt", "Narrow repetitive task at scale? Consider fine-tuning a small model"]}
> Start with prompting plus RAG; fine-tune only when evaluations show a clear gap.

# The ingestion pipeline
Ingestion turns raw documents into a searchable index. Quality here determines everything downstream.

### Sources
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["SharePoint and file shares", "Confluence and wikis", "PDFs and scanned documents", "Emails and tickets", "ERP and CRM records"]}
### Metadata that helps
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Date", "b": "prefer recent versions"}, {"a": "Document type", "b": "policy vs draft"}, {"a": "Owner and department", "b": "filtering and accountability"}, {"a": "Access groups", "b": "permission filtering"}, {"a": "Version", "b": "avoid duplicates"}]}

Complex PDFs, scanned documents, tables, headers and footers often break naive text extraction. Specialised document-parsing tools and vision-language models help preserve structure.

# Retrieval quality is everything
The model can only answer from what's retrieved. If the right passage isn't in the top results, even the best model will fail or guess.

### Query rewriting
Users ask vague or conversational questions ("what about the other one?"). Rewriting the query into a standalone, specific search, or generating several variations, improves retrieval.

# Citations
Citations link each claim to its source passage, letting users verify answers and building trust. They also discourage the model from inventing information.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"The demurrage rate is $18,000\"", "r": "\"The demurrage rate is $18,000 per day [Contract 4471, clause 12.3, p. 8]\""}]}
### Implementation
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Number retrieved passages", "Instruct the model to cite after each claim", "Link citations to document and page", "Check cited passages actually support claims"]}
### Faithfulness checks
Automated checks can verify that cited passages contain the claimed information, flagging unsupported statements.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Answer with citations", "Check each claim against its passage", "Flag unsupported claims", "Human review"]}
### Saying "I don't know"
Instruct the system to say when the documents don't contain the answer, rather than filling gaps from memory. Users trust a system more when it admits limits.

# Access control
Enterprise RAG must enforce the same permissions as the source systems. Otherwise, a chatbot could reveal salaries, legal cases or board papers to anyone who asks.

### Approaches
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Filter at retrieval", "b": "store access groups per chunk"}, {"a": "Separate indexes", "b": "per department or sensitivity"}, {"a": "Query-time checks", "b": "verify access with source system"}, {"a": "Data classification", "b": "exclude highly sensitive data entirely"}]}
### Common mistakes

• Permissions changing in the source but not in the index

> "Oversharing" through AI search is one of the top enterprise concerns; fix permissions in sources before indexing.

== gen-finetune

# When to fine-tune
Fine-tuning continues training a pre-trained model on your examples. It's powerful but adds cost, maintenance and risk. Use it when cheaper methods fall short.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Fine-tuning: change model behaviour", "RAG: add knowledge", "Few-shot examples: show the pattern", "Prompting: instructions"]}
### Good reasons to fine-tune
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Consistent output format at scale", "House style or tone", "Narrow classification or extraction tasks", "Shorter prompts to cut cost and latency", "Making a small model match a large one on one task"]}
### Poor reasons
• Teaching frequently changing facts (use RAG)
• Fixing problems that clearer prompts would solve
• Before having an evaluation set to measure improvement
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Try first", "items": ["prompting", "examples", "RAG"]}, "r": {"t": "Then consider", "items": ["fine-tuning if evals show a gap"]}}
> The ladder: prompt → add examples → add retrieval → fine-tune → train from scratch (almost never).

# What you need
Fine-tuning needs well-curated training examples, a held-out evaluation set and clear success criteria.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Training examples", "b": "hundreds to thousands of input–output pairs"}, {"a": "Validation set", "b": "examples held out for tuning"}, {"a": "Test set", "b": "final, untouched evaluation"}, {"a": "Baseline", "b": "best prompt-only result to beat"}]}
### Data quality
Examples must be consistent: if three annotators format outputs differently, the model learns inconsistency. Write guidelines and review samples.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "accuracy (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "500 noisy examples", "v": 78.0}, {"l": "500 clean examples", "v": 88.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "5,000 noisy examples", "v": 82.0}]}
### Data sources
• Historical records (past classifications, approved emails)
• Expert-written examples
• Outputs from a stronger model, reviewed by humans
### Privacy
Training data may include personal or confidential information; check legal basis, anonymise where possible and confirm where training happens.
!? Fine-tuned models can memorise and repeat training examples, so don't include secrets you wouldn't want the model to output.

# Full fine-tuning vs LoRA
Full fine-tuning updates all weights and needs memory for weights, gradients and optimiser states, several times the model size. LoRA (2021) freezes the original weights and learns small low-rank matrices added to certain layers.

### How small?
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "trainable parameters, % of a 7B model (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Full fine-tune", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "LoRA", "v": 0.5, "hi": true}]}
### QLoRA
QLoRA (2023) combines 4-bit quantisation of the base model with LoRA adapters, letting people fine-tune large models on a single GPU.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Load base model in 4-bit", "Train small LoRA adapters", "Save adapter file (MBs)", "Load with base model to use"]}
### Multiple adapters
One base model can serve many LoRA adapters, one per task or customer, switching quickly. This makes customisation cheap at scale.
> Parameter-efficient fine-tuning made customising open models accessible to small teams.

# Distillation
Distillation transfers capability from a large teacher to a smaller student. For language models, this often means the teacher generates high-quality answers or reasoning traces for many prompts, and the student is fine-tuned on them.

### Why
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Cost", "b": "small models cost far less per request"}, {"a": "Speed", "b": "lower latency"}, {"a": "Deployment", "b": "can run on-premise or on devices"}, {"a": "Focus", "b": "specialised on your task"}]}
### Results
Distilled models can match much larger models on narrow tasks, though they usually lose some general ability.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "cost per million requests (illustrative, €)", "items": [{"l": "Large model", "v": 5000.0}, {"l": "Distilled small model", "v": 300.0, "hi": true}]}
### Terms of service
Some model providers restrict using their outputs to train competing models; check licences before distilling.
!? DeepSeek released smaller models distilled from its R1 reasoning model in 2025, showing reasoning skills can transfer to compact models.

# Preference tuning
Preference tuning trains on comparisons: for a prompt, a preferred response and a rejected one. It shapes subtle qualities like tone, helpfulness and conciseness that are hard to specify with single "correct" answers.

### DPO
Direct Preference Optimisation (2023) trains directly on preference pairs without a separate reward model or reinforcement learning loop, making preference tuning simpler and more stable.

### Business uses
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Brand tone in customer replies", "Concise vs detailed answers", "Polite refusals", "Domain-appropriate caution"]}
### Collecting preferences
Preferences can come from user feedback (thumbs up/down, edits), expert comparisons or AI judges guided by guidelines.
> Edits users make to AI drafts are a rich source of preference data.

# Catastrophic forgetting
When fine-tuning hard on narrow data, neural networks can overwrite general abilities learned earlier: catastrophic forgetting. A model tuned on invoices might become worse at general reasoning or safety behaviour.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"Train longer on our data = better model\"", "r": "Over-training can erase general skills and safety behaviour"}]}
### Mitigations
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lower learning rates", "Fewer epochs", "LoRA adapters", "Mix in general data", "Evaluate on general tasks too"]}
### Safety erosion
Research in 2023 found that fine-tuning on even small datasets could weaken a model's safety training, sometimes unintentionally. Re-test safety after fine-tuning.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before fine-tune", "items": ["general skills", "safety behaviour"]}, "r": {"t": "After aggressive fine-tune", "items": ["narrow skill", "weaker general and safety behaviour"]}}
### Evaluate broadly
Keep a general evaluation set alongside your task set to detect regressions.
!? LoRA's frozen base weights reduce forgetting because the original knowledge isn't overwritten.

# Domain models
Domain-specific models are trained or adapted on specialised text: medicine, law, finance, chemistry. BloombergGPT (2023), trained partly on financial data, is a well-known example.

### The general-model surprise
Evaluations showed that subsequent general models often matched or beat earlier domain-specific models on domain tasks, because general capability improved so quickly.
### When domain adaptation helps

# Tool use
Tool use connects a model to the outside world: databases, search, calculators, business systems and other software. The model chooses which tool to call and with what arguments.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["User request", "Model chooses a tool", "Application runs it", "Result returned to model", "Model answers or calls another tool"]}
### Why tools matter
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fresh information", "b": "search and APIs"}, {"a": "Exact calculation", "b": "code and calculators"}, {"a": "Private data", "b": "company systems"}, {"a": "Actions", "b": "create tickets, send messages, update records"}]}
### Read vs write tools
Read-only tools (search, query) are low risk; write tools (send email, place order, change records) need stronger controls and often human confirmation.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Read tools", "items": ["look up", "lower risk"]}, "r": {"t": "Write tools", "items": ["change things", "need approval and limits"]}}
### Tool descriptions matter
Clear names, descriptions and parameter definitions help the model use tools correctly. Poor descriptions cause wrong calls.
> Tools turn a model from a talker into a doer, which multiplies both usefulness and risk.

# How function calling works
Function calling is a structured protocol between the application and the model. The model never runs code itself; it requests a call, and your application decides whether to execute it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Define tools with JSON schemas", "Send with the user message", "Model returns a tool call", "App executes and returns result", "Model continues"]}
### Example
ex: Tool: get_order_status(order_id) | Model returns: {"name": "get_order_status", "arguments": {"order_id": "PO-4471"}} | App returns: {"status": "shipped", "eta": "2026-11-03"}
### Parallel and multi-step calls
Models can request several tools at once (check stock in three warehouses) or chain calls (find customer, then their orders, then shipment status).
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Single call", "b": "one lookup"}, {"a": "Parallel calls", "b": "several lookups together"}, {"a": "Sequential calls", "b": "each depends on the last"}, {"a": "Loop", "b": "agent continues until done"}]}
### Control points
Your application can validate arguments, check permissions, ask the user for confirmation and log every call before executing.
!? The application, not the model, holds credentials to systems, so access is controlled by your code.

# Why evals matter
Evaluations (evals) turn subjective impressions into measurable evidence. They're essential because generative outputs vary, models update, and small changes can have surprising effects.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without evals", "items": ["\"seems good\" from a few tries", "surprises in production"]}, "r": {"t": "With evals", "items": ["measured quality", "confident changes"]}}
### What evals enable
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Compare models and prompts", "Catch regressions", "Justify investments", "Detect drift", "Demonstrate compliance"]}
### The demo trap
Impressive demos use favourable examples. Real users bring messy, varied inputs; only evaluation on representative data reveals true performance.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Demo: hand-picked examples", "Pilot: real but few", "Evaluation: representative set", "Production monitoring"]}
> Evals are the unit tests of AI systems: tedious to build, invaluable once you have them.

# Build a test set
A good test set reflects the real distribution of inputs plus the hard cases that matter most.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Typical cases", "b": "what users usually ask"}, {"a": "Hard cases", "b": "complex, ambiguous inputs"}, {"a": "Edge cases", "b": "unusual formats, empty inputs"}, {"a": "Adversarial cases", "b": "attempts to break or misuse"}]}

### Expected outputs
For each case, define the ideal answer or clear criteria: required facts, forbidden content, format rules.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "recommended size (rough)", "items": [{"l": "Quick start", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Solid coverage", "v": 200.0}, {"l": "High-stakes system", "v": 1000.0, "hi": true}]}

# Exact metrics
When there's a clear right answer, automatic comparison is cheap, fast and objective.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Classification", "b": "accuracy, precision, recall, F1"}, {"a": "Extraction", "b": "field-level exact or fuzzy match"}, {"a": "Code", "b": "tests passing"}, {"a": "Structured output", "b": "schema validity"}]}
### Field-level extraction scoring
ex: Expected: {qty: 25000, price: 312.5, port: "Santos"} | Got: {qty: 25000, price: 312.5, port: "Santos, BR"} | Exact: 2/3; normalised match: 3/3

Compare normalised values (dates in one format, numbers without separators, trimmed text) so trivial differences don't count as errors.
### Confusion matrices
For classification, look at which labels get confused; it reveals ambiguous categories or missing guidance.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Exact match", "items": ["strict", "simple"]}, "r": {"t": "Normalised or fuzzy match", "items": ["fairer", "needs rules"]}}

== gen-halluc

# What a hallucination is
In generative AI, a hallucination is content that's fluent and confident but false, fabricated or unsupported by the provided sources. The term covers invented facts, fake citations, wrong numbers and misread documents.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Hallucination types", "items": ["Factual: wrong facts about the world", "Faithfulness: contradicts provided sources", "Fabricated references: fake citations, URLs", "Reasoning errors: wrong logical steps"]}
### Why it happens
• The training objective rewards plausible continuations, not verified truth
• Evaluation and training have historically rewarded guessing over abstaining
• Rare facts are weakly encoded and easily blended with similar ones
• Models are pushed to be helpful and complete
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Question about a rare fact", "Weak memory", "Plausible pattern fills the gap", "Confident wrong answer"]}
### A 2025 view
OpenAI researchers argued in 2025 that hallucinations persist partly because benchmarks score a confident guess better than "I don't know", so models learn to guess. Rewarding calibrated abstention could reduce it.
> Hallucination isn't lying: the model has no intent; it's producing likely text without a built-in truth check.

# When it's most likely
Risk depends on the question type, the domain and whether the model has sources.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "hallucination risk (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Common knowledge", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Summarising provided text", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Niche facts", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Citations from memory", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}]}
### High-risk patterns
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Exact figures and statistics", "Dates and timelines", "Citations, case law, URLs", "Biographical details of lesser-known people", "Questions with false premises"]}
### False premises
"Why did Portugal win the 1966 World Cup?" may elicit an invented explanation instead of a correction (Portugal finished third). Good models now challenge false premises more often, but not always.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Answering a false premise", "r": "Correcting it: \"Portugal didn't win, it finished third in 1966\""}]}
### Long outputs
Errors accumulate over long lists and documents; spot-check the middle and end, not just the start.
!? Asking for "10 examples" of something rare can push a model to invent the last few to meet the number.

# Grounding
Grounding ties answers to authoritative sources supplied at question time: documents, database results or search results. The model's job shifts from recalling to reading and synthesising.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ungrounded", "items": ["answers from training memory", "unverifiable"]}, "r": {"t": "Grounded", "items": ["answers from supplied sources", "checkable"]}}
### Grounding instructions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["\"Answer only using the documents provided\"", "\"Quote the relevant passage\"", "\"If the answer isn't there, say so\"", "\"Note any conflicts between sources\""]}
### Measuring faithfulness

# The context window
The context window is the total number of tokens the model can process in one request: input plus output. It's the model's working memory for that request.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Output being generated", "User's latest message", "Retrieved documents and tool results", "Conversation history", "System instructions"]}
### Sizes today
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "context window, thousand tokens (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Typical small model", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "Common models", "v": 128.0}, {"l": "Large-context models", "v": 200.0}, {"l": "Longest", "v": 1000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Input vs output limits
Models also have a maximum output length, often much smaller than the context window. Long reports may need to be generated in sections.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "128k tokens", "b": "~300 pages of text"}, {"a": "1M tokens", "b": "~2,500 pages"}, {"a": "Max output", "b": "often 8k–64k tokens"}, {"a": "Images", "b": "also consume tokens"}]}
### What counts
Everything counts: system prompt, earlier messages, attached files, tool results and the answer itself. Long chats fill the window quickly.
!? An image attached to a chat may consume around a thousand tokens or more, depending on resolution and the model.

Long context is convenient but has costs. Price, latency and sometimes accuracy suffer as context grows.

Benchmarks like "needle in a haystack" show modern models can find a single planted fact in long contexts, but harder tasks, reasoning over many scattered facts, degrade with length.

# Prompt caching
Prompt caching stores the processed state of a repeated prompt prefix, such as long system instructions or a large document. Subsequent requests reusing that prefix skip recomputation.

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Put stable content first: instructions, documents", "Put variable content last: the user's question", "Reuse the same prefix across many requests", "Keep cache windows in mind"]}

• Batch processing with shared instructions

@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Use AI", "items": ["drafts", "research starts", "analysis", "learning", "formatting"]}, "r": {"t": "Avoid or be careful", "items": ["unverifiable critical facts", "forbidden confidential data", "sensitive personal conversations", "final legal or medical decisions"]}}
### Human connection
Condolence messages, difficult feedback and personal apologies carry weight because they come from you. AI can help you think, but the words should be yours.
### Verification cost test
If checking the AI's output would take as long as doing the task yourself, and errors matter, AI may not save time.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fast to verify", "b": "good AI task"}, {"a": "Slow to verify, low stakes", "b": "acceptable"}, {"a": "Slow to verify, high stakes", "b": "do it yourself or with experts"}]}
### Confidentiality
When in doubt about data sensitivity, don't paste it; ask your data protection or security team.
!? People report AI is most useful for tasks they already know well enough to judge the result.
