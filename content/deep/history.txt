== hi-turns

# Human history as one day
The trick of squeezing history into a day works because our minds can't feel the difference between 3,000 and 300,000 years. Turn the numbers into clock time and the proportions suddenly become obvious.
ex: 300,000 years ÷ 1,440 minutes in a day | ≈ 208 years per minute | Farming began about 12,000 years ago → 58 minutes before midnight → about 23:02
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "00:00", "l": "Homo sapiens appears"}, {"y": "23:02", "l": "Farming begins"}, {"y": "23:36", "l": "Writing in Sumer"}, {"y": "23:57", "l": "Printing press"}, {"y": "23:59", "l": "Steam engine"}]}
### What filled the rest of the day
For about 96% of our existence, people lived in small, mobile bands of perhaps 20–50, hunting, fishing and gathering plants. They were not primitive. They had the same brains as us, made jewellery, buried their dead, traded shells over hundreds of kilometres and painted caves.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of Homo sapiens' time", "items": [{"l": "Hunter-gatherers", "v": 96.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Farming & after", "v": 4.0}]}
### Why the pace speeds up
Each turning point made the next one come faster. Farming fed more people; more people meant more inventors; writing and printing let ideas pile up instead of being forgotten. So the clock is crowded at the end not because recent people are cleverer, but because knowledge accumulates.
• Farming: about 58 minutes before midnight
• Writing: about 25 minutes before midnight
• Everything since 1800, including electricity, cars and phones: roughly the last minute
> On the human clock, almost everything we call "history" happened in the last half-hour.
!? Portugal's Côa Valley holds thousands of rock engravings of horses, aurochs and ibex, many over 20,000 years old: art made by hunter-gatherers long before 23:02.

# Farming changed everything
Farming wasn't one invention. Over many generations, people kept the seeds of plants that didn't scatter on the wind and the animals that were calmer to handle. Slowly, wild wheat, barley, goats and sheep became domesticated: dependent on humans, and far more productive.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plant crops, herd animals", "Food surplus", "Store, trade and tax it", "Non-farmers can be fed", "Villages become cities"]}
### It happened more than once
The Fertile Crescent was first, but people elsewhere invented farming independently, with their own crops. That tells us it was a common human response to a warmer, more stable climate after the last Ice Age.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Farming began in", "items": ["Fertile Crescent: wheat", "China: rice, millet", "Mesoamerica: maize", "Andes: potatoes"]}
### The hidden costs
Early farmers often had a harder life than foragers. Skeletons show they were shorter, had more tooth decay and suffered from diseases caught from animals and crowded villages. A single bad harvest could mean famine. But farming fed far more people per square kilometre, so farmers outnumbered and slowly replaced most hunter-gatherers.
• Farming reached Portugal around 5500 BCE, spreading along the Mediterranean coast
• Megaliths such as the Almendres Cromlech near Évora were raised by early farming communities
!! Farming made life easier => It produced more food overall, but early farmers often worked longer and were less healthy than foragers
> Farming didn't make people richer at first; it made them more numerous, and numbers changed everything.

# Writing started as bookkeeping
Long before writing, people in Mesopotamia used small clay tokens to count goods: a cone for a measure of grain, a cylinder for an animal. To make a contract tamper-proof, tokens were sealed in clay envelopes, with their shapes pressed on the outside. Eventually someone realised the marks alone were enough.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clay counting tokens", "Marks pressed on clay", "Pictures for words", "Signs for sounds"]}
### From accounts to literature
Early tablets mix numbers with pictures of goods. Over centuries the signs became abstract wedges, and many began to stand for sounds, so scribes could write names, laws, letters and stories. Epics like Gilgamesh came over a thousand years after the first receipts.
• Cuneiform was used for about 3,000 years and for more than a dozen languages
• Hundreds of thousands of tablets survive, because baked clay lasts
• It was deciphered by European scholars in the 1800s
### Invented several times
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "3200 BCE", "l": "Cuneiform in Sumer"}, {"y": "3200 BCE", "l": "Hieroglyphs in Egypt"}, {"y": "1200 BCE", "l": "Chinese characters"}, {"y": "1050 BCE", "l": "Phoenician alphabet"}]}
The Phoenician alphabet, with a sign for each sound, was passed to the Greeks and then the Romans. The letters you're reading now descend from it.
!? One of the oldest known customer complaints is a clay tablet from Ur, around 1750 BCE, in which a man called Nanni grumbles to a merchant, Ea-nasir, about poor-quality copper.
> Writing was invented to keep track of debts and taxes; stories came later.

# The printing press
Gutenberg didn't invent printing. China printed with woodblocks by the 800s, and Korea used metal movable type in the 1300s. His breakthrough was a complete system that suited an alphabet of a few dozen letters.
### The system
• A hand mould to cast thousands of identical letters quickly
• A metal alloy of lead, tin and antimony that cast cleanly
• Oil-based ink that stuck to metal
• A screw press, like those used for grapes and olives
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cast metal letters", "Set them into a page", "Ink and press", "Reuse letters next page"]}
### Speed and price
His Bible, finished around 1455, was printed in about 180 copies; around 49 survive. By 1500 presses were working in more than 250 European towns, and books cost a fraction of a hand copy.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Scribe", "items": ["a few pages a day", "one copy at a time", "errors creep in"]}, "r": {"t": "Printing press", "items": ["hundreds of sheets a day", "identical copies", "cheaper every year"]}}
### Why it mattered
Identical copies meant scholars across Europe could cite the same page, compare data and correct errors. Pamphlets let Luther reach hundreds of thousands of readers. Authorities tried to license and ban books, but they could not keep up.
!! Gutenberg invented printing => China and Korea printed centuries earlier; Gutenberg made it fast, cheap and practical for alphabets
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "c. 1455", "l": "Gutenberg Bible"}, {"v": "250+", "l": "towns with presses by 1500"}]}
> The press didn't just copy books faster; it made knowledge identical, cheap and hard to suppress.

# The Industrial Revolution
Britain had a rare mix: lots of cheap coal, high wages that made machines worth buying, a big trading empire, patents, and canals to move heavy goods. Inventors had a reason to replace muscle with machines.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1712", "l": "Newcomen steam pump"}, {"y": "1769", "l": "Watt's better engine"}, {"y": "1830", "l": "Liverpool–Manchester rail"}, {"y": "1856", "l": "Portugal's first railway"}]}
### How it worked
Textiles came first. Spinning machines like the spinning jenny and Arkwright's water frame multiplied what one worker could produce. James Watt's engine, with a separate condenser, burned far less coal than earlier ones, so steam could power mills anywhere, not just beside rivers. Then came railways and steamships.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Coal mined", "Steam engines", "Factories and railways", "Cheaper goods", "Cities grow"]}
### The costs
• Children as young as five or six worked in mills and mines
• Cities like Manchester grew fast, with crowded, dirty housing
• Burning coal began the rise in atmospheric CO₂
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈280 ppm", "l": "CO₂ before 1750"}, {"v": "≈420 ppm", "l": "CO₂ in the 2020s"}]}
Portugal industrialised later, with textiles in the north and cork and canning in the south. Its first railway, Lisbon to Carregado, opened in 1856.
!! The Industrial Revolution was a sudden explosion => Growth was only about 1–2% a year, but it never stopped, and over two centuries that compounded into a revolution
> For the first time, living standards could rise every generation instead of being eaten up by more mouths.

# Fire and tools
Tool-making is far older than our species. The oldest known stone tools, from Kenya, are about 3.3 million years old. Around 1.7 million years ago, Homo erectus began shaping symmetrical hand axes, a design used for over a million years.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "3.3M ago", "l": "First stone tools"}, {"y": "1.7M ago", "l": "Hand axes"}, {"y": "1M ago", "l": "Early traces of fire"}, {"y": "400k ago", "l": "Regular use of fire"}]}
### Why cooking mattered
Cooking breaks down fibres and starches, kills germs and makes food softer, so we get more energy from it with less chewing. Some scientists argue this freed energy for a bigger, hungrier brain and allowed smaller guts and teeth. The idea is popular but still debated.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "brain volume, cm³ (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Chimpanzee", "v": 400.0}, {"l": "Homo erectus", "v": 900.0}, {"l": "Modern human", "v": 1350.0, "hi": true}]}
### Fire did more than cook
• Warmth let people live in colder places
• Light extended the day for talking and making things
• Fire scared off predators and could reshape landscapes
• Heat let Neanderthals make glue from birch bark
!! Stone Age tools were crude lumps of rock => Later tools were precise blades, needles and spear-throwers that demanded years of skill
> Fire turned humans from animals that eat whatever they find into the only animal that processes its food.

# Out of Africa
The oldest known Homo sapiens fossils, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old. For most of our history we lived only in Africa. Small groups left earlier, but the main dispersal that gave rise to today's non-African populations began roughly 60,000–70,000 years ago.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Africa", "Middle East", "South Asia and Australia", "Europe", "The Americas"]}
### Dates, in years ago
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "300,000", "l": "Sapiens in Africa"}, {"y": "55,000", "l": "Reaching Australia"}, {"y": "45,000", "l": "Reaching Europe"}, {"y": "15,000+", "l": "Reaching the Americas"}]}
Reaching Australia meant crossing open sea, so these people had boats. The Americas were reached from Siberia across a land bridge exposed when sea levels were lower in the Ice Age.
### Meeting other humans
Our ancestors weren't alone. Neanderthals lived in Europe and western Asia, and Denisovans further east. Groups met and sometimes had children together.
• Most people outside Africa carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA
• Iberia was one of the last Neanderthal refuges
• In 1998 the skeleton of a child, buried over 20,000 years ago, was found at Lapedo near Leiria
!? Many scientists think the Lapedo child shows a mix of modern human and Neanderthal features, though this is still debated.
> Every person alive today is descended from Africans, and many carry a little Neanderthal too.

# Cities and states
Uruk, in southern Iraq, may have had 40,000 people or more by about 3000 BCE, an astonishing size at the time. Irrigation canals needed organising, harvests needed storing, and stores needed guarding. Out of these jobs grew officials, priests, soldiers and kings.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["King", "Priests and officials", "Soldiers and scribes", "Craftspeople and traders", "Farmers"]}
### What made a state
• A ruler with power to tax and command
• Officials and records, which is why writing mattered
• Laws written down and enforced
• An army to defend and conquer
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "3200 BCE", "l": "Uruk, first big city"}, {"y": "2300 BCE", "l": "Sargon's Akkad empire"}, {"y": "2100 BCE", "l": "Ur's great ziggurat"}, {"y": "1754 BCE", "l": "Hammurabi's laws"}]}
### Not only Mesopotamia
Cities also grew independently along the Nile, the Indus (Mohenjo-daro, around 2500 BCE), the Yellow River in China and later in Mexico and Peru. Each time, the pattern was similar: farming surplus, then cities, then kings and writing.
!! "An eye for an eye" meant equal justice => In Hammurabi's code, punishment depended on rank: blinding a nobleman cost an eye, blinding a commoner cost silver
> Cities and states grew from the problem of managing a surplus: someone had to count it, guard it and decide who got it.

# Money
Money does three jobs: it lets you buy without swapping goods directly, it measures value, and it stores wealth for later. Long before coins, Mesopotamians used weighed silver to keep accounts; the shekel was originally a weight of about 8 grams.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Medium of exchange", "b": "accepted for any purchase"}, {"a": "Unit of account", "b": "prices in one measure"}, {"a": "Store of value", "b": "keeps worth over time"}]}
### Coins, then paper
Lydian kings stamped lumps of electrum, a natural gold and silver alloy, so no one had to weigh them. The idea spread to Greek cities and Persia. In China, merchants used paper receipts for heavy coins, and the Song government issued official paper money in the 1020s. Europe's first banknotes came from Stockholm in 1661.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "3000 BCE", "l": "Weighed silver"}, {"y": "600 BCE", "l": "Lydian coins"}, {"y": "1020s", "l": "Chinese paper money"}, {"y": "1661", "l": "Europe's first notes"}, {"y": "2002", "l": "Euro coins and notes"}]}
### Portugal's money
• The **real** was used for centuries, so big sums were counted in thousands of réis
• The escudo replaced it in 1911
• The euro arrived in notes and coins in 2002, at 200.482 escudos per euro
!! Before money, people bartered goods => Historians and anthropologists find little evidence of barter economies; communities mostly ran on credit and debts, recorded or remembered
> Money is a shared promise: it works only because everyone trusts others will accept it.

# The internet age
The internet began as ARPANET, a US research network that sent its first message in 1969. The shared language TCP/IP arrived in 1983, and in 1989–91 Tim Berners-Lee at CERN, near Geneva, created the World Wide Web, making it easy for anyone to publish and link pages.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1969", "l": "ARPANET's first link"}, {"y": "1991", "l": "Web goes public"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "iPhone launched"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "Most people online"}]}
### Faster than any earlier turning point
Writing took thousands of years to spread and printing centuries. The internet reached most of humanity in about 30 years.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "internet users, billions (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1995", "v": 0.04}, {"l": "2000", "v": 0.4}, {"l": "2010", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "2024", "v": 5.5, "hi": true}]}
### What changed
• Work: email, remote work and global supply chains
• Learning: almost any knowledge is a search away
• Politics and news: anyone can publish, including misinformation
• Daily life: maps, banking and shopping in your pocket
Still, around a quarter to a third of people remain offline, mostly in poorer, rural regions.
!? Well over 95% of intercontinental data travels through undersea cables, not satellites. Several, such as EllaLink to Brazil, land on Portugal's coast.
> The internet is the printing press again, but for everyone at once, at almost no cost per copy.

== hi-discovery

# Why Portugal turned to the sea
Portugal had perhaps a million people in the 1400s, poor soils in much of the country and a long land border with a much bigger Castile. Expansion by land was blocked. But it had a long Atlantic coast, skilled fishermen, and Lisbon, a busy port where Genoese and other merchants brought money and know-how.
### The motives
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why sail south?", "items": ["Gold from West Africa", "Spices from Asia", "Christian allies", "Crusade and glory"]}
West African gold reached Europe through Muslim traders across the Sahara. Reaching it by sea would cut out the middlemen. Europeans also hoped to find "Prester John", a legendary Christian king in Africa or Asia, as an ally against Islam.
### Step by step
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1415", "l": "Ceuta captured"}, {"y": "1419", "l": "Madeira reached"}, {"y": "1427", "l": "Azores sighted"}, {"y": "1434", "l": "Cape Bojador passed"}, {"y": "1488", "l": "Cape of Good Hope"}]}
The capture of Ceuta, a North African port, gave Portugal's nobles a taste for overseas ventures. Islands like Madeira soon paid for themselves with wheat and then sugar, funding further voyages.
• Peace with Castile from 1411 freed resources for adventures abroad
• Royal support meant voyages could continue for decades
!? Cape Bojador, on today's Western Sahara coast, was feared as a point of no return. Gil Eanes finally sailed past it in 1434 and found the sea beyond was perfectly ordinary.
> Portugal went to sea not because it was strong, but because the ocean was the one direction it could grow.

# The caravel
The caravel was a small, light ship, typically 20–25 metres long with a crew of about 20. Its **lateen** sails, triangular and set along the length of the ship, came from Mediterranean and Arab sailing traditions.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Square sail", "items": ["great with wind behind", "poor into the wind", "good for cargo ships"]}, "r": {"t": "Lateen sail", "items": ["sails closer to wind", "needs skilled crew", "ideal for exploring"]}}
### The return problem
Sailing south along Africa was easy: the trade winds blew that way. Coming back meant beating against them for weeks. The solution was the **volta do mar** ("turn of the sea"): sail far out into the open Atlantic, curve north to the latitude of the Azores, and ride the westerly winds home.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sail south along Africa", "Head out into Atlantic", "Swing north to Azores", "Westerlies blow you home"]}
### Shallow and nimble
A shallow hull let caravels creep along unknown coasts, enter river mouths and avoid sandbanks. They were less useful for heavy cargo, so later fleets mixed caravels with bigger ships.
• Columbus' Niña and Pinta were caravels
• The "caravela redonda" added square sails for speed downwind
• Da Gama's fleet to India used larger naus plus one caravel, the Bérrio
!! Caravels carried the spice trade => They were exploration ships; the cargo was carried by big naus (carracks) able to hold hundreds of tonnes
> The caravel's genius was not size but agility: it could go into the wind and into the unknown.

# Finding latitude from the stars
Latitude is how far north or south of the equator you are. Because Earth is round, the sky looks different at different latitudes. At the North Pole, the Pole Star is overhead (90°); at the equator it sits on the horizon (0°). In between, its height above the horizon matches your latitude.
### Using the Sun instead
Below the equator, the Pole Star disappears. The Sun is always there, but its noon height changes with the seasons. Portuguese astronomers produced tables of the Sun's **declination**: how far north or south of the equator it is on each day.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "lat = 90° − h + d", "items": [{"s": "lat", "m": "your latitude"}, {"s": "h", "m": "Sun's height at noon"}, {"s": "d", "m": "Sun's declination (table)"}]}
ex: 21 June, Sun at noon is 74.7° high | Table says declination = +23.4° | 90 − 74.7 + 23.4 = 38.7° N, the latitude of Lisbon
### The instruments
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Quadrant", "b": "plumb line marks the angle"}, {"a": "Mariner's astrolabe", "b": "heavy brass ring, sights the Sun"}, {"a": "Cross-staff", "b": "slides to match star height"}]}
Tables printed in the 1490s, including Abraham Zacuto's almanac, published in Leiria in 1496, made the method reliable.
### What they couldn't do
Longitude, your east–west position, needed accurate time at sea. That problem wasn't solved until marine chronometers in the late 1700s. Until then, navigators sailed to the right latitude and then ran east or west along it.
!? In the 1400s the Pole Star was about 3.5° away from the true pole, so pilots learned a rule, the "regimento do Norte", to correct for its position.
> A simple angle, measured with care and corrected with tables, told sailors where they were on an empty ocean.

# The great voyages
Each voyage built on the one before. Pilots, maps and wind knowledge were carefully gathered in Lisbon and often kept secret from rivals.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1488", "l": "Dias rounds Africa"}, {"y": "1498", "l": "Da Gama in Calicut"}, {"y": "1500", "l": "Cabral in Brazil"}, {"y": "1522", "l": "Round the world"}]}
### Dias and da Gama
Bartolomeu Dias was blown south by a storm and only realised he had passed Africa's tip when he turned north and found the coast on his left. He called it the Cape of Storms; the king renamed it the Cape of Good Hope. Vasco da Gama left Lisbon in July 1497 with four ships and took about ten months to reach Calicut. He made a huge loop through the South Atlantic, months without sight of land. Many of his men died of scurvy; perhaps a third returned in 1499.
### Cabral and Magellan
Cabral's fleet of 13 ships landed at Porto Seguro in April 1500, then continued to India. Fernão de Magalhães, after a quarrel with King Manuel I, offered his services to Spain. He found the strait at South America's tip that bears his name, crossed the Pacific, and was killed in the Philippines in 1521. Juan Sebastián Elcano brought the last ship home.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "5 ships", "l": "left Spain in 1519"}, {"v": "≈270", "l": "men set out"}, {"v": "18", "l": "came home in 1522"}]}
!? Magellan named the Pacific ("peaceful") because his crossing met calm weather; it is the largest ocean on Earth.
> In 34 years, Portuguese navigators turned the edge of the known world into a set of sea routes.

# Splitting the world: Tordesillas
After Columbus returned in 1493, the Pope issued a bull giving Castile lands west of a line 100 leagues beyond the Azores and Cape Verde. King João II of Portugal protested and negotiated directly with Castile, moving the line much further west.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "West of the line", "items": ["went to Castile", "most of the Americas"]}, "r": {"t": "East of the line", "items": ["went to Portugal", "Africa, Asia route, Brazil"]}}
ex: 370 leagues west of Cape Verde | leagues varied, so about 2,000 km | line at roughly 46° W, cutting through today's Brazil
### Why move the line?
Historians debate whether Portugal already suspected land in the west. Either way, the wider sea gave room for the Atlantic loop on the way to India, and it put Brazil's eastern bulge on Portugal's side.
### What happened next
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1494", "l": "Treaty of Tordesillas"}, {"y": "1500", "l": "Cabral lands in Brazil"}, {"y": "1529", "l": "Zaragoza: Asian line"}, {"y": "1750", "l": "Madrid: new borders"}]}
• Other powers like France, England and the Netherlands ignored the treaty
• Portuguese settlers and explorers pushed far west of the line into the Amazon
• The Treaty of Madrid in 1750 accepted borders close to Brazil's modern ones
!! The Pope divided the world between Spain and Portugal => The final line came from a direct treaty between the two crowns; the Pope later confirmed it
> A line drawn on a map in a small Castilian town decided the language of half of South America.

# Prince Henry's role
D. Henrique was the third surviving son of King João I and Philippa of Lancaster. He fought at Ceuta in 1415 and became governor of the Order of Christ, a wealthy military-religious order whose income helped pay for ships.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Funding", "b": "Order of Christ and trade rights"}, {"a": "Base", "b": "Lagos and Sagres, Algarve"}, {"a": "People", "b": "pilots, mapmakers, builders"}, {"a": "Goals", "b": "gold, crusade, knowledge"}]}
### What he achieved
Under his sponsorship, expeditions colonised Madeira and the Azores, passed Cape Bojador and reached as far as Sierra Leone by the time he died in 1460. He held monopolies on trade along the coast and took a share of the profits.
### The myth of Sagres
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Ran a navigation school", "r": "Gathered experts; no formal school"}, {"w": "A great sailor", "r": "An organiser who rarely sailed far"}]}
### A complicated figure
His ventures also began the Portuguese trade in enslaved Africans: in 1444 captives were sold at Lagos, watched by Henry himself. He was also behind the disastrous attack on Tangier in 1437.
!? The nickname "the Navigator" was popularised by English and German writers in the 1800s; Portuguese sources usually just call him the Infante.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1394", "l": "Born in Porto"}, {"y": "1415", "l": "Fights at Ceuta"}, {"y": "1420", "l": "Heads Order of Christ"}, {"y": "1460", "l": "Dies at Sagres"}]}
> Henry's real invention was persistence: decades of funded, step-by-step voyages instead of one heroic gamble.

# The spice trade
In medieval Europe, spices were luxuries and status symbols. Pepper came from India's Malabar coast, cinnamon from Sri Lanka, and cloves and nutmeg only from a few tiny islands in today's Indonesia.
### The old route
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Spice islands", "Indian ports", "Red Sea or Gulf", "Alexandria, Beirut", "Venice, then Europe"]}
At each stage, a merchant or ruler took a cut, so prices multiplied many times on the way. Venice grew rich as the main European buyer.
### The Cape route
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Overland route", "items": ["many middlemen", "taxes at every port"]}, "r": {"t": "Cape route", "items": ["buy at the source", "one long sea voyage"]}}
The voyage was dangerous; ships and lives were often lost. But one successful fleet could pay for several failures. The crown controlled imports through the Casa da Índia in Lisbon, and pepper became the state's great business.
• Lisbon became Europe's pepper market in the early 1500s
• Venice's trade fell sharply at first but partly recovered later
• From about 1600 the Dutch and English pushed Portugal aside
!! Spices were used to hide rotten meat => Those who could afford spices could afford fresh meat; spices were about flavour, medicine and prestige
> The spice trade was about cutting out middlemen, and whoever controlled the sea route controlled the profit.

# The Portuguese in Asia
Portugal couldn't conquer Asia's great empires. Instead, governor Afonso de Albuquerque seized a few key ports to control the main sea lanes, creating the **Estado da Índia**, a network of forts, trading posts and fleets.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1510", "l": "Goa"}, {"y": "1511", "l": "Malacca"}, {"y": "1515", "l": "Hormuz"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "First reach Japan"}, {"y": "1557", "l": "Macau"}]}
### How it worked
Goa was the capital. Malacca controlled the strait to the spice islands, Hormuz the entrance to the Persian Gulf. Portugal forced local ships to buy passes, called **cartazes**, and attacked those without them. It was a sea empire run by violence as well as trade.
### Long-lasting traces
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Goa Portuguese until 1961", "Macau handed to China in 1999", "Kristang creole in Malacca", "Japanese \"pan\" from pão"]}
In Japan, Portuguese traders brought firearms, which spread rapidly, and Jesuit missionaries converted many people before Christianity was banned in the early 1600s.
!! Japanese "arigatō" comes from "obrigado" => The Japanese word existed before the Portuguese arrived; it's a coincidence
!? Tempura is often linked to Portuguese missionaries' meatless fasting days ("tempora"), though the exact origin is debated.
> Portugal's Asian empire was a chain of ports, not territory, and its traces are still in Asian food, words and families.

# The dark side
The Portuguese began trading enslaved people from West Africa in the 1440s. After sugar plantations spread to the Atlantic islands and then Brazil, the trade grew enormously. Enslaved Africans were forced across the ocean in brutal conditions; many died on the way.
### The scale
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "millions embarked, by ship's flag (est.)", "items": [{"l": "Portugal/Brazil", "v": 5.8, "hi": true}, {"l": "Britain", "v": 3.3}, {"l": "France", "v": 1.4}, {"l": "Spain", "v": 1.1}, {"l": "Netherlands", "v": 0.55}]}
In total about 12.5 million people were forced onto ships from Africa between the 1500s and 1860s, and about 10.7 million survived the crossing. Brazil received the largest share, about 4.8 million.
### Ending it
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1444", "l": "Captives sold in Lagos"}, {"y": "1761", "l": "No new slaves in Portugal"}, {"y": "1869", "l": "Slavery ends in empire"}, {"y": "1888", "l": "Brazil abolishes slavery"}]}
Brazil was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. The legacy lives on in Brazil's population, culture and inequalities, and in African communities shaped by the trade.
• Lagos opened a museum on the site of its old slave market in 2016
• Lisbon has planned a memorial to the enslaved people

# The gift of the Nile
The Greek historian Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the Nile", and he was right. Egypt gets almost no rain. Without the river, it would be desert like the land on either side.
### Why the river flooded
Every summer, monsoon rains fell on the highlands of Ethiopia. The Blue Nile carried that water, and tonnes of mineral-rich silt, north. From about July to October the river spread over its banks; when it fell back, it left a fresh layer of black mud. Farmers sowed straight into it.

@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Kemet", "items": ["the \"black land\"", "fertile river valley"]}, "r": {"t": "Deshret", "items": ["the \"red land\"", "desert all around"]}}
### Floods meant taxes
A good flood meant a big harvest; a low one meant famine; a very high one could wash villages away. Officials measured the river with **nilometers**, stone steps or shafts marked with levels, and set taxes according to how high the water rose.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈6,650 km", "l": "length of the Nile"}, {"v": "≈95%", "l": "of Egyptians live near it"}]}
• The river was also Egypt's highway: boats sailed south with the wind and drifted north with the current
• Papyrus reeds from its banks became paper, rope and boats
!? The annual flood stopped after the Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970. Egypt now relies on fertilisers, as the silt is trapped behind the dam.

In the intermediate periods, central power broke down. Local governors ruled their own areas, harvests failed or foreigners took over. # The pyramids
Pyramids grew out of earlier tombs. Kings were first buried under flat-topped benches of mud brick called mastabas. Around 2670 BCE, the architect Imhotep stacked six of them in stone for King Djoser: the Step Pyramid at Saqqara. Khufu's father, Sneferu, then experimented until he got smooth sides right.

### The numbers
The Great Pyramid was about 146 m tall when finished (about 139 m today, after losing its smooth white casing and tip). Its base is about 230 m on each side, and the sides face north, south, east and west with an error of a small fraction of a degree.

The pyramid was a machine for eternity: it protected the king's body and helped him join the Sun god in the sky. Its shape may echo the sun's rays or the mound from which Egyptians believed creation began.

# Hieroglyphs
Hieroglyphs mixed three kinds of signs. Some stood for whole words, some for sounds, and some were silent hints that told the reader what kind of word it was. A scribe might spell a word with sound signs, then add a picture of a man to show it was a person.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Word signs", "b": "a picture means the thing"}, {"a": "Sound signs", "b": "about 24 single consonants"}, {"a": "Determinatives", "b": "silent hints of meaning"}]}
### Faster scripts
Carving was slow, so scribes also wrote a joined-up cursive called hieratic with reed pens on papyrus, and later an even quicker script called Demotic. Only a small elite, perhaps a few per cent of people, could read.
### Cracking the code
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "The Rosetta Stone", "items": ["Hieroglyphs: for temples", "Demotic: everyday script", "Greek: rulers' language"]}
The stone, a priestly decree of 196 BCE, was found by French soldiers in 1799 and has been in the British Museum since 1802. Because scholars could read the Greek, they had a key. Thomas Young made progress, but Jean-François Champollion cracked it in 1822. He spotted that names in oval frames (cartouches), like Ptolemy and Cleopatra, were spelled with sound signs, and he knew Coptic, a late form of Egyptian.

# Mummies
The first Egyptian mummies were accidental: bodies buried in hot, dry sand dried out naturally. When elites began burying people in coffins and tombs, bodies rotted, so embalmers learned to dry them artificially.

### The process
The brain was pulled out through the nose with a hook and thrown away; Egyptians thought it unimportant. The heart, seat of thought and feeling, was left in place. Natron, a natural salt from dry lake beds, sucked out the water. Hundreds of square metres of linen might be used, with protective amulets tucked between layers.
### Where the organs went

"Pharaoh" comes from the Egyptian for "great house", meaning the palace, a bit like saying "the White House". The king's job was to keep **ma'at**: cosmic order, truth and justice. He built temples, made offerings and defeated enemies so that the Nile would flood and the Sun would rise.

# Philosophy

# Greek science

# Theatre
Greek theatre grew out of festivals for Dionysus, god of wine. At Athens' City Dionysia, playwrights competed for prizes. Around 534 BCE, Thespis is said to have stepped out of the chorus as the first actor, which is why actors are still called "thespians".
### Tragedy and comedy
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Aeschylus", "b": "tragedy: the Oresteia"}, {"a": "Sophocles", "b": "tragedy: Oedipus the King"}, {"a": "Euripides", "b": "tragedy: Medea"}, {"a": "Aristophanes", "b": "comedy: The Frogs"}]}
Plays used at most three speaking actors, all men, wearing masks to switch roles, plus a chorus that sang and danced. Tragedies explored fate, pride and justice; comedies mocked politicians by name.
### The building
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Theatron: tiered seats", "Orchestra: chorus dances", "Skene: stage building"]}
Epidaurus, built in the 300s BCE, seats about 14,000. Its rows of limestone seats seem to damp low background noise while reflecting voices. Guides love to demonstrate the acoustics with a coin dropped on stage; recent measurements show normal speech carries very well, though whether a true whisper reaches the top rows is debated.
### Words from the stage
• Scene from skene, the stage building
• Orchestra from the dancing floor
• Tragedy, possibly "goat song"
!? The Greek word for actor, hypokritēs, gave us "hypocrite": someone playing a part.
> The Greeks invented drama as a public event where a whole city watched itself.

== hi-rome

# Kingdom, republic, empire
Legend says Rome was founded by Romulus, a twin raised by a she-wolf. Archaeology shows villages on Rome's hills from around that time, which merged into a town by a ford on the Tiber.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "753 BCE", "l": "Traditional founding"}, {"y": "509 BCE", "l": "Last king expelled"}, {"y": "264 BCE", "l": "Wars with Carthage"}, {"y": "44 BCE", "l": "Caesar murdered"}, {"y": "27 BCE", "l": "Augustus, emperor"}]}
### The republic
After expelling their last king, Romans hated the word "king" so much they built a system to stop any one man taking power. Power was shared and limited in time.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Two consuls", "b": "elected, ruled for one year"}, {"a": "Senate", "b": "ex-officials, advised"}, {"a": "Assemblies", "b": "citizens voted, passed laws"}, {"a": "Tribunes", "b": "protected ordinary people"}]}
Around 450 BCE, Rome's laws were written down on the Twelve Tables so that everyone could know them.
### Why it became an empire
Conquests brought slaves, loot and huge armies loyal to their generals rather than to the state. In the first century BCE, generals like Marius, Sulla, Pompey and Julius Caesar fought civil wars. Caesar's adopted heir Octavian won, kept the Senate and elections for show, and took the name Augustus. He held real power until his death in 14 CE.
!! Julius Caesar was the first Roman emperor => Caesar was dictator; Augustus was the first emperor. "Caesar" became a title, which gave us Kaiser and Tsar
> Rome kept the look of a republic long after one man held all the power.

# The empire's size
At its largest, the Roman Empire covered about 5 million km² and every coast of the Mediterranean, which Romans called **Mare Nostrum**, "our sea".
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈5 M km²", "l": "area under Trajan"}, {"v": "50–70 M", "l": "people"}, {"v": "≈1 M", "l": "living in Rome itself"}]}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "area, million km² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Roman Empire, 117", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "European Union", "v": 4.2}, {"l": "Portugal", "v": 0.09}]}
### What held it together
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["About 30 legions on the borders", "Roads and sea lanes", "Latin in the west, Greek in east", "Citizenship for loyal elites"]}
Local towns largely governed themselves, collected taxes and copied Roman life: baths, forums, theatres. In 212 CE, almost all free men in the empire were made Roman citizens.
### Too big to hold
Trajan's conquests in Mesopotamia were given up by his successor Hadrian almost at once. Hadrian preferred fixed borders and built a wall across northern Britain from 122 CE.
ex: Rome to Britain's Hadrian's Wall | about 2,000 km by road | roughly 6–8 weeks for an official messenger at normal pace
!? The empire's population was perhaps a fifth to a quarter of all humans alive at the time.
> Rome ruled a world, but the distances were so great that it depended on local elites wanting to be Roman.

# Lusitania
Romans first came to Iberia in 218 BCE, during their war with Carthage. Conquering the whole peninsula then took about 200 years, longer than anywhere else in the empire.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "218 BCE", "l": "Romans land in Iberia"}, {"y": "147 BCE", "l": "Viriato leads revolt"}, {"y": "139 BCE", "l": "Viriato murdered"}, {"y": "25 BCE", "l": "Mérida founded"}]}
### Viriato
Viriato was a Lusitanian, possibly a shepherd, who survived a Roman massacre of his people in 150 BCE. He led a guerrilla war, using the hills to ambush and defeat several Roman armies. Rome could not beat him in battle, so it bribed three of his companions to kill him in his sleep.
### Two provinces

So the Minho and Trás-os-Montes were not actually in Lusitania, but in the region of Gallaecia, shared with today's Galicia.

> Lusitania became Roman slowly and painfully, and Viriato became Portugal's first national hero.

# Roman Portugal today
Roman Portugal is unusually easy to visit. Many sites are small, quiet and right in the middle of towns.

# Roads and aqueducts
Roman roads were built for armies first. They went as straight as possible, with stone milestones at every Roman mile (about 1.48 km). Major roads were layered for strength and drainage.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Paving stones, curved to drain", "Fine gravel and lime", "Coarse rubble", "Large flat stones", "Dug trench"]}
### Aqueducts
An aqueduct is a gently sloping channel that lets gravity pull water from springs in the hills to a city. Most of it ran underground. Arches were used only to cross valleys, which is why they are the famous bit.
ex: The aqueduct to Nîmes falls about 12.6 m over about 50 km | ≈ 25 cm per km | a slope of only about 0.025%
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Spring in the hills", "Gently sloping channel", "Bridges across valleys", "Settling tanks", "Fountains and baths"]}
Rome itself had 11 aqueducts, supplying public fountains, baths and rich homes with water flowing day and night.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Lisbon's aqueduct is Roman", "r": "Águas Livres was built in 1700s"}]}
!? Some Roman bridges, like Chaves' Trajan Bridge, carried traffic for about 1,900 years.
> Roman engineers moved water across whole regions using only gravity and patience.

# Roman concrete
Roman concrete (**opus caementicium**) mixed lime, water and volcanic ash called pozzolana, from near Pozzuoli by Naples, with chunks of stone or brick. The ash reacts with lime to make a hard, durable binder, even underwater.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Lime + volcanic ash", "Add rubble and water", "Pour into moulds", "Hardens, even in sea"]}
### Why it lasts
Studies of Roman harbour walls show seawater seeping in slowly grows new mineral crystals that strengthen the concrete. Research in 2023 suggested small lumps of lime left in the mix could also dissolve and refill cracks: a kind of self-healing.
### The Pantheon
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "43.3 m", "l": "dome diameter = height"}, {"v": "≈9 m", "l": "open hole (oculus)"}, {"v": "≈1,900", "l": "years standing"}]}
The builders made the dome thinner and lighter near the top, using lighter stones such as pumice, and set coffers (sunken panels) into it to cut weight.

> Roman concrete wasn't magic; it was chemistry that keeps working for centuries.

# Roman law
Rome produced professional legal experts, the jurists, who wrote opinions on real cases. Their reasoning about contracts, property, inheritance and families became a huge body of law.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "450 BCE", "l": "Twelve Tables"}, {"y": "534 CE", "l": "Justinian's Corpus"}, {"y": "c. 1100", "l": "Studied in Bologna"}, {"y": "1804", "l": "Napoleon's Code"}, {"y": "1966", "l": "Portugal's Civil Code"}]}
### Justinian's collection
In Constantinople, Emperor Justinian ordered all of it organised. The result, later called the **Corpus Juris Civilis**, had four parts.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Code", "b": "emperors' laws"}, {"a": "Digest", "b": "jurists' opinions"}, {"a": "Institutes", "b": "textbook for students"}, {"a": "Novels", "b": "new laws after 534"}]}
### Rediscovery
In the west it was largely forgotten until a copy of the Digest resurfaced in Italy in the late 1000s. Scholars at Bologna studied it, and lawyers trained in Roman law spread across Europe, including Portugal.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Civil law", "items": ["written codes", "Portugal, Brazil, France"]}, "r": {"t": "Common law", "items": ["judges' precedents", "UK, USA, Australia"]}}
• Legal terms like "contrato", "obrigação" and "usufruto" come from Roman law
• Ideas such as legal persons, written contracts and property rights shaped modern economies
!? Brazil, Mozambique and Angola inherited civil law through Portugal, so Roman legal ideas reach well beyond Europe.
> Rome's armies vanished, but its legal reasoning still governs contracts in Lisbon and São Paulo.

Ordinary Romans spoke **Vulgar Latin**, the everyday language of soldiers, traders and settlers. After the empire broke up, regions drifted apart until their speech became separate languages.

# The fall of the West
The empire faced crises long before 476: civil wars, plagues, inflation and invasions in the 200s. Emperors responded by splitting the work. In 330 Constantine founded a new capital, Constantinople, and after 395 East and West had separate emperors.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "395", "l": "Empire split for good"}, {"y": "410", "l": "Visigoths sack Rome"}, {"y": "476", "l": "Last western emperor"}, {"y": "1453", "l": "Constantinople falls"}]}
### Why the West fell
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Many causes", "items": ["Civil wars, weak emperors", "Money and tax troubles", "Pressure from migrations", "Richer East went its way"]}
The East was richer, more urban and easier to defend. It survived and is known today as the Byzantine Empire, until the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople in 1453.
### In Iberia
In 409, Germanic peoples crossed into Iberia. The Suebi set up a kingdom in the northwest with its capital at Braga, which lasted until the Visigoths absorbed it in 585.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "West", "items": ["fell in 476", "Germanic kingdoms"]}, "r": {"t": "East", "items": ["lasted to 1453", "Greek-speaking"]}}
!! Rome fell suddenly in 476 => It was a slow transformation; many people barely noticed, as Roman towns, law and Church carried on
!? A German historian once collected 210 different explanations that have been offered for Rome's fall.
> The western empire didn't crash in a day; it faded into the kingdoms that became medieval Europe.

# Christianity
Christianity began among the followers of Jesus, executed in Jerusalem around 30 CE. Missionaries such as Paul spread it along Roman roads and sea routes, first to Jewish communities and then to others, especially in cities.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 30", "l": "Jesus crucified"}, {"y": "64", "l": "Nero's persecution"}, {"y": "313", "l": "Edict of Milan"}, {"y": "325", "l": "Council of Nicaea"}, {"y": "380", "l": "Official religion"}]}
### Persecution and tolerance
Christians refused to sacrifice to the Roman gods and the emperor, which looked like disloyalty. Persecutions were occasional, but some, like Diocletian's from 303, were severe. Constantine, after a victory he credited to the Christian God, legalised Christianity with his co-emperor in 313. Under Theodosius it became the state religion in 380.
### Built on Roman foundations
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Diocese", "b": "a Roman administrative unit"}, {"a": "Bishops", "b": "one per Roman city"}, {"a": "Latin", "b": "language of western Church"}, {"a": "Rome", "b": "seat of the Pope"}]}
In Portugal, Braga became an important bishopric; its archbishops later called themselves "primate of the Spains".
!? Portuguese weekdays (segunda-feira, terça-feira…) avoid the pagan god names other languages use; tradition credits the 6th-century bishop Martinho de Dume, in Braga.
> Christianity used the empire's roads, cities and language to spread, and then outlived the empire itself.

== hi-medieval

# A thousand years
Italian writers like Petrarch, in the 1300s, saw a glorious ancient past, then a long gloomy time, then a revival in their own day. The term "Middle Ages" (medium aevum) took hold in the 1400s–1600s for that middle period.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "476", "l": "Western empire ends"}, {"y": "800", "l": "Charlemagne crowned"}, {"y": "1143", "l": "Portugal a kingdom"}, {"y": "1347", "l": "Black Death"}, {"y": "1492", "l": "Columbus, Granada"}]}
### Three stages
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Late: 1300–1500, plague, war", "High: 1000–1300, growth", "Early: 500–1000, kingdoms form"]}
Historians usually split the period. The early centuries saw fewer and smaller towns in the west. After about 1000, population, trade, towns and building boomed, until famine and plague struck in the 1300s.
### When did it end?
There's no single date. Historians choose different markers depending on what they study.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1450s", "b": "printing press"}, {"a": "1453", "b": "fall of Constantinople"}, {"a": "1492", "b": "voyage to the Americas"}, {"a": "1517", "b": "start of Reformation"}]}

# Feudalism
After Rome's fall, there was little money and few strong states. Rulers paid for soldiers with the one thing they had: land. A lord promised loyalty and service; in return his lord granted him a **fief**, land plus the peasants who worked it.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["King", "Great lords and bishops", "Knights", "Peasants and serfs"]}
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Lord grants land", "Vassal swears loyalty", "Vassal gives service", "Lord gives protection"]}
### Life on the manor
Serfs weren't slaves, but they were tied to the land. They farmed their own strips and owed the lord work, often several days a week, plus payments in grain, eggs or money. They also paid a tithe to the Church.
### Portugal was different
Portuguese kings stayed strong, partly because the Reconquista made them war leaders. Many towns received a **foral**, a royal charter setting out their rights and taxes, which limited what lords could demand.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lord's side", "items": ["grants land", "protects", "judges disputes"]}, "r": {"t": "Vassal's side", "items": ["fights for lord", "gives advice", "pays dues"]}}
!! Feudalism was a neat pyramid everywhere => It was messy and varied by region; the word "feudalism" was coined centuries later, and historians debate how useful it is
> In a world without much money, land was how you paid for loyalty.

# The Church
The Church touched everything: birth (baptism), marriage, death and burial. Bells marked the hours, saints' days set the calendar, and the parish priest was often the only local person who could read.

# The Black Death
The plague is caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, which lives in wild rodents. It arrived in Europe in 1347 on ships from the Black Sea and spread along trade routes, reaching Portugal in 1348.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bacteria in rodents", "Fleas bite rats", "Fleas bite humans", "Coughing spreads it"]}
### Three forms
• Bubonic: swollen, painful lymph nodes (buboes); many died within a week
• Pneumonic: in the lungs, spread by coughing, almost always fatal
• Septicaemic: in the blood, killing fastest of all
Some researchers now think human fleas and lice also spread it between people, helping explain how fast it moved.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1346", "l": "Siege of Caffa, Crimea"}, {"y": "1347", "l": "Reaches Sicily"}, {"y": "1348", "l": "Reaches Portugal"}, {"y": "1377", "l": "First quarantine law"}]}
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1/3 to 1/2", "l": "of Europeans died"}, {"v": "≈200 years", "l": "for population to recover"}]}
### Responses
Nobody knew about bacteria. People blamed bad air, the stars or God's anger. Some groups whipped themselves in public; others attacked Jewish communities, falsely accused of poisoning wells. Ragusa (Dubrovnik) and then Venice developed isolation for arriving ships.
ex: Ships made to wait 30 days, later 40 | Italian "quaranta giorni" = 40 days | → the word "quarantine"
!? The plague kept returning for centuries; one of the last big western European outbreaks hit Marseille in 1720.
> The Black Death was the deadliest pandemic in European history, and quarantine was one of its lasting lessons.

# After the plague
The plague killed people but left land, tools and buildings intact. Suddenly there were too few workers for the work, and labour became valuable.

### Lords fight back
Governments tried to force wages back to pre-plague levels. In Portugal, King Fernando's **Lei das Sesmarias** of 1375 ordered idle land to be farmed and pushed labourers back to the fields.

### What students learned
Teaching was in Latin. Students started with the seven liberal arts, then could move to higher faculties.

# 711: a new era in Iberia
In 711 a commander called **Tariq ibn Ziyad** crossed the strait from North Africa with an army of perhaps 7,000–12,000 men, most of them Berbers rather than Arabs. The rock where he landed still carries his name: Jabal Tariq, "Tariq's mountain", which became Gibraltar.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "711", "l": "Tariq crosses strait"}, {"y": "c. 716", "l": "Most of Iberia taken"}, {"y": "756", "l": "Emirate of Córdoba"}, {"y": "929", "l": "Caliphate declared"}, {"y": "1031", "l": "Caliphate breaks up"}]}
### Why it was so fast
The Visigothic kingdom was divided. Its king, Roderic, had just taken the throne in a disputed succession, and his rivals may even have helped the invaders. Roderic died at the Battle of Guadalete, and many towns surrendered by treaty, keeping their property and religion in exchange for tribute.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "A quick conquest", "items": ["Visigoths split by feuds", "King killed in battle", "Towns made deals", "Few big cities to besiege"]}
### Who ruled
At first al-Andalus was a far-western province of the Umayyad caliphate, ruled from Damascus. When the Umayyads were overthrown in 750, a surviving prince, Abd al-Rahman I, escaped to Iberia and set up an independent emirate in Córdoba in 756.
• The far north, around Asturias, was never conquered
• Tradition dates the first Christian victory to Covadonga, around 722
• The name al-Andalus may echo the Vandals, but its origin is still debated
!! The Arabs swept in and replaced the population => Most people stayed; over generations many converted and learnt Arabic, while a small Arab and Berber elite ruled
> The conquest of 711 was quick because a divided kingdom gave way, not because Iberia was emptied and refilled.

# Córdoba
Under Abd al-Rahman III, who declared himself caliph in 929, Córdoba became the capital of the richest state in western Europe. Population estimates vary widely, from about 100,000 to several hundred thousand, but it was certainly far bigger than Paris or London at the time.

### The Great Mosque
Building began in 785 and was enlarged by later rulers until it held a forest of more than 800 columns, topped by striped red-and-white double arches. Some columns were reused from Roman and Visigothic buildings.

### City life
• Running water from restored Roman aqueducts and many public baths
• Markets selling silk, leather, paper and ivory
• The library of caliph al-Hakam II, said to hold hundreds of thousands of books (the number is probably exaggerated)
• A palace-city, Madinat al-Zahra, built just outside town

> For about a century, Córdoba was the place western Europe looked to for luxury, learning and power.

# Three faiths
Islamic law treated Christians and Jews as **dhimmis**, "protected people" of the Book. They could keep their religion, property and own courts, but paid a special tax (the jizya) and faced limits: no new churches, no public processions, no authority over Muslims.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Muslims", "b": "ruling faith, a minority at first"}, {"a": "Mozarabs", "b": "Christians speaking Arabic"}, {"a": "Muladíes", "b": "local converts to Islam"}, {"a": "Jews", "b": "protected, often prosperous"}]}
### A changing picture
Over centuries, conversion slowly turned Muslims into the majority. Some Christians and Jews rose high: the Jewish doctor Hasdai ibn Shaprut served as a diplomat for Abd al-Rahman III. But tensions flared too. In the 850s about 48 Christians in Córdoba were executed after deliberately insulting Islam, and in 1066 a mob in Granada killed many Jews.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Coexistence", "items": ["shared trade and language", "scholars worked together"]}, "r": {"t": "Inequality", "items": ["extra taxes", "legal limits"]}}
### Harder times
From the late 1000s, rulers from North Africa, the Almoravids and then the Almohads, were far stricter. Maimonides' family fled Córdoba in the 1140s. The Christian kingdoms, for their part, later forced Jews and Muslims to convert or leave.
!! Al-Andalus was a paradise of tolerance => It allowed more diversity than most of Europe then, but within a clear hierarchy, and conditions changed a lot over time
> Al-Andalus offered protection, not equality, and that was unusual enough for its time.

# Islamic golden age
From the 700s the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad paid for the translation of Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic. A centre often called the **House of Wisdom** gathered scholars. They didn't just copy; they tested, corrected and added.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Greek and Indian texts", "Translated into Arabic", "Corrected and extended", "Toledo: into Latin", "Read in European schools"]}
### Big names
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Al-Khwarizmi", "b": "algebra, Baghdad, c. 820"}, {"a": "Ibn Sina", "b": "Canon of Medicine, c. 1025"}, {"a": "Ibn al-Haytham", "b": "optics, Cairo, c. 1020"}, {"a": "Ibn Rushd", "b": "Aristotle's commentator"}]}
Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine was still a textbook in European universities in the 1600s. Ibn al-Haytham showed that we see because light enters the eye, not because the eye sends out rays, and insisted on testing ideas with experiments.
### Paper made it possible
Papermaking reached the Islamic world from China in the 700s; Baghdad had paper mills by about 800. Cheap paper made books far more common than in Christian Europe. One of Europe's first paper mills worked at Xàtiva, near Valencia, under Muslim rule.
### The route to Europe
After Castile took Toledo in 1085, translators there turned Arabic books into Latin. Gerard of Cremona alone translated dozens of works, including Ptolemy's astronomy and Ibn Sina.
!? Many star names, like Aldebaran, Betelgeuse and Rigel, come from Arabic.
> Much of the "rediscovery" of Greek science in medieval Europe arrived by way of Arabic.

# Numbers we use
Indian mathematicians developed a system with just nine digits plus a sign for zero, where a digit's **place** sets its value. By 628 the astronomer Brahmagupta was writing rules for calculating with zero as a number.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["India: 9 digits + zero", "Baghdad: al-Khwarizmi", "Al-Andalus, Arabic Spain", "Fibonacci, 1202", "Common in Europe, 1500s"]}
### Why place value wins
Roman numerals have no zero and no place value, so written sums are awkward. With Hindu-Arabic digits you can add, multiply and keep accounts on paper, which merchants and bankers loved.
ex: 1,888 in Roman numerals | MDCCCLXXXVIII | 13 symbols instead of 4
### The man behind the words
Al-Khwarizmi wrote a book explaining Indian reckoning around 825. Its Latin translation began "Algoritmi dicit…", "Al-Khwarizmi says…", and his name became **algorithm**. His other book, on "al-jabr" (restoring), gave us **algebra**.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "al-Khwarizmi", "b": "algorithm, algarismo"}, {"a": "al-jabr", "b": "algebra, álgebra"}, {"a": "sifr (empty)", "b": "zero, cifra"}]}
Change was slow. Some European cities, worried about fraud, banned the new digits in official accounts in the 1200s, because a 0 could easily be changed into a 6 or 9.
!? In Portuguese a digit is an **algarismo**, a word that comes straight from al-Khwarizmi's name.
> The numbers on every price tag travelled from India through Baghdad and al-Andalus before reaching Portugal.

# Agriculture and irrigation
Al-Andalus drew on farming know-how from across the Islamic world, from Persia to Egypt. Andalusi agronomists like Ibn al-Awwam wrote detailed manuals on grafting, soils and watering.

### Lifting water
The **nora** is a wheel with clay pots (alcatruzes) tied to it. Turned by a donkey or a mule, it lifts water from a well and tips it into channels (acéquias). Small dams (açudes) held river water back. Portuguese farmers used noras well into the 20th century.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Animal turns gears", "Pots dip into well", "Pots rise full", "Water tips into channel"]}
### Summer crops
Many new crops needed water in the hot, dry summer. Irrigation let farmers grow a second harvest, so the same field fed more people. That supported bigger towns like Córdoba and Seville.
!! The Arabs brought the sweet orange => They brought the bitter orange; the sweet orange arrived around 1500, partly via Portuguese traders, which is why Greeks call it "portokali"
!? Valencia's Water Tribunal, which settles irrigation disputes every Thursday by the cathedral door, is said to go back to Andalusi times.
> Irrigation turned dry southern Iberia into a garden that could grow crops all year.

# Arabic in Portuguese
After Latin, Arabic gave Portuguese more words than any other language in the Middle Ages, with estimates from several hundred to around a thousand. Most are **nouns** for things that were new or traded: food, farming, building, trade and government.
### Why so many start with "a"
"Al" means "the" in Arabic. Portuguese speakers heard it as part of the word. Before certain consonants Arabic changes al- to as-, az- or ar-, which is why we get açúcar and arroz.

Grammar, verbs and everyday basics like "pai", "água" and "comer" stayed Latin. Arabic added vocabulary, not structure. Even "fulano", meaning "so-and-so", comes from Arabic fulan.

# The Reconquista
The word **Reconquista** ("reconquest") was coined much later and is debated by historians, because it suggests a single planned campaign. In reality it was nearly 800 years of advances, truces, alliances and civil wars on both sides.

### How the frontier moved
When al-Andalus split into small rival kingdoms (taifas) after 1031, Christian kings forced them to pay tribute called parias. North African dynasties then came in twice to reunite the south and push back. The great Christian victory at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 broke Almohad power.

### Allies across faiths
El Cid, Castile's hero, also fought for the Muslim king of Zaragoza. Alfonso VI of Castile took a Muslim princess, Zaida of Seville, as a partner, and Muslim rulers often hired Christian soldiers.

> The Reconquista was a long, messy shifting of borders, not a single crusade.

# The County of Portucale
The name **Portucale** joined two places on the Douro: Portus (a port on the north bank, today's Porto) and Cale (on the south bank, near Gaia). In 868 a nobleman, Vímara Peres, took the area back from Muslim control and it became a county, a territory run by a count for the king of León.

### Henry and Teresa
Around 1096 King Alfonso VI of León and Castile gave the county to **Henry of Burgundy**, a French knight who had come to fight the Muslims. Henry married Teresa, Alfonso's daughter (born outside marriage). The county stretched roughly from the Minho to around Coimbra.

Henry died in 1112. Teresa ruled the county and called herself "queen". She grew close to a powerful Galician noble, Fernão Peres de Trava, which worried Portuguese barons who feared being swallowed by Galicia.

# São Mamede, 1128
On 24 June 1128, near Guimarães, the young **Afonso Henriques**, then about 17–20 years old, beat the forces of his mother Teresa and her ally Fernão Peres de Trava. Teresa left for Galicia, where she died two years later.

### Why it mattered
It was a modest battle, but it put power in the hands of a ruler backed by the local nobility. From then on Afonso pushed for independence from León and expansion south against the Muslims.

### The cradle
Guimarães was the county's main seat. Its castle and the Palace of the Dukes of Braganza sit on the "sacred hill", and a section of the old town wall bears the words "Aqui nasceu Portugal", "Portugal was born here".
• Afonso's exact birth year and place are uncertain; Guimarães, Viseu and Coimbra all claim him
• Tradition says he was baptised in the small church of São Miguel by the castle

> At São Mamede a son beat his mother, and a county started on the road to being a kingdom.

# Recognition: 1143 and 1179
Medieval kingdoms needed recognition. After victories such as **Ourique** (1139), Afonso began calling himself king. In 1143, at Zamora, his cousin Alfonso VII of León, who called himself "emperor of Spain", accepted Afonso's royal title, with a papal envoy present.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1139", "l": "Ourique victory"}, {"y": "1143", "l": "Treaty of Zamora"}, {"y": "1169", "l": "Defeat at Badajoz"}, {"y": "1179", "l": "Pope confirms king"}]}
### Going over León's head
Afonso then declared himself a vassal of the Pope and promised an annual tribute of gold. This was clever: owing loyalty directly to Rome meant not owing it to León. The Popes delayed for decades, but in 1179 Alexander III issued the bull **Manifestis Probatum**, calling Afonso king and his land a kingdom.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Pope: confirms 1179", "Afonso: vassal of Rome", "León: recognises 1143", "Barons: back Afonso"]}
### Why the Pope agreed
Afonso had proved useful: he had taken Lisbon and pushed far south against Muslim rulers, which the Church saw as a crusade. Money and military success made the case.
!? The Treaty of Zamora is traditionally dated 5 October 1143, the same day of the year the Portuguese Republic was proclaimed in 1910.
> Portugal became a kingdom in two steps: a neighbour's nod in 1143 and the Pope's seal in 1179.

# Moving south
Expansion was both conquest and settlement. Kings granted land and charters (forais) to attract settlers, and military orders such as the Templars, Hospitallers and Order of Santiago held frontier castles.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1147", "l": "Santarém and Lisbon"}, {"y": "1165", "l": "Évora taken"}, {"y": "1249", "l": "Faro, Algarve"}, {"y": "1297", "l": "Alcañices border"}]}
### Lisbon, 1147
Santarém fell to a surprise night attack in March 1147. That summer, a fleet of crusaders from England, Flanders and Germany, sailing to the Holy Land, stopped in Porto and agreed to help. After a siege of about 17 weeks, Lisbon surrendered in October. The crusaders were paid in plunder, and some stayed.
### Setbacks
In 1169 Afonso was wounded and captured at Badajoz, and the Almohads fought back hard in the 1190s. It took four more kings to reach the coast of the Algarve: Afonso III took Faro in 1249.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈17 weeks", "l": "siege of Lisbon, 1147"}, {"v": "≈160", "l": "crusader ships"}, {"v": "102 years", "l": "from Lisbon to Faro"}]}
### Fixed borders
In 1297 King Dinis and Castile signed the Treaty of Alcañices. Portugal gained the Riba-Côa area, and the land border has barely moved since, apart from the disputed town of Olivença, taken by Spain in 1801.
!? Portugal's land border with Spain is about 1,200 km long and one of the oldest stable borders in Europe.
> In about 150 years Portugal reached the south coast, and then kept the same shape for more than seven centuries.

# King Dinis
Dom Dinis came to the throne in 1279 at 17. With the reconquest finished, he could focus on building the country. He was nicknamed **o Lavrador**, "the Farmer", and also "the Poet King".
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Dinis's legacy", "items": ["Pine forest of Leiria", "University, 1290", "Portuguese in documents", "Order of Christ, 1319"]}
### Order of Christ
When the Pope dissolved the Templars in 1312, many kings seized their wealth. Dinis instead created a new order, the **Order of Christ**, in 1319, and handed it the Templars' castles and lands. A century later its money would help pay for Henry the Navigator's voyages, and its red cross flew on the sails.
### Land and sea
He set up fairs, protected farmers, rebuilt dozens of castles along the border and hired a Genoese admiral, Manuel Pessanha, in 1317 to organise a navy. The pine forest near Leiria held back moving sand dunes and later supplied timber for ships.

The House of Avis ruled from 1385 to 1580. With peace with Castile signed in 1411, Portugal's kings, short of land to conquer at home, looked overseas for gold, trade and crusading glory.

# Florence and patrons
Florence, a city of perhaps 40,000–60,000 people after the Black Death, grew rich on wool cloth and banking. Its gold coin, the **florin**, first struck in 1252, was trusted across Europe.

# Perspective
Medieval painters often made the most important figures biggest, not the nearest ones. Around 1415–1420, **Filippo Brunelleschi** showed how to make a painting match what the eye really sees.
### The experiment
He painted the Baptistery on a panel and drilled a small hole through it. Standing in the cathedral doorway, a viewer looked through the hole from the back at a mirror held in front. Lowering the mirror revealed the real building, lined up exactly with the painting.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Horizon line at eye level", "Vanishing point on horizon", "Parallel lines meet there", "Far things drawn smaller"]}
### Written down
In 1435 Leon Battista Alberti explained the method in his book On Painting, so any artist could learn it. Masaccio's Holy Trinity fresco in Florence, from about 1427, is one of the first surviving paintings to use it fully: a painted chapel seems to open into the wall.
= apparent size ∝ 1 ÷ distance
ex: A person 1.8 m tall at 10 m | the same person at 20 m | looks half as tall in the picture

> Perspective turned painting into a window, built with geometry.

# Humanism
The **studia humanitatis**, the "humanities", were five subjects: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy. Humanists wanted to read the classics in good original texts and write elegant Latin, and believed education made better citizens.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Find original texts", "Compare manuscripts", "Fix copying errors", "Teach and publish"]}
### Reading critically
Going back to sources, **ad fontes**, could be explosive. In 1440 Lorenzo Valla showed that the "Donation of Constantine", a document used to support the Pope's claim to land, was a forgery: its Latin was centuries too late.
### Erasmus
Erasmus of Rotterdam (about 1466–1536) was Europe's best-known scholar. He published a Greek New Testament in 1516 with a new Latin translation, and mocked corrupt clergy in his satire In Praise of Folly. He wanted reform, but never left the Catholic Church.

### A Portuguese humanist
Damião de Góis, a diplomat and chronicler, befriended Erasmus and stayed in his house in 1534. Back in Portugal he was later tried by the Inquisition and died soon after, in unclear circumstances.

> Humanism trusted that careful reading and good education could improve both people and society.

# Manueline style
Manuel I reigned from 1495 to 1521, when spices from India made Lisbon one of Europe's richest ports. The name "Manueline" was coined only in the 1800s, but the style is unmistakable: late-Gothic buildings covered in carving of the sea.

# Renaissance science
Artists measured bodies and light; navigators measured stars and coasts. That habit of careful observation fed directly into science.
### 1543: two books

**Andreas Vesalius**, a 28-year-old professor at Padua, published a huge illustrated anatomy, with woodcuts probably from Titian's workshop. Doctors had followed the Roman physician Galen, who mostly dissected animals. Vesalius showed many of his errors: the human jaw is one bone, not two, for example.
### Portugal's contribution

Neither Galen nor Ptolemy was thrown out overnight. But each time careful observation contradicted the ancients, trust slowly shifted from old books to evidence.

== hi-reform

# Luther's 95 theses
Martin Luther was an Augustinian friar and theology professor in Wittenberg, Germany. An **indulgence** was a Church document that reduced the time a soul would spend in purgatory. In 1517 the preacher Johann Tetzel was selling them nearby to help pay for rebuilding St Peter's in Rome.

### What Luther argued
His 95 points, in Latin, were meant for academic debate. He said forgiveness can't be bought, that the Pope had no power over purgatory, and that salvation comes through faith in God's grace.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Theses in Latin", "Printed in German", "All over Germany in weeks", "Pope condemns Luther", "Church splits"]}
### Printing made him
Within weeks, printers turned his theses and pamphlets into German, with woodcut cartoons for people who couldn't read well. Luther became the best-selling author of his time. At the Diet of Worms in 1521 he refused to take back his writings before Emperor Charles V and was declared an outlaw.
### Hidden in a castle
His prince, Frederick the Wise, had him "kidnapped" for his own safety and hidden in Wartburg Castle. There Luther translated the New Testament into German in about 11 weeks. His Bible helped shape the modern German language.

# Protestant and Catholic
The name **Protestant** comes from a formal protest by Lutheran princes in 1529. The movement soon split into several branches.

### The main differences

### The Catholic response
The **Council of Trent** (1545–1563) restated Catholic doctrine, tightened discipline and ordered seminaries to train priests properly. The Church also listed forbidden books from 1559 and used grand Baroque art and music to inspire the faithful.

# Wars of religion
In the 1500s most people believed a state needed one religion to hold together. When subjects and rulers disagreed, religion mixed with politics, dynasties and money, and war followed.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1555", "l": "Peace of Augsburg"}, {"y": "1562", "l": "French wars begin"}, {"y": "1572", "l": "St Bartholomew's Day"}, {"y": "1598", "l": "Edict of Nantes"}, {"y": "1618", "l": "Thirty Years' War"}]}
### France and the Netherlands
In France, Catholics and Protestant Huguenots fought eight wars. In the St Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, thousands of Protestants were killed. The Edict of Nantes in 1598 gave Huguenots limited tolerance. The Dutch fought Spain for 80 years, partly over religion, and won independence in 1648.
### The Thirty Years' War
It began in 1618 when Protestant nobles in Prague threw two Catholic officials out of a castle window. It grew into a European war involving Sweden, France, Spain and the Habsburg emperor; Catholic France even backed Protestants to weaken the Habsburgs.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "4–8 million", "l": "estimated dead"}, {"v": "1648", "l": "Peace of Westphalia"}, {"v": "30 years", "l": "1618 to 1648"}]}
Most victims died not in battle but from hunger, plague and armies living off the land.
### The peace
The Peace of Westphalia (1648) let rulers choose their state's religion and treated states as sovereign, a principle of international law still cited today.
!? Portugal's war to regain independence from Spain began in 1640, when Spain was stretched thin by the Thirty Years' War.
> Europe learnt through a century of slaughter that faith could not be imposed by force.

# The Inquisition in Portugal
In 1496–97 King Manuel I ordered Portugal's Jews to leave, then forced most of them to be baptised so they would stay. They became **New Christians**. Many were suspected of secretly practising Judaism, and in 1506 a mob in Lisbon killed perhaps 2,000 or more of them.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1497", "l": "Forced conversions"}, {"y": "1506", "l": "Lisbon massacre"}, {"y": "1536", "l": "Inquisition founded"}, {"y": "1773", "l": "Pombal ends labels"}, {"y": "1821", "l": "Inquisition abolished"}]}
### How it worked
King João III asked the Pope for an Inquisition, granted in 1536. Tribunals sat in Lisbon, Évora, Coimbra and, from 1560, Goa. Accusations were often anonymous; torture was used; property was confiscated. Sentences were read at public ceremonies called **autos-da-fé**.

### The diaspora
Many New Christian families fled, taking skills and trade networks with them.

The philosopher Spinoza was born in Amsterdam in 1632 to a Portuguese-Jewish family. Jesuit Father António Vieira, who criticised the Inquisition, was himself tried by it in the 1660s.
!? In Belmonte, in Beira Interior, a community kept Jewish practices in secret for more than 400 years, until it was revealed in the 20th century.
> Portugal's Inquisition drove out many of its most enterprising families, and other countries gained what Portugal lost.

# Copernicus
Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) was a Polish churchman, doctor and astronomer. The Greek Aristarchus had suggested a Sun-centred system around 250 BCE, but **Ptolemy**'s Earth-centred model ruled for about 1,400 years.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ptolemy", "items": ["Earth at centre", "complex epicycles"]}, "r": {"t": "Copernicus", "items": ["Sun near centre", "Earth spins daily"]}}
### Why it was attractive
Planets like Mars sometimes seem to move backwards across the sky for a few weeks. Ptolemy explained this with small circles on circles. Copernicus showed it happens naturally when the faster Earth overtakes Mars. His model also put the planets in a clear order with sizes of orbits that could be calculated.
### Why people hesitated
• His orbits were still perfect circles, so he still needed epicycles and was no more accurate than Ptolemy
• If Earth moved, the stars should shift slightly through the year; nobody could see that until 1838
• It clashed with everyday experience and with some Bible passages
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1543", "l": "De revolutionibus"}, {"y": "1609", "l": "Kepler's ellipses"}, {"y": "1610", "l": "Galileo's telescope"}, {"y": "1687", "l": "Newton explains why"}]}
ex: Earth's orbit is about 940 million km | ÷ 365 days × 24 h | ≈ 107,000 km/h, or about 30 km/s
!! The Church banned Copernicus at once => His book circulated freely for 73 years; it was put on the list of forbidden books only in 1616, "until corrected"
> Copernicus moved Earth from the centre, but it took others to prove he was right.

# Galileo's trial
In 1609 Galileo Galilei, a professor at Padua, heard of a Dutch spyglass and built his own, eventually magnifying about 20–30 times. Pointing it at the sky, he saw things no one had seen.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Moon", "b": "mountains and craters"}, {"a": "Jupiter", "b": "four moons orbit it"}, {"a": "Venus", "b": "full set of phases"}, {"a": "Milky Way", "b": "countless stars"}]}
### Why Venus mattered
In Ptolemy's model Venus could never appear full. Galileo saw it go through all its phases, like the Moon. That fitted Venus orbiting the Sun.
### The trial
In 1616 the Church warned Galileo not to hold or defend Copernicus' view. In 1632 he published a Dialogue in which the Earth-centred view was voiced by a character named Simplicio, which Pope Urban VIII, once his friend, took as mockery.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1610", "l": "Starry Messenger"}, {"y": "1616", "l": "Warned by Church"}, {"y": "1632", "l": "The Dialogue"}, {"y": "1633", "l": "Recants, house arrest"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "Church admits error"}]}
Aged 69, under threat of torture, he formally renounced the idea on 22 June 1633. He spent his last years under house arrest near Florence, where he wrote his great book on motion, smuggled out and printed in the Netherlands in 1638.
!! Galileo muttered "And yet it moves" as he left the court => The line first appears more than a century later and is almost certainly invented
> Galileo's trial became a symbol of authority clashing with evidence, though the evidence won in the end.

# The scientific method
For centuries, learning meant mastering authorities: Aristotle on nature, Galen on medicine. In the 1600s thinkers asked a sharper question: how do we actually know?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Francis Bacon", "items": ["collect observations", "experiment, induction"]}, "r": {"t": "René Descartes", "items": ["doubt everything", "reason, mathematics"]}}
### Bacon
The English lawyer Francis Bacon, in his Novum Organum (1620), urged people to gather facts systematically and test them with experiments. He warned of "idols", the habits that distort our thinking, such as seeing what we expect to see.
### Descartes
The French philosopher René Descartes, in his Discourse on the Method (1637), began by doubting everything he could, and rebuilt knowledge step by step on clear reasoning and mathematics.
### Putting it together
Modern science combines both: experiments to check ideas against nature, and maths to make precise predictions.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Observe", "Form a hypothesis", "Predict and test", "Revise or keep"]}
• A good theory makes predictions that could turn out wrong
• Results must be repeatable by others
• Measurement beats opinion, even an expert's
!? Bacon is said to have died in 1626 after catching a chill while stuffing a chicken with snow to see if cold preserved meat; the story may be embroidered.
> Science became a method, not a set of books: test ideas against nature and let the results decide.

# Newton's synthesis
Isaac Newton's **Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica** ("Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy") appeared in Latin in 1687. Edmond Halley, of comet fame, urged him to write it and paid for its printing.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "First law", "b": "no force, no change of motion"}, {"a": "Second law", "b": "force = mass × acceleration"}, {"a": "Third law", "b": "every push has a push back"}, {"a": "Gravitation", "b": "all masses attract"}]}
### The big idea
The same force that pulls an apple down keeps the Moon in orbit. Gravity weakens with the square of distance, and with that one rule Newton explained Kepler's elliptical orbits, the tides and the paths of comets.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = G m₁ m₂ ÷ r²", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "gravity force"}, {"s": "m₁, m₂", "m": "the two masses"}, {"s": "r", "m": "distance apart"}, {"s": "G", "m": "a universal constant"}]}
ex: The Moon is about 60 Earth radii away | gravity there is 60 × 60 = 3,600 times weaker | 9.8 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 0.0027 m/s², matching the Moon's orbit
### Afterwards
Using Newton's methods, Halley predicted that a comet would return in 1758. It did, after his death, and now bears his name. Newton also invented calculus, at about the same time as the German Leibniz, which led to a bitter fight over who came first.
!! Einstein proved Newton wrong => Einstein refined Newton for extreme speeds and gravity; Newton's laws still guide spacecraft
> Newton showed that the heavens and the Earth follow the same few mathematical rules.

# Scientific societies
Before journals, scientists shared work by private letters, slowly and unevenly. Societies made it public and regular.

### The Royal Society
Founded in London in 1660, with a royal charter from 1662, its motto is **Nullius in verba**, "take nobody's word for it". Members watched experiments performed in front of them, often by its curator, Robert Hooke. Newton was its president from 1703 until his death.
### Different models

### Journals
Philosophical Transactions, first published in 1665, is the world's oldest scientific journal still running. Publishing gave a clear date to discoveries, so priority could be settled and results checked by others.

# Instruments
Instruments did two things: they extended the senses, and they turned qualities into numbers. "Warm" became 25 °C; "heavy air" became a height of mercury.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Telescope, 1608", "b": "Dutch, then Galileo"}, {"a": "Microscope, 1590s", "b": "Hooke, Leeuwenhoek"}, {"a": "Barometer, 1643", "b": "Torricelli"}, {"a": "Pendulum clock, 1656", "b": "Huygens"}]}
### Seeing the small
Robert Hooke's Micrographia (1665) showed a flea in huge detail and used the word "cell" for the tiny boxes he saw in cork. The Dutch draper Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, with tiny single lenses, saw bacteria in 1676.
### Measuring air
Evangelista Torricelli filled a glass tube with mercury and turned it upside down in a dish: the column fell to about 76 cm. The weight of the air held the rest up. In 1648 Pascal's brother-in-law carried one up a mountain and the column was lower at the top.
ex: Mercury is 13.6 times denser than water | 0.76 m × 13.6 | a water barometer would need a tube about 10.3 m tall
### Keeping time

> New instruments turned guesses into measurements, and measurement became the language of science.

== hi-1755

# 1 November 1755
The first shock hit at about 9:40 in the morning. Lisbon was one of Europe's richest ports, with perhaps 200,000 people, and on a holy day most of them were at Mass. Stone churches packed with worshippers were among the deadliest places to be.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~9:40", "l": "First violent shaking"}, {"y": "+6 min", "l": "Shaking stops"}, {"y": "+40 min", "l": "Tsunami reaches the Tagus"}, {"y": "Days", "l": "Fires burn the city"}]}
### Minutes of chaos
Witnesses described three separate shocks lasting in total somewhere between about three and six minutes. Cracks several metres wide opened in the city centre. Roofs and vaults fell on the congregations, and narrow medieval streets filled with rubble, trapping people who ran outside.
• Many fled to the open riverside at Terreiro do Paço, the palace square
• The royal family survived because they were away at Belém, then outside the city
• King José I was so shaken that he reportedly refused to live under stone roofs again, and the court lived in tents and wooden buildings for years
### What was lost
The Ribeira Palace on the river collapsed with its royal library of tens of thousands of books, archives of the voyages of discovery and paintings by masters such as Titian and Rubens. A grand new opera house, opened only months before, was destroyed. The ruined Carmo Convent, still roofless above the Baixa, is the best place to picture the day.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ribeira Palace and royal library", "New opera house (Ópera do Tejo)", "Dozens of churches and convents", "Hospital of All Saints"]}
> The earthquake struck at the worst possible moment: a holy day, a full city and burning candles in every church.
!? The shaking was felt as far away as southern France and North Africa, and some lakes in Scandinavia were reported to slosh oddly that morning.

# Earthquake, tsunami, fire
Lisbon suffered three disasters in a row, each making the next worse. The epicentre lay in the Atlantic, probably a couple of hundred kilometres south-west of Cape St Vincent, where the African and Eurasian plates meet. Its exact location is still debated.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Undersea earthquake", "Buildings collapse", "Tsunami floods riverside", "Candles start fires", "Fires burn ~5 days"]}
### The wave
Around 40 minutes after the shaking, survivors who had gathered on the riverbank saw the Tagus draw back, exposing the riverbed and old wrecks. Then the sea rushed in. In Lisbon the waves reached about 5–6 metres; in parts of the Algarve, such as Lagos, they may have been higher still. The tsunami also hit Cádiz and Morocco and was recorded across the Atlantic in the Caribbean.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. arrival after quake, minutes", "items": [{"l": "Algarve coast", "v": 20.0, "t": "~15–20"}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 40.0, "t": "~40", "hi": true}, {"l": "Cornwall (UK)", "v": 270.0, "t": "~4–5 h"}]}
### The fire
Overturned candles, kitchen hearths and looters started fires that merged into a firestorm. It burned for about five or six days and destroyed much of what the shaking had left standing, including warehouses full of goods from Brazil and Asia.
• Estimates of the dead in Lisbon range from about 30,000 to 50,000 or more
• Thousands more died in the Algarve, Spain and Morocco
• Records were themselves destroyed, which is why figures remain uncertain
> Most victims died not in one event but in a chain: falling stone, then water, then fire.
!! Most people died in the tsunami => The collapse of buildings and the fires probably killed more people than the wave in Lisbon itself

# The Marquis of Pombal
Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo (1699–1782) was a former diplomat in London and Vienna who had become one of King José I's ministers in 1750. While others saw divine punishment, he treated the disaster as a practical problem. The famous phrase "bury the dead and feed the living" is attributed to him, though some historians credit it to another nobleman.
### The emergency plan
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bodies", "b": "sunk at sea to stop disease"}, {"a": "Looting", "b": "gallows put up, troops posted"}, {"a": "Food", "b": "price caps, tax waived on grain"}, {"a": "Rebuilding", "b": "no new building till a plan"}]}
Within days, troops ringed the city to stop people leaving and to catch looters. Food was brought in by sea, and its prices were fixed. Building was frozen until engineers could prepare a new plan, so that landowners could not simply rebuild the old maze.
### Power and its price
The crisis made Carvalho e Melo the most powerful man in Portugal for 22 years. He became Count of Oeiras in 1759 and Marquis of Pombal only in 1769, so the name we use came much later. He ruled with a hard hand: after an attempt on the king's life in 1758, members of the noble Távora family were executed publicly in Belém in 1759.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1750", "l": "Becomes minister"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Leads the response"}, {"y": "1769", "l": "Made Marquis of Pombal"}, {"y": "1777", "l": "Falls from power"}]}
When José I died in 1777, Queen Maria I dismissed him. Hundreds of political prisoners were released, and he was exiled to his estate at Pombal.
> Pombal was both a brilliant crisis manager and a ruthless autocrat, and Portugal remembers him as both.

# The new Baixa
The flattened lower town between Rossio and the river was not patched up but redesigned. The chief engineer, Manuel da Maia, then in his late seventies, drew up several options, from rebuilding as before to moving the capital. The chosen plan, drawn by Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel, wiped the slate clean.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before 1755", "items": ["narrow winding lanes", "mixed heights", "fire spread easily"]}, "r": {"t": "Pombaline Baixa", "items": ["straight wide grid", "uniform façades", "firewalls on roofs"]}}
### The gaiola pombalina
Inside the stone walls of each building stands a wooden "cage": a three-dimensional frame of posts and beams braced with diagonal crosses. Masonry holds it in place, but in an earthquake the timber flexes and keeps the building together instead of letting walls burst outwards. According to tradition, a model was tested by soldiers marching around it to imitate shaking.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Tiled roof with firewalls", "Floors on timber gaiola frame", "Ground floor of stone arches", "Pine piles in wet ground"]}
### An early prefab city
Windows, doors, iron railings and tiles came in a few standard sizes, made off-site and assembled quickly. Streets were named after trades: Rua do Ouro for goldsmiths, Rua da Prata for silversmiths, Rua dos Fanqueiros for cloth sellers. The palace square became Praça do Comércio, the "square of commerce", a sign that trade, not the court, would own the riverfront.
> The Baixa was one of the first city centres planned to resist earthquakes, built from standard parts on a rational grid.
!? The buildings stand on thousands of pine stakes driven into waterlogged ground. As long as they stay wet they barely rot, which is why lowering the water table is a real concern.

# The birth of seismology
Before 1755, earthquakes were mostly explained as acts of God or by vague ideas about underground winds. Pombal's government did something new: it asked the same set of questions everywhere and collected the answers. That is the basic method of modern science.
### The questionnaire
Through the Church, every parish priest in the country received a list of questions. They included:
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["What time did it begin?", "How long did it last?", "Where did it seem to come from?", "How much damage and death?", "Did the sea or wells change?"]}
Many of the answers survive in the national archive, the Torre do Tombo, in a collection known as the parish memories of 1758. Modern seismologists have used them to map how strongly each place shook, and from that to estimate the epicentre and size.
ex: Collect reports of damage per parish | Draw lines of equal intensity on a map | Strongest shaking points towards the source offshore
### From stories to science
• In 1756 the young Immanuel Kant wrote three essays seeking natural causes for the quake
• In 1760 the English geologist John Michell argued earthquakes were waves travelling through the ground, partly using Lisbon data
• Seismometers came only in the 19th century, but the idea of systematic observation started here
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Same questions everywhere", "Answers collected", "Intensity mapped", "Source estimated"]}
> By asking every parish the same questions, Portugal turned a catastrophe into data.

# Shaking European thought
Before 1755, a popular philosophy called optimism, linked to Leibniz and the poet Alexander Pope, held that we live in the best of all possible worlds and that every evil serves a larger good. Lisbon made that idea very hard to defend. Why would a good God destroy a pious city, on a holy day, while worshippers prayed?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Voltaire (1756)", "b": "poem: why the innocent?"}, {"a": "Rousseau (1756)", "b": "humans built the danger"}, {"a": "Kant (1756)", "b": "look for natural causes"}, {"a": "Malagrida", "b": "God punishing sinners"}]}
### Voltaire against optimism
In his "Poem on the Lisbon Disaster" Voltaire asked whether Lisbon really had more vices than London or Paris, which still stood. In Candide (1759) he satirised optimism: his naïve heroes arrive in Lisbon during the earthquake, then watch the Inquisition burn people to prevent further quakes, while their tutor Pangloss insists all is for the best.
### Rousseau's reply
Rousseau wrote to Voltaire that nature had not built 20,000 houses of six or seven storeys in one place. If people had lived more spread out, and had not stayed behind to save their belongings, far fewer would have died. It is an early argument that "natural" disasters are partly made by human choices.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old view", "items": ["God's punishment", "pray and repent"]}, "r": {"t": "New view", "items": ["natural event", "plan and prepare"]}}
The Jesuit preacher Gabriel Malagrida insisted the quake was divine punishment and blamed sinners. Pombal, who favoured practical measures, saw this as a threat; Malagrida was later burned by the Inquisition in 1761.
> Lisbon 1755 pushed European thinkers from asking why God allows disasters to asking how humans can reduce them.

# The Enlightenment
The Enlightenment was a movement of ideas across 18th-century Europe and its colonies. Its core belief was that human reason, not tradition or religious authority alone, should guide how we understand nature and organise society. Kant summed it up in 1784 with the motto "dare to know".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Locke", "b": "natural rights, consent"}, {"a": "Montesquieu", "b": "separation of powers"}, {"a": "Voltaire", "b": "religious tolerance"}, {"a": "Rousseau", "b": "the social contract"}]}
### Big ideas
• Government gets its legitimacy from the people, not from God
• Power should be split between those who make, apply and judge laws
• Torture and cruel punishments are irrational (Beccaria, 1764)
• Free trade and markets can create wealth (Adam Smith, 1776)
These ideas spread through books, newspapers, scientific academies, salons and coffee houses, where people of different ranks could debate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Scientific revolution", "Reason applied to society", "Books, salons, coffee houses", "Revolutions after 1776"]}
### Portugal's version
Portuguese reformers, often called "estrangeirados" because they had lived abroad, brought these ideas home. Luís António Verney's book on "the true method of studying" (1746) attacked old-fashioned teaching. Pombal, like Frederick the Great of Prussia or Catherine the Great of Russia, was an **enlightened despot**: he used reason to modernise, but kept absolute power.
> The Enlightenment taught that the world can be understood and society can be improved by reason, an idea behind modern science and democracy.
!! Enlightenment thinkers all wanted democracy => Many, like Voltaire, preferred a wise king to rule by the people

# Pombal's reforms
Pombal wanted a stronger, richer state that depended less on Britain and on the nobility and the Church. His reforms touched trade, schools, religion and law, and many still shape Portugal today.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1756", "l": "Douro wine company"}, {"y": "1759", "l": "Jesuits expelled"}, {"y": "1772", "l": "Coimbra University reform"}, {"y": "1773", "l": "New Christian label ended"}]}
### Port wine and the Douro
British merchants dominated the port trade, and quality was falling as cheap wine was mixed in. In 1756 Pombal created a royal company to control production and marked the boundaries of the Douro vineyards with granite posts, many still standing. It is one of the world's oldest regulated wine regions, alongside earlier efforts in Tokaj and Chianti.
### Church and education
The Jesuits ran most schools and had great influence at court. Pombal accused them of plotting and expelled them from Portugal and the empire in 1759, before France and Spain did the same. The state then had to build its own school system. The University of Coimbra was reformed in 1772, gaining mathematics and natural science faculties, a botanical garden, an observatory and laboratories.
### Society
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Old/New Christian distinction ended", "Slave imports to mainland banned", "School of commerce founded (1759)", "State censorship board (1768)"]}
New Christians, descendants of Jews forced to convert in 1497, had faced discrimination for centuries; in 1773 the legal distinction was abolished. In 1761 Pombal banned bringing enslaved people into mainland Portugal, though slavery continued in Brazil and Africa.
> Pombal modernised Portugal from the top down, with reforms that were bold, lasting and imposed without consent.
!? The granite boundary markers of 1758–61 are known as "marcos pombalinos", and over a hundred survive in the Douro vineyards.

# The Encyclopédie
It began as a plan to translate an English encyclopaedia and grew into one of the great publishing projects in history. Denis Diderot and the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert edited it, with well over a hundred contributors, including Voltaire, Rousseau and Montesquieu.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "28", "l": "volumes, text and plates"}, {"v": "~72,000", "l": "articles"}, {"v": "21 years", "l": "from 1751 to 1772"}]}
### Why it was radical
The Encyclopédie organised knowledge by human reason, following Francis Bacon's division into memory, reason and imagination, rather than putting theology at the top. Cross-references sometimes led readers from a pious article to a critical one, a clever way past the censors.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Human knowledge", "items": ["Memory: history", "Reason: philosophy, science", "Imagination: arts"]}
### Respect for crafts
Eleven volumes were plates: detailed engravings of looms, forges, glassworks, printing presses and mines. Treating the work of artisans as real knowledge, worth studying alongside philosophy, was itself an Enlightenment statement.
### Banned and unstoppable
• Its licence was revoked in 1759 and the Pope condemned it
• Diderot carried on in secret; the printer even cut passages behind his back
• Cheaper editions in Switzerland and Italy spread it across Europe, including to readers in Lisbon
> The Encyclopédie aimed to put all useful knowledge in reach of any educated reader, and in doing so challenged who decides what is true.
!? One contributor, the Chevalier de Jaucourt, wrote about 17,000 articles, roughly a quarter of the whole work.

# Lessons for today
Lisbon sits in a seismically active region. Large earthquakes can come from offshore faults along the boundary between the African and Eurasian plates, as in 1755, or from faults closer by in the lower Tagus valley. The question is not if another strong earthquake will come, but when.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1531", "l": "Lisbon quake, Tagus valley"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Great offshore quake"}, {"y": "1909", "l": "Benavente quake"}, {"y": "1969", "l": "Offshore quake, M ~7.9"}]}
### What modern science adds
Seismologists of IPMA, the national weather and earthquake institute, monitor the ground constantly. If a large quake strikes offshore, a tsunami could reach the Algarve in perhaps 15–20 minutes and Lisbon in about half an hour or more, so people must act on the shaking itself rather than wait for an official alert.
### Building codes
Portugal introduced its first seismic building rules in 1958, and today follows the European standard Eurocode 8. Buildings designed to these rules are meant to bend without collapsing. The weak spots are older buildings, including Pombaline ones whose wooden cages were cut away to open shops.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Higher risk", "items": ["pre-1958 masonry", "gaiola walls removed", "soft ground near river"]}, "r": {"t": "Lower risk", "items": ["modern code design", "intact gaiola", "bedrock foundations"]}}
### What you can do
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Drop to the ground", "Cover under a sturdy table", "Hold on until it stops", "Coast: walk to high ground"]}
Portugal runs a national drill, "A Terra Treme", every autumn, in which schools and offices practise these steps for one minute.
> The best tribute to 1755 is preparation: strong buildings, fast warnings and people who know what to do.
!! Earthquakes can be predicted days ahead => No method can predict the time of an earthquake; we can only map risk and prepare

== hi-revolutions

# The American Revolution
After the Seven Years' War (1756–63), Britain was deep in debt and decided the American colonies should help pay for their own defence. Colonists objected not so much to the size of the taxes as to the principle: they had no members in the British Parliament that voted them.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1765", "l": "Stamp Act protests"}, {"y": "1773", "l": "Boston Tea Party"}, {"y": "1776", "l": "Declaration of Independence"}, {"y": "1783", "l": "Britain recognises USA"}]}
### From protest to war
Fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. On 4 July 1776 the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson. It argued, in the language of Locke, that people have rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness, and may replace a government that destroys them.
### Why the colonists won
• France joined in 1778 after the American victory at Saratoga, followed by Spain
• The British had to fight across a huge territory, 5,000 km from home
• The French fleet helped trap the British army at Yorktown in 1781
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["No vote in Parliament", "Protests and boycotts", "War from 1775", "French help, victory 1781"]}
### The contradiction
About one in five people in the colonies was enslaved. Jefferson himself enslaved hundreds of people over his lifetime. The equality the Declaration promised did not reach enslaved Africans, Native Americans or women; Black Americans would wait until the 1860s for abolition and the 1960s for full civil rights.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Promised", "items": ["all men equal", "consent of governed"]}, "r": {"t": "Reality", "items": ["slavery kept", "no vote for women"]}}
> The American Revolution put the idea that government rests on the consent of the governed into practice for the first time on a large scale.
!? France's spending on the American war worsened its own debt crisis, helping to trigger the French Revolution a few years later.

# The French Revolution
France in 1789 was rich but its government was broke. Wars, including support for the Americans, had piled up debt, and interest payments swallowed a huge share of the budget. The nobles and clergy largely escaped taxation, so the king needed their consent for reform, and they refused.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["King: absolute power", "First Estate: clergy", "Second Estate: nobles", "Third Estate: ~97% of people"]}
### Bread and anger
Bad harvests in 1788 pushed bread prices to record levels. A labourer could spend more than half his daily wage on bread alone. Hunger in the cities and resentment of feudal dues in the countryside made people ready to act.
### How it unfolded
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "May 1789", "l": "Estates-General meets"}, {"y": "Jun 1789", "l": "National Assembly"}, {"y": "Jul 1789", "l": "Bastille stormed"}, {"y": "Aug 1789", "l": "Privileges abolished"}]}
Louis XVI called the Estates-General for the first time since 1614. When the Third Estate was outvoted, its deputies declared themselves the National Assembly and swore not to separate until France had a constitution. On 14 July, Parisians stormed the Bastille, a fortress-prison and symbol of royal power, to seize gunpowder.
• On the night of 4 August, the Assembly abolished feudal privileges
• Church lands were nationalised to pay the debt
• The monarchy was abolished in 1792, and Louis XVI executed in January 1793
> The French Revolution replaced a society of inherited ranks with the idea of equal citizens.
!! The Bastille was full of political prisoners => It held only seven prisoners on 14 July 1789; its capture mattered as a symbol

# Liberty, equality, fraternity
Adopted on 26 August 1789, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen has 17 short articles. Its first states that men are born and remain free and equal in rights. It drew on Enlightenment thinkers and on the American example; Lafayette, who had fought in America, helped draft it.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Equality before the law", "Freedom of speech and religion", "Sovereignty lies with the nation", "Innocent until proven guilty", "Taxes shared by ability to pay"]}
### Who was left out?
The "rights of man" did not, in practice, include women. In 1791 the playwright Olympe de Gouges answered with a Declaration of the Rights of Woman; she was guillotined in 1793. France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794, after a slave uprising in Saint-Domingue, but Napoleon restored it in 1802. Saint-Domingue fought on and became independent Haiti in 1804.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Included", "items": ["male citizens", "property protected"]}, "r": {"t": "Left out", "items": ["women", "enslaved people"]}}
### How the ideas travelled
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["1789 Declaration", "Napoleon's conquests", "Liberal constitutions", "1948 UN Declaration"]}
Portugal's first constitution, in 1822, borrowed directly from this language. The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the European Convention on Human Rights carry the same core ideas.
> The Declaration of 1789 turned privileges for some into rights for all, at least on paper, and set a standard people could demand.
!? The motto "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity" was used during the Revolution but became France's official motto only in the late 19th century.

# The Terror
By 1793 France was at war with much of Europe, facing a royalist uprising in the Vendée in the west, and short of food. The radical Jacobins argued that the Revolution could survive only by crushing its enemies at home. A Committee of Public Safety, increasingly led by Maximilien Robespierre, took emergency powers.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Foreign war and revolt", "Fear of traitors", "Law of Suspects", "Mass executions"]}
### How it worked
The Law of Suspects (September 1793) allowed the arrest of almost anyone suspected of disloyalty. Trials became quick and the defence limited. Victims included Queen Marie Antoinette, the moderate Girondins, Olympe de Gouges, the chemist Lavoisier and finally revolutionaries like Danton.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~17,000", "l": "official death sentences"}, {"v": "~300,000", "l": "people arrested"}, {"v": "11 months", "l": "Sep 1793 to Jul 1794"}]}
Many more died in prison or were killed without trial, and the repression of the Vendée cost tens of thousands of lives, perhaps far more; the totals are disputed.
### The end
On 27 July 1794 (9 Thermidor in the revolutionary calendar), fellow deputies afraid they would be next turned on Robespierre. He was guillotined the following day.
• The word "terrorism" comes from this period
• The guillotine was meant to be humane and equal: one quick death for all classes
> The Terror showed how a revolution for rights can turn on its own people when fear rules.
!! Most victims were nobles => Most were ordinary people: peasants, workers and townspeople, not aristocrats

# Napoleon
Born in Corsica in 1769, Napoleon Bonaparte rose fast in the revolutionary army, which promoted talent rather than birth. Victories in Italy made him a hero. In November 1799 he took power in a coup, and in 1804 he crowned himself emperor in Notre-Dame, taking the crown from the Pope's hands.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1799", "l": "Coup: First Consul"}, {"y": "1804", "l": "Emperor; Civil Code"}, {"y": "1812", "l": "Disaster in Russia"}, {"y": "1815", "l": "Defeat at Waterloo"}]}
### The Napoleonic Code
The Civil Code of 1804 replaced hundreds of local laws with one clear, written system. It guaranteed equality before the law, secure property and civil marriage, and ended feudal rights. But it also made wives legally subordinate to their husbands.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Modern", "items": ["equality before law", "one written code", "civil marriage"]}, "r": {"t": "Conservative", "items": ["wives under husbands", "strong central state"]}}
Portugal's first Civil Code of 1867 drew on it, as did codes in Spain, Italy, Belgium and Latin America. The metric system also spread with French armies.
### Rise and fall
• At its peak around 1810–12, Napoleon controlled or dominated most of continental Europe
• About 600,000 soldiers invaded Russia in 1812; only a fraction came back
• Defeated at Leipzig (1813) and exiled to Elba, he returned for 100 days before Waterloo
He died in exile on Saint Helena in 1821.
> Napoleon spread the Revolution's legal ideas across Europe while destroying its democracy at home.
!! Napoleon was unusually short => He was about 1.69 m, average for his time; British cartoons and old French measures created the myth

# The French invasions of Portugal
In 1806 Napoleon ordered a Continental Blockade: no European country should trade with Britain. Portugal, Britain's oldest ally and dependent on British trade, could not comply. In late 1807 a French army under General Junot marched through Spain towards Lisbon.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1807", "l": "Junot takes Lisbon"}, {"y": "1809", "l": "Soult takes Porto"}, {"y": "1810", "l": "Masséna invades"}, {"y": "1811", "l": "French retreat"}]}
### Three invasions
• 1807–08: Junot occupied Lisbon; British troops landed and won at Vimeiro
• 1809: Soult invaded the north; as Porto fell, a pontoon bridge over the Douro collapsed under fleeing crowds and many drowned
• 1810–11: Masséna was checked at Buçaco, then stopped by the Lines of Torres Vedras
### The Lines of Torres Vedras
Wellington had thousands of Portuguese labourers secretly build three lines of forts, redoubts and blocked valleys across the peninsula north of Lisbon. When Masséna arrived in October 1810, he found them impossible to break.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Secret forts built", "Land emptied of food", "French army starves", "Retreat in March 1811"]}
The countryside in front of the lines had been stripped of food and people. The French waited for months, hungry, and then retreated, losing many men. The scorched-earth tactic saved Lisbon but caused great suffering for Portuguese civilians.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "3", "l": "defensive lines"}, {"v": "~150", "l": "forts and redoubts"}, {"v": "~85 km", "l": "total length (approx.)"}]}
> Portugal survived Napoleon through British alliance, a fortified line and the endurance of its people, at a heavy civilian cost.
!? The phrase "ficar a ver navios" (to be left watching ships) is popularly linked to Junot arriving in Lisbon just as the royal fleet sailed away.

# The court flees to Brazil
With Junot's army days from Lisbon, Prince Regent João, who ruled for his mentally ill mother Queen Maria I, made a radical choice: rather than be captured, he moved the whole government across the Atlantic. The fleet left on 29 November 1807, escorted by the British navy; the French entered Lisbon the next day.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~10,000+", "l": "people on board (est.)"}, {"v": "~2 months", "l": "voyage to Brazil"}, {"v": "13 years", "l": "court stays in Rio"}]}
### Rio transformed
The court arrived in Salvador in January 1808 and settled in Rio de Janeiro in March. A colonial town suddenly hosted ministries, courts and a royal household.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ports opened to friendly nations", "Bank of Brazil founded", "First printing press allowed", "Botanical garden, royal library"]}
### A colony becomes a kingdom
In 1815 Brazil was raised to a kingdom equal with Portugal: the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves. For Brazilians this was a huge step up. For many in Portugal it felt like the opposite: the mother country governed from its colony, with a British marshal, Beresford, running the army at home.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Brazil gained", "items": ["capital status", "trade and institutions"]}, "r": {"t": "Portugal lost", "items": ["court and money", "control of trade"]}}
> By moving the court to Rio, Portugal saved its monarchy but set Brazil on the road to independence.
!? Rio was the only European capital ever transferred to a colony, and Maria I became the first reigning European monarch to set foot in the Americas.

# Brazil's independence
The Liberal Revolution of 1820 in Portugal demanded the king's return, and João VI sailed back to Lisbon in 1821. He left his son Pedro as regent in Rio. The new Portuguese parliament then tried to reduce Brazil to colonial status again, which angered Brazil's elites.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1821", "l": "João VI returns to Lisbon"}, {"y": "Jan 1822", "l": "Pedro refuses to leave"}, {"y": "Sep 1822", "l": "Cry of Ipiranga"}, {"y": "1825", "l": "Portugal recognises Brazil"}]}
### "Independence or death"
When Lisbon ordered Pedro home, he refused in January 1822 (the "Dia do Fico", "I'm staying" day). On 7 September 1822, on the banks of the Ipiranga stream near São Paulo, he received new orders from Lisbon and declared independence. He was crowned Emperor Pedro I in December.
### Different from Spanish America
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Brazil", "items": ["one country", "a monarchy", "fairly little war"]}, "r": {"t": "Spanish America", "items": ["many republics", "long wars"]}}
Because a member of the royal family led independence, Brazil kept its unity and a monarchy until 1889. Slavery also continued, which suited the landowning elite.
### One man, two crowns
In 1826 Pedro inherited the Portuguese throne as Pedro IV. He gave Portugal a constitutional charter, then abdicated in favour of his young daughter, Maria II, which sparked conflict with his brother Miguel.
> Brazil's independence was led by the heir to Portugal's own throne, which made it unusually peaceful and kept the country united.
!? Portugal recognised Brazil's independence in 1825 only after Brazil agreed to pay compensation, largely by taking on a Portuguese debt owed to Britain.

# The Liberal Revolution
In 1820 Portugal was in a strange state: the king lived in Brazil, the army was run by a British marshal, and trade had collapsed. On 24 August 1820, military officers and merchants in Porto rose up and called for a parliament (Cortes) and a written constitution.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1820", "l": "Porto revolt"}, {"y": "1822", "l": "First constitution"}, {"y": "1828", "l": "Miguel seizes throne"}, {"y": "1834", "l": "Liberals win"}]}
### Two brothers, two visions
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Liberals: Pedro", "items": ["constitution", "parliament", "Maria II as queen"]}, "r": {"t": "Absolutists: Miguel", "items": ["king rules alone", "Church and nobles"]}}
Miguel, backed by the Church, most nobles and much of the countryside, was acclaimed absolute king in 1828. Pedro gave up the Brazilian crown in 1831 and came to fight for his daughter's rights.
### The siege of Porto
Pedro landed near Porto in July 1832 with about 7,500 men. Miguel's much larger army besieged the city for about a year. Porto held out through hunger, cholera and bombardment, which is why it is still called "a Cidade Invicta", the unconquered city. Liberal victories at sea and on land ended the war with the agreement of Évora Monte in 1834.
• Miguel went into exile
• Religious orders were dissolved and their property sold
• Pedro died in 1834, only months after victory
> The civil war decided that Portugal would be a constitutional monarchy, not an absolute one.
!? Pedro left his heart to Porto in gratitude. It is kept in the Lapa church in the city.

# Revolutions in Latin America
In 1808 Napoleon forced the Spanish king to abdicate and put his brother Joseph on the throne. In the colonies, local elites asked who now had authority. Many formed their own governing councils in 1810, at first claiming loyalty to the captive king. They soon moved towards independence.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Napoleon takes Spain", "Local juntas in 1810", "Wars of independence", "New republics by 1825"]}
### The creole elite
Leadership came mostly from creoles, people of Spanish descent born in America, who resented that the top posts went to Spaniards. They read Enlightenment books and knew the American and French examples. Enslaved people, Indigenous peoples and mixed-race soldiers did much of the fighting, often with promises that were only partly kept.
### Leaders
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Simón Bolívar", "b": "Venezuela to Peru, Bolivia"}, {"a": "José de San Martín", "b": "Argentina, Chile, Peru"}, {"a": "Miguel Hidalgo", "b": "started Mexico's revolt, 1810"}, {"a": "Toussaint Louverture", "b": "Haiti's revolution leader"}]}
San Martín led an army across the Andes in 1817, one of the boldest marches in military history. The battle of Ayacucho in Peru (1824) effectively ended Spanish rule on the mainland.
### What came after
• Bolívar dreamed of a united Spanish America, but Gran Colombia split in 1830
• Many new republics suffered decades of civil wars and strongmen
• Cuba and Puerto Rico stayed Spanish until 1898
> Napoleon's invasion of Spain opened the door, but Latin Americans themselves fought for and won their independence.
!? Bolivia is named after Simón Bolívar, who was its first president.

# Railways
The railway combined two older ideas: wagons on rails, used in mines for centuries, and the steam engine. In 1825 George Stephenson's locomotive pulled the first public steam train on the Stockton and Darlington line in north-east England. In 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester Railway became the first intercity line run entirely by steam.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical speed, km/h (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Walking", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Stagecoach", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Train, 1850s", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}]}
### What railways changed
• Fresh food, coal and post moved fast and cheaply, so prices fell
• Towns along the line grew; towns bypassed often declined
• Ordinary people could travel for work or leisure, inventing the day trip and the seaside holiday
• Clocks were standardised: Britain adopted "railway time" in the 1840s so timetables made sense
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Steam engine on rails", "Cheap fast transport", "Wider markets", "Industry and cities grow"]}
### Portugal gets on board
Portugal came later. Under the "Regeneração" governments, minister Fontes Pereira de Melo pushed roads and railways to modernise the country. The first line, about 36 km from Lisbon to Carregado, opened on 28 October 1856. By 1864 trains linked Lisbon with Porto (at Gaia), and in 1877 Gustave Eiffel's company finished the Maria Pia bridge across the Douro so trains could enter Porto.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1825", "l": "Stockton–Darlington"}, {"y": "1830", "l": "Liverpool–Manchester"}, {"y": "1856", "l": "Lisbon–Carregado"}, {"y": "1877", "l": "Maria Pia bridge, Porto"}]}
> Railways shrank distances, created national markets and made time itself uniform.
!? Portugal and Spain use a wider track gauge (about 1.67 m) than most of Europe (1.435 m), one reason Iberian trains long had to stop or change axles at the French border.

# Steamships and telegraph
Sailing ships depended on the wind and could be stuck for days in calm seas. Steamships kept schedules. From the 1840s regular steam lines crossed the Atlantic in about two weeks, and iron hulls and the screw propeller made ships bigger and faster through the century.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sail", "items": ["at the wind's mercy", "weeks, uncertain"]}, "r": {"t": "Steam", "items": ["fixed timetables", "needs coal stations"]}}
### Coal and islands
Steamers needed coal every few thousand kilometres. Atlantic islands became filling stations: Madeira, and above all Mindelo on São Vicente in Cape Verde, grew busy supplying British ships on their way to South America and Africa.
### The Victorian internet
The electric telegraph sent coded pulses along a wire. Samuel Morse's code and his first long line in the USA (1844) showed it worked. Undersea cables followed. A transatlantic cable of 1858 failed within weeks; in 1866 the giant ship Great Eastern laid one that lasted.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "London to New York message time", "items": [{"l": "By sailing ship", "v": 30.0, "t": "~4–6 weeks"}, {"l": "By steamship", "v": 10.0, "t": "~10 days"}, {"l": "By cable, 1866", "v": 0.2, "t": "minutes", "hi": true}]}
### Portugal as a cable hub
In 1870 a cable station at Carcavelos, near Lisbon, linked Britain with Gibraltar, Malta and India. Later, Horta on the island of Faial in the Azores became a crossroads of transatlantic cables, with operators from several countries living there.
> Steam and the telegraph separated information from transport: news could now travel far faster than people or goods.
!? Governments and newspapers could now learn of wars and market prices in minutes, and stock exchanges in London and New York began to move together.

# Cities and factories
Factories needed workers in one place, near coal, water and transport. Farm workers, pushed by poverty and pulled by wages, moved in. By 1851 Britain became the first country where more people lived in towns than in the countryside. Manchester grew from a market town into the world's first great industrial city.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Factories need workers", "People move to cities", "Crowded, dirty housing", "Protests and reforms"]}
### Life in the industrial city
• Working days of 12–14 hours, six days a week, were normal
• Children worked in mills and mines, crawling under machines and pulling coal carts
• Whole families lived in single rooms, sharing outdoor toilets
• Cholera spread through dirty water; in 1854 John Snow traced a London outbreak to one water pump
### How things improved
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1833", "l": "UK Factory Act limits"}, {"y": "1842", "l": "No women, kids in mines"}, {"y": "1847", "l": "UK 10-hour day law"}, {"y": "1871", "l": "UK unions legal"}]}
Reforms came from campaigners, doctors, journalists and novelists like Charles Dickens, and from workers organising unions and strikes. Sewers, clean water and building rules eventually made cities healthier.
### Portugal's slower path
Portugal industrialised late and on a smaller scale. Textile mills grew in Porto, the Ave valley and Covilhã, where wool was spun from mountain sheep, and Lisbon had tobacco, cork and metal works. Portugal set an eight-hour working day for industry in 1919.
> Industry made more goods than ever before, but it took decades of struggle to make industrial cities fit to live in.

# New ideologies
Industry, cities and the French Revolution raised new questions. Who should rule? What holds a nation together? Why are workers poor in such a rich society? The 19th century answered with "-isms" that still shape politics today.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Liberalism", "b": "rights, constitutions, markets"}, {"a": "Nationalism", "b": "each nation its own state"}, {"a": "Socialism", "b": "share wealth, protect workers"}, {"a": "Conservatism", "b": "tradition, slow change"}]}
### 1848: the springtime of peoples
In 1848 revolutions broke out in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Budapest and elsewhere. Liberals wanted constitutions, nationalists wanted their own states, and workers wanted jobs and votes. Most uprisings were crushed within a year, but France gained universal male suffrage and serfdom ended in the Austrian Empire.
### Marx and Marxism
In the same year Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published the Communist Manifesto. Marx argued that history is a struggle between classes, and that capitalism would be overthrown by the workers it exploited.

### In Portugal
• The Portuguese Socialist Party was founded in 1875
• The Republican Party (1876) mixed liberal, nationalist and anticlerical ideas
• Republicanism won in 1910, when the monarchy fell

> The big political families of today, from liberal to socialist and nationalist, were born in the 19th century.
!? The terms "left" and "right" come from the French Revolution, when supporters of change sat on the left of the assembly's president.

# Abolition of slavery
Between the 1500s and the 1860s, about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships crossing the Atlantic, and about 10.7 million survived the voyage. Ships under the Portuguese and Brazilian flags carried the largest share, close to half of them, mostly to Brazil.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~12.5 m", "l": "Africans embarked"}, {"v": "~10.7 m", "l": "survived the crossing"}, {"v": "~45%", "l": "on Portuguese/Brazilian ships"}]}
### Why abolition happened
Enlightenment and religious arguments, especially from Quakers and evangelicals like William Wilberforce, slowly turned opinion. Enslaved people resisted constantly; the Haitian revolution and major revolts in Jamaica raised the cost of slavery. Economic changes mattered too, though historians debate how much.
### Portugal's slow steps

After 1869, people were renamed "libertos" but still had to work for their former owners until 1878. Even after that, forced labour schemes in the African colonies continued well into the 20th century, until the early 1960s.
### Compensation for whom?
When Britain ended slavery in 1833, it paid £20 million to slave owners, about 40% of the government's yearly budget. Enslaved people received nothing and were made to work as unpaid "apprentices" for several more years.

# The Scramble for Africa
In 1870 Europeans controlled only about a tenth of Africa, mostly along the coasts. Portugal held old trading footholds in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea. Within 40 years almost the whole continent was divided up.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of Africa under European rule (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1870", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "1914", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}]}
### The Berlin Conference
Germany's chancellor Bismarck hosted European powers and the USA in Berlin from November 1884 to February 1885. No Africans were invited. The conference set the rule of "effective occupation": to claim territory, a power had to actually control it, which triggered a rush to send soldiers, officials and treaties inland.
### Why it was possible now
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Quinine against malaria", "Steamboats up the rivers", "Machine guns and rifles", "Railways and telegraph"]}
### The consequences
• Borders often ignored existing peoples and kingdoms; many were drawn as straight lines on maps
• Colonies were run to extract rubber, minerals, cotton and labour
• In King Leopold II's Congo Free State, forced labour for rubber caused mass deaths, probably in the millions
• Only Ethiopia, which defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896, and Liberia remained independent
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Berlin rules (1884–85)", "Race to occupy", "Borders drawn in Europe", "Colonial rule to 1960s"]}
> The Scramble for Africa was decided around European tables, and its borders still frame African states today.
!? Most of today's African borders were set in this period; after independence, African states agreed in 1964 to keep them to avoid endless wars.

# The Pink Map and the Ultimatum
Portugal had coastal colonies on both sides of southern Africa: Angola on the Atlantic and Mozambique on the Indian Ocean. Explorers such as Serpa Pinto, and Capelo and Ivens, crossed the continent in the 1870s and 1880s. In 1886 Portugal published a map showing a belt of pink linking the two: the "Mapa Cor-de-Rosa".

### The collision
The two plans crossed in the lands that are now Zambia, Zimbabwe and Malawi. British businessman Cecil Rhodes wanted that territory, and the British government backed him. On 11 January 1890 Britain sent Portugal an ultimatum: withdraw its forces or face the Royal Navy. Portugal, with no hope of winning, gave in.

### A national humiliation
Britain was Portugal's oldest ally, so the shock was enormous. Crowds protested, British goods were boycotted and writers like Guerra Junqueiro attacked King Carlos. Many blamed the monarchy for weakness. In 1891 republicans in Porto attempted a revolt, which failed.

> The 1890 Ultimatum turned colonial frustration into anger at the monarchy and helped bring the republic two decades later.

# Science and medicine
In 1800, surgery was done without anaesthesia, nobody knew that germs cause disease, and most people believed species had been created unchanged. By 1900 all three ideas had been overturned.
### Evolution
In On the Origin of Species (1859), Charles Darwin argued that living things change over generations by natural selection. Alfred Russel Wallace reached the same idea independently. Gregor Mendel's pea experiments (1866) showed how traits are inherited, though they were ignored until 1900.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Offspring vary", "Some suit the place better", "They leave more young", "Traits spread over time"]}
### Germs and medicine
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1846", "l": "Ether anaesthesia"}, {"y": "1867", "l": "Lister's antiseptics"}, {"y": "1882", "l": "Koch finds TB germ"}, {"y": "1885", "l": "Pasteur's rabies vaccine"}]}
Louis Pasteur showed that microbes cause fermentation and decay, and that heating kills them, the origin of pasteurised milk. Robert Koch identified the bacteria behind anthrax, tuberculosis and cholera. Joseph Lister used carbolic acid to clean wounds and instruments, and deaths after surgery fell sharply.
!? In the 1840s Ignaz Semmelweis made doctors in his Vienna clinic wash their hands, and deaths of new mothers fell from around 10% to under 2%. Colleagues mocked him.
### Portugal's part
In 1899 the doctor Ricardo Jorge identified bubonic plague in Porto and imposed a quarantine. It was unpopular, but it helped contain the outbreak and led to Portugal's modern public health service.

# Unification of Italy and Germany
In 1850, "Italy" and "Germany" were ideas rather than states. Italy was split into kingdoms, duchies and the Papal States, with Austria ruling the north-east. Germans lived in nearly 40 states loosely tied together, the biggest being Prussia and Austria.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Italy", "items": ["led by Piedmont", "Cavour and Garibaldi", "finished 1871"]}, "r": {"t": "Germany", "items": ["led by Prussia", "Bismarck", "proclaimed 1871"]}}
### Italy
Piedmont's prime minister Cavour won French help against Austria in 1859. In 1860 Giuseppe Garibaldi and his "Thousand" volunteers conquered Sicily and Naples, then handed them to the king of Piedmont. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861, took Venice in 1866 and Rome in 1870, which became its capital in 1871.
### Germany
Prussia's chancellor Otto von Bismarck said Germany would be united not by speeches but by "blood and iron". Three short wars did it:
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1864", "l": "War with Denmark"}, {"y": "1866", "l": "War with Austria"}, {"y": "1870–71", "l": "War with France"}, {"y": "1871", "l": "German Empire proclaimed"}]}
The German Empire was proclaimed in January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, a deliberate humiliation of France, which also lost Alsace and Lorraine.
### A new balance
• Germany quickly became Europe's leading industrial and military power
• France wanted revenge and its lost provinces back
• Bismarck built alliances to isolate France; after he left in 1890, rivalries hardened into two blocs
> Two new great powers in the heart of Europe upset the old balance and planted rivalries that exploded in 1914.
!? When Italy unified, only a small minority of its people spoke standard Italian at home; most used regional languages and dialects.

# Electricity and the telephone
Electricity was studied for centuries, but only after Michael Faraday showed in 1831 how moving magnets create electric current could it be produced on a large scale. In the last quarter of the 19th century, a cluster of inventions turned it into everyday technology.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1876", "l": "Telephone patented"}, {"y": "1879", "l": "Practical light bulb"}, {"y": "1882", "l": "First power stations"}, {"y": "1886", "l": "Benz motor car"}, {"y": "1895", "l": "Radio, cinema"}]}
### Who invented what?
Inventions often had several parents. Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876, but others, including Antonio Meucci and Elisha Gray, worked on similar devices. Thomas Edison in the USA and Joseph Swan in Britain both made working light bulbs around 1879. Edison's genius was the whole system: power station, cables, meters and bulbs.
### The war of the currents

AC won, because transformers let it travel far at high voltage and then be stepped down safely. Our sockets still use it: 230 volts at 50 hertz in Portugal.
### Portugal lights up
In 1878 electric lamps lit the citadel of Cascais for the birthday of the future King Carlos, the first electric lighting in Portugal. Lisbon's first electric trams ran in 1901.

> Electricity turned energy into something that could be sent down a wire to every home and factory, the base of 20th-century life.
!? Before electric light, the working day for most people ended with sunset; cheap lighting changed how long people worked, studied and socialised.

# The spark
On 28 June 1914 Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, visited Sarajevo, capital of Bosnia, which Austria had annexed in 1908. A group of young Bosnian Serb nationalists, armed with help from a secret Serbian network, lay in wait. A bomb missed. Later the archduke's driver took a wrong turn, and 19-year-old Gavrilo Princip shot the archduke and his wife Sophie.

### From murder to world war
Austria-Hungary, with Germany's promise of support, sent Serbia an ultimatum designed to be rejected. Russia backed Serbia. Germany's war plan, the Schlieffen Plan, required knocking out France quickly through Belgium before Russia could mobilise. Invading neutral Belgium brought Britain in.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Austria v Serbia", "Russia mobilises", "Germany attacks France", "Via Belgium: Britain in"]}
### The deeper causes
Historians often summarise them as four forces:

> The assassination was the spark; alliances, war plans and rivalry were the fuel.
!! The war was inevitable after Sarajevo => Leaders made choices for five weeks; historians still debate how much blame lies with Germany and Austria-Hungary

# Two alliances
Before 1914, Europe's great powers had settled into two blocs. Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy formed the Triple Alliance in 1882. France and Russia allied in 1894, and Britain, worried by Germany's growing navy, reached understandings with France (1904) and Russia (1907): the Triple Entente.

### Changing sides and joining late
• Italy stayed out in 1914, then joined the Allies in 1915, promised Austrian lands
• The Ottoman Empire joined Germany in late 1914, opening fronts in the Middle East and at Gallipoli
• Bulgaria joined the Central Powers in 1915; Romania and Greece joined the Allies
• Japan joined the Allies and took Germany's colonies in China and the Pacific
### How Portugal entered
Portugal was already fighting German forces in Africa from 1914, but was not formally at war in Europe. In February 1916, at Britain's request, Portugal seized dozens of German merchant ships sheltering in its ports. Germany declared war on Portugal on 9 March 1916.

> Alliances meant to deter war instead turned a Balkan crisis into a war of every great power.
!? By 1918 more than 25 countries were on the Allied side, from Brazil and China to Siam (Thailand).

# Trench warfare
In autumn 1914 both sides tried to outflank each other until the line reached the sea. Soldiers dug in to survive. The result was a line of trenches about 700 km long, from the Belgian coast to the Swiss border, that barely moved for three and a half years.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Front-line trench", "Support trench, 100s of m back", "Reserve trench", "Artillery and supply lines"]}
### Why attacks failed
Defenders had machine guns firing hundreds of rounds a minute, barbed wire and dugouts deep enough to survive shelling. Attackers had to cross open "no man's land" on foot. Even when they broke into enemy trenches, reserves arrived by rail faster than the attackers could advance on foot.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Defence", "items": ["machine guns", "wire, deep dugouts", "railways bring reserves"]}, "r": {"t": "Attack", "items": ["men on foot", "shells cut wire poorly", "slow to reinforce"]}}
### The Somme
On 1 July 1916 the British army suffered about 57,000 casualties, nearly 20,000 of them killed, the worst day in its history. By November, more than a million men on both sides had been killed or wounded, and the Allies had advanced about 10 km.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~57,000", "l": "British casualties, day 1"}, {"v": "1 million+", "l": "casualties, 4.5 months"}, {"v": "~10 km", "l": "Allied gain"}]}
### Daily life
Most time in the trenches was boredom, mud, rats, lice and cold, broken by shelling and raids. Units rotated: a few days at the front, then support, then rest.
> On the Western Front, defence was stronger than attack, so armies paid enormous prices for tiny gains.
!! Soldiers spent years without leaving the front line => Most rotated in and out every few days, though some units, like the Portuguese, were kept in the line for far too long

# New weapons
The First World War was the first great war of the industrial age. Factories produced shells, guns and chemicals on a scale never seen, and both sides searched for weapons that could break the deadlock of the trenches.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1915", "l": "Chlorine gas at Ypres"}, {"y": "1915", "l": "Lusitania sunk by U-boat"}, {"y": "1916", "l": "First tanks, the Somme"}, {"y": "1917", "l": "Mustard gas; mass tanks"}]}
### Gas
Germany first used chlorine on a large scale at Ypres in April 1915; soon both sides used gas. Mustard gas burned skin and lungs. Gas masks quickly limited its effect: gas caused perhaps 90,000 deaths, a small share of the total, but it terrified soldiers and was later banned by the 1925 Geneva Protocol.
### Tanks, planes and submarines
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Artillery", "b": "caused most wounds"}, {"a": "Tanks", "b": "cross wire and trenches"}, {"a": "Aircraft", "b": "spotting, fights, bombs"}, {"a": "Submarines", "b": "sink supply ships"}]}
• Early tanks were slow (about 6 km/h) and broke down often, but by 1918 they helped break German lines
• Planes started as scouts; by 1918 there were fighter squadrons and bombing raids on cities
• German U-boats sank thousands of merchant ships and nearly starved Britain in 1917
### The deadliest weapon
Artillery was the real killer: shells and shrapnel caused the majority of wounds. Millions of tonnes of shells were fired, and farmers in France and Belgium still dig up unexploded ones every year.
> Industry made war mechanical: the side with more factories, not the bravest soldiers, had the advantage.
!? The British called the first tanks "water carriers" or "tanks" as a cover story to keep the weapon secret; the name stuck.

# Portugal at war
The young Republic had several reasons to fight: to protect Angola and Mozambique, which Germany and Britain had discussed dividing; to keep Britain's support; and to win respect for a regime many European monarchies distrusted. Many Portuguese, including monarchists and much of the army, opposed going to war.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why Portugal fought", "items": ["Defend the colonies", "Stay close to Britain", "Legitimacy of Republic"]}
### Africa
German forces from South West Africa (now Namibia) clashed with Portuguese troops in southern Angola from 1914. In northern Mozambique, Portuguese forces faced German East Africa's skilled commander Lettow-Vorbeck. Disease, heat and poor supplies killed more Portuguese soldiers than combat, and many African soldiers and porters died too.
### Flanders
From early 1917 about 55,000 men of the Portuguese Expeditionary Corps (CEP) went to France. Poorly equipped and rarely relieved, morale sank, especially after Sidónio Pais took power in December 1917 and lost interest in the war.
### La Lys, 9 April 1918
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Germans' spring offensive", "Massive bombardment", "2nd Div. overwhelmed", "7,000+ lost in hours"]}
On the morning the exhausted Portuguese 2nd Division was due to be relieved, German forces attacked in overwhelming numbers. In a few hours, over 7,000 men were killed, wounded or captured.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~55,000", "l": "Portuguese sent to France"}, {"v": "~2,000", "l": "died in France (approx.)"}, {"v": "9 Apr 1918", "l": "Battle of La Lys"}]}
> Portugal entered the war for its empire and its Republic, and paid heavily in Africa and Flanders.
!? Private Aníbal Milhais held off German attackers with his machine gun at La Lys and became a hero; his commander reportedly said he was worth a million, hence his nickname "Soldado Milhões".

# The home front
This was "total war": victory depended on factories, farms and civilians as much as soldiers. Governments took powers they had never had in peacetime, controlling industry, food, transport and information.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Conscription of millions", "Rationing of food and fuel", "Censorship and propaganda", "State control of factories", "War loans and higher taxes"]}
### Women at work
With millions of men at the front, women filled jobs in munitions factories, transport, offices and farms. In Britain, nearly a million women worked making munitions; many were poisoned by the chemicals, and their skin turned yellow. After the war many were pushed out of these jobs, but votes for women followed in Britain (1918, women over 30) and Germany (1918).
### Hunger
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Britain", "items": ["U-boats sink food ships", "rationing from 1918"]}, "r": {"t": "Germany", "items": ["naval blockade", "turnip winter 1916–17"]}}
Britain's naval blockade caused severe shortages in Germany and Austria-Hungary. In the winter of 1916–17, when potatoes failed, many Germans lived largely on turnips. Hunger and related illnesses killed hundreds of thousands of German civilians.
### In Portugal
Food shortages and rising prices hit Portuguese cities hard. In 1917 hungry crowds in Lisbon attacked shops and warehouses, the so-called "potato revolt", and strikes spread.
> In total war, the whole of society becomes part of the war machine, and its strains can topple governments.
!? To save fuel, Germany introduced daylight saving time in 1916; Britain, Portugal and others followed within weeks.

# The USA joins
In 1914 the USA declared neutrality, and most Americans wanted to stay out. But American banks lent heavily to Britain and France, and American goods fed the Allies. Germany's U-boats, trying to cut that lifeline, kept killing Americans at sea.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1915", "l": "Lusitania sunk"}, {"y": "Feb 1917", "l": "Unrestricted U-boat war"}, {"y": "Mar 1917", "l": "Zimmermann note public"}, {"y": "Apr 1917", "l": "USA declares war"}]}
### The Zimmermann telegram
In January 1917 Germany's foreign minister sent a coded message to Mexico: if the USA entered the war, Mexico should attack it and recover Texas, New Mexico and Arizona. British code-breakers intercepted and decoded it, and its publication outraged Americans.
### Russia exits
In March 1917 a revolution forced Tsar Nicholas II to abdicate. In November, the Bolsheviks under Lenin seized power, promising "peace, land and bread". In March 1918 they signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, giving up vast territories.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Germany gained", "items": ["no eastern front", "troops moved west"]}, "r": {"t": "Allies gained", "items": ["US money, ships", "~2 million US troops"]}}
### The race of 1918
Germany gambled on massive spring offensives in 1918 before the Americans arrived in strength. They gained ground but exhausted the army. From August the Allies counter-attacked; by autumn Germany's allies collapsed, and an armistice took effect at 11 am on 11 November 1918.
> The USA's entry and Russia's exit reshaped the war, and 1918 became a race Germany lost.
!! American troops won the war => They tipped the balance in 1918, but France and Britain had done most of the fighting for four years

# The human cost
No war before had killed so many soldiers. Around 65 million men were mobilised; about 9–10 million died and around 20 million were wounded, many disabled for life. Civilians died from massacres, hunger and disease.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "military dead, millions (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Germany", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Russia", "v": 1.8}, {"l": "France", "v": 1.4}, {"l": "Austria-Hungary", "v": 1.1}, {"l": "British Empire", "v": 0.9}]}
### A lost generation
France lost about one in six of the men it mobilised. Villages across Europe lost most of their young men; war memorials in almost every town list them. Survivors came back with missing limbs, disfigured faces and "shell shock", what we would now call post-traumatic stress.
### Civilian victims
In 1915 the Ottoman government deported and massacred its Armenian population; most historians call this a genocide, with estimates of the dead ranging from about 600,000 to over a million.
### The 1918 flu
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~50 m", "l": "flu deaths (est. range wide)"}, {"v": "~9–10 m", "l": "soldiers killed in war"}, {"v": "~60,000", "l": "Portuguese flu deaths (est.)"}]}
The flu spread through troop camps, ships and trains. Unusually, it killed many healthy young adults. In Portugal, where it was called the "pneumónica", it hit in autumn 1918; estimates of deaths start at about 60,000.
> The war and the pandemic together made 1914–1920 one of the deadliest periods in modern history.
!! It started in Spain, hence "Spanish flu" => Spain was neutral and reported it freely; warring countries censored news, so the name stuck to Spain

# The Treaty of Versailles
The peace conference met in Paris in 1919, dominated by France, Britain and the USA. Germany was not allowed to negotiate. The treaty was signed on 28 June 1919, five years to the day after Sarajevo, in the same Hall of Mirrors where the German Empire had been proclaimed.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["War guilt clause (Art. 231)", "Reparations: huge payments", "Army capped at 100,000", "No air force, tanks, U-boats", "All colonies and ~13% land lost"]}
### Different goals
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "France (Clemenceau)", "b": "security, weaken Germany"}, {"a": "USA (Wilson)", "b": "fair peace, League"}, {"a": "Britain (Lloyd George)", "b": "in between, trade"}]}
### A new map
Four empires fell: German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman. New or restored states appeared: Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania. Austria and Hungary became small separate countries. In the Middle East, Britain and France took Ottoman lands as "mandates", drawing borders for Iraq, Syria, Lebanon and Palestine.
### The League of Nations
US president Woodrow Wilson championed a League of Nations to settle disputes peacefully. But the US Senate refused to ratify the treaty, so the USA never joined, weakening the League from the start.
!? Portugal attended the conference as one of the victors. It received a small share of reparations and the small Kionga triangle in northern Mozambique.
> Versailles redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, but left Germany humiliated rather than reconciled.

# Seeds of the next war
Many Germans did not feel defeated: in November 1918 their armies were still on foreign soil. Nationalists spread a myth that the army had been "stabbed in the back" by socialists, Jews and politicians at home. The new democratic Weimar Republic was blamed for signing the treaty.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Humiliating peace", "Hyperinflation 1923", "Depression 1929", "Extremists win votes"]}
### Money that became paper
In 1923, after Germany fell behind on reparations, France occupied the Ruhr industrial region. The German government printed money to pay striking workers, and prices exploded. Savings were wiped out, hurting the middle classes most.
ex: Jan 1923: 1 US dollar ≈ 18,000 marks | Nov 1923: 1 US dollar ≈ 4.2 trillion marks | Prices roughly doubling every few days at the peak
### Unfinished business
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Germany", "b": "wants lost lands back"}, {"a": "Italy", "b": "felt cheated: 'mutilated'"}, {"a": "Japan", "b": "wants empire in Asia"}, {"a": "USSR", "b": "outside the system"}]}
### Why it all fell apart
The League of Nations had no army and lacked the USA. When the Great Depression struck in 1929, unemployment helped extremists, above all the Nazis, gain votes. Hitler promised to tear up Versailles, and many Germans believed him.
> The First World War did not end the rivalries that caused it; it created new grievances that extremists exploited.
!? French marshal Ferdinand Foch is said to have called the treaty not a peace but an armistice for twenty years. The Second World War began twenty years and two months later.

# The roaring twenties
After the horror of the war and the 1918 flu, many people, especially in American and European cities, wanted to enjoy life. The USA had come out of the war as the world's largest economy and its biggest lender. Mass production made new goods affordable for ordinary families for the first time.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Cars: Ford's Model T", "Radio in the living room", "Cinema, then 'talkies' (1927)", "Jazz and the Charleston", "Electric fridges, vacuums"]}
### The consumer society
Henry Ford's assembly line cut the time to build a car from over 12 hours to about an hour and a half. About 15 million Model Ts were made by 1927. Buying on credit, "pay as you go", spread to cars, radios and furniture, and advertising grew into an industry.
### New freedoms for women
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1918", "l": "UK: women over 30"}, {"y": "1920", "l": "USA: women vote"}, {"y": "1928", "l": "UK: equal voting age"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "Portugal: limited vote"}]}
Young women called "flappers" cut their hair short, wore shorter skirts, smoked and danced in public, shocking their parents. More women worked in offices and shops.
### The other side
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Boom", "items": ["cars, radio, jazz", "rising stock prices"]}, "r": {"t": "Shadows", "items": ["farmers in crisis", "Prohibition, gangsters"]}}
Not everyone roared. American farmers suffered from falling prices, racial segregation was harsh, and the ban on alcohol (Prohibition, 1920–33) fed organised crime. In Germany the decade began with hyperinflation, and in Portugal it was a time of political chaos.
> The 1920s created the modern consumer lifestyle, built on new technology, mass culture and credit.
!? In Portugal, Carolina Beatriz Ângelo voted in 1911, the first woman to vote in southern Europe, using a loophole for literate heads of household; the law was then changed to exclude women.

# The 1929 crash
Through the 1920s, share prices on Wall Street soared. Many investors bought shares "on margin", paying perhaps only 10% themselves and borrowing the rest, betting prices would keep rising. When prices began to fall, lenders demanded their money, forcing sales that pushed prices down further.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Prices fall", "Margin loans called in", "Panic selling", "Banks fail, credit dries up"]}
### Black Thursday and Black Tuesday
On 24 October 1929 panic hit the market; on 29 October, "Black Tuesday", it collapsed. But the crash was only the beginning. The Dow Jones index kept sliding for almost three years.
ex: Sept 1929 peak: Dow ≈ 381 | July 1932 low: Dow ≈ 41 | Fall ≈ 89%
### From crash to Depression
• About 9,000 American banks failed between 1930 and 1933, wiping out savers
• US unemployment rose to about 25% in 1933
• Countries raised tariffs to protect their industries, starting with the USA in 1930, so world trade shrank by about two-thirds in value
• US banks called back loans from Europe, spreading the crisis, especially to Germany
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "unemployment rate, 1932–33 (approx. %)", "items": [{"l": "USA", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Germany", "v": 30.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "UK", "v": 22.0}]}
### Portugal and the crisis
Portugal's mostly rural and fairly closed economy was hit less than industrial countries, though emigration to Brazil and the USA collapsed and exports suffered. The crisis helped Salazar argue that strict budgets and strong rule were needed.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Less spending", "Firms cut jobs", "Less income", "Even less spending"]}
> The crash turned into a Great Depression because banks, trade and spending collapsed together, each making the others worse.
!! The Depression was caused only by the stock crash => Bank failures, tight money, the gold standard and trade wars did most of the damage

# The Soviet Union
The Bolsheviks seized power in November 1917 and won a brutal civil war (1918–21). In 1922 they created the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR). After Lenin's death in 1924, Joseph Stalin outmanoeuvred his rivals and by about 1928 held supreme power.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1917", "l": "Bolshevik revolution"}, {"y": "1922", "l": "USSR founded"}, {"y": "1928", "l": "First Five-Year Plan"}, {"y": "1932–33", "l": "Great famine"}, {"y": "1937–38", "l": "Great Terror"}]}
### Industry at any cost
Stalin's Five-Year Plans built steel works, dams and whole new industrial cities at enormous speed. Output of coal, steel and electricity rose sharply, which later helped the USSR survive Hitler's invasion. But the plans relied on forced labour and fear.
### Collectivisation and famine
Peasants were forced to give up their land and animals to join collective farms. Those labelled "kulaks", supposedly rich peasants, were deported or shot. Grain was seized for the cities and for export even as harvests failed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Farms collectivised", "Grain quotas seized", "Villages starve", "Millions die 1932–33"]}
In Ukraine, the Holodomor ("death by hunger") killed roughly 3.5–5 million people; Kazakhstan lost perhaps a third or more of its Kazakh population. Ukraine and many other countries recognise the Holodomor as a genocide.
### The Great Terror
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~680,000", "l": "executed in 1937–38"}, {"v": "~18 m", "l": "passed through the Gulag"}, {"v": "1930–53", "l": "Gulag's peak years"}]}
Party members, army officers, engineers and ordinary people were arrested on invented charges, forced to confess and shot or sent to Gulag labour camps.
> Stalin turned the USSR into an industrial power through terror, famine and forced labour that killed millions of its own people.
!? Soviet censors routinely erased purged officials from photographs, so that people "disappeared" from history as well as from life.

# Fascism in Italy
Italy was on the winning side in 1918, but many Italians felt cheated: the peace gave them less territory than promised, and the economy was in trouble. Strikes and factory occupations in 1919–20 frightened landowners and industrialists, who feared a communist revolution.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["'Mutilated victory'", "Strikes and fear of reds", "Blackshirt violence", "March on Rome, 1922"]}
### The march on Rome
Benito Mussolini, a former socialist journalist, founded the fascist movement in 1919. His "blackshirt" squads beat and killed socialists and burned union offices. In October 1922, as fascists gathered to march on Rome, King Victor Emmanuel III refused to use the army against them and asked Mussolini to form a government.
### What fascism meant
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["One party, one leader (Il Duce)", "State above the individual", "Censorship and secret police", "Glory through war and empire", "Corporations in place of unions"]}
In 1924 the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti, who denounced fascist violence, was murdered. Within two years opposition parties were banned. In 1929 Mussolini made peace with the Pope, creating the Vatican City state. In 1935–36 Italy invaded Ethiopia, using poison gas.
### A model for others
• The name comes from the "fasces", a bundle of rods carried in ancient Rome as a symbol of authority
• Fascist movements copied Mussolini across Europe, from Germany to Spain
• Salazar admired some of his methods but kept his own distance from fascist mass politics
> Fascism was born in Italy: a violent, nationalist movement that took power legally and then destroyed democracy from within.
!! Mussolini made the trains run on time => It was mostly propaganda; much railway improvement predated him

# Hitler's rise
Adolf Hitler, an Austrian-born former corporal, led a small party of extremists that tried and failed to seize power in Munich in 1923. In prison he wrote Mein Kampf, setting out his racism and plans for conquest. For most of the 1920s the Nazis were a fringe party.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "Nazi % of vote, Reichstag elections", "items": [{"l": "May 1928", "v": 2.6}, {"l": "Sep 1930", "v": 18.3}, {"l": "Jul 1932", "v": 37.3, "hi": true}, {"l": "Nov 1932", "v": 33.1}]}
### How the Depression helped
With six million unemployed, many voters turned to parties promising radical change: the Nazis and the communists. The Nazis blamed Versailles, communists and Jews, and promised jobs, order and national pride. They never won a majority in a free election.
### From chancellor to dictator
Conservative politicians persuaded President Hindenburg to appoint Hitler chancellor on 30 January 1933, believing they could control him. They were wrong.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Jan 1933", "l": "Hitler chancellor"}, {"y": "Feb 1933", "l": "Reichstag fire"}, {"y": "Mar 1933", "l": "Enabling Act"}, {"y": "Aug 1934", "l": "Hitler the Führer"}, {"y": "1935", "l": "Nuremberg Laws"}]}
After a fire at the parliament building, civil liberties were suspended. The Enabling Act let Hitler make laws without parliament. Unions and other parties were abolished, and the first concentration camp opened at Dachau in March 1933.
### Persecution and rearmament
• Jews were boycotted, expelled from jobs and, from 1935, stripped of citizenship
• On Kristallnacht in November 1938, synagogues burned across Germany
• Conscription returned in 1935 and military spending soared, which reduced unemployment
> Hitler came to power through elections and a backroom deal, then used legal powers to destroy democracy in months.

The USA dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. The Soviet Union tested its own bomb in 1949, years earlier than Washington expected, partly thanks to spies. From then on each side raced to build more, and more powerful, weapons.
### Bigger and bigger
The hydrogen bomb (USA 1952, USSR 1953) used fusion and could be a thousand times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb. In 1961 the Soviets tested the "Tsar Bomba", about 50 megatons, the largest explosion ever made by humans.

### Too many to use
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~70,000", "l": "warheads at the 1986 peak"}, {"v": "~12,000", "l": "warheads in the world today"}, {"v": "9", "l": "countries with nuclear arms"}]}
Rockets meant a warhead could cross the world in about 30 minutes. Both sides kept missiles on land, bombers in the air and submarines hidden at sea, so that even after a surprise attack they could still strike back. This is **mutually assured destruction** (MAD): if attacking guarantees your own ruin, nobody attacks.

# The Cuban Missile Crisis
Fidel Castro took power in Cuba in 1959 and moved towards Moscow. After a failed US-backed invasion at the Bay of Pigs (1961), Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev secretly sent nuclear missiles to the island, about 150 km from Florida. He also wanted to answer US missiles placed in Turkey, next to the USSR.

President John F. Kennedy rejected an immediate air strike and chose a naval "quarantine" around Cuba. Soviet ships approached, then turned back. On 27 October, the most dangerous day, a US spy plane was shot down over Cuba and secret talks raced against the military's plans.
### The deal

# Proxy wars
A proxy war is one where big powers fight through others: they supply weapons, money, advisers and sometimes troops, while avoiding a direct clash with each other. Most Cold War deaths happened this way, far from Washington and Moscow.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Cold War proxy wars", "items": ["Korea, 1950–53", "Vietnam, to 1975", "Afghanistan, 1979–89", "Angola, 1975–2002"]}
### Korea and Vietnam
In Korea, the communist North invaded the South in 1950. A US-led UN force fought North Korea and China. The war ended in 1953 with an armistice, not a peace treaty, and the peninsula is still divided near where it began. In Vietnam, the USA had over 500,000 troops at its 1969 peak; about 58,000 Americans and probably well over a million Vietnamese died before Saigon fell in 1975.
### Afghanistan and Angola
The Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan in 1979; the USA, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia armed the mujahideen fighters. After about 15,000 Soviet deaths, Moscow withdrew in 1989. In Angola, after independence from Portugal in 1975, the MPLA government was backed by the USSR and tens of thousands of Cuban troops, while UNITA had US and South African support.

# The space race
On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, a 58 cm metal ball that beeped radio signals as it circled Earth every 96 minutes. Americans were shocked: a rocket that could reach orbit could also carry a warhead to any city.

The USSR led for years: the first animal in orbit (the dog Laika, 1957), the first man (Yuri Gagarin, 108 minutes, 12 April 1961), the first woman (Valentina Tereshkova, 1963) and the first spacewalk (Alexei Leonov, 1965).

# Détente and reform
**Détente** is French for "relaxation". By the late 1960s both superpowers were spending huge sums on weapons, and the USSR had caught up in missiles. Leaders such as Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev decided limits were safer than an endless race.

### Freeze and thaw
The 1975 Helsinki agreements accepted Europe's borders but also committed the East to human rights, which dissidents then used against their own governments. Détente collapsed after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979; the early 1980s brought new missiles and fear.

# The Wall falls
In 1989 the Soviet bloc unravelled, country by country. Gorbachev made clear that Soviet troops would not crush reform, as they had in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968. Without that threat, the communist regimes had little support of their own.

# Life under the Estado Novo
António de Oliveira Salazar, an economics professor, became finance minister in 1928 and prime minister in 1932. His 1933 constitution created the **Estado Novo** ("New State"), which lasted until 1974, one of Europe's longest dictatorships.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Salazar: unchallenged leader", "Single party: União Nacional", "PIDE, censors, Legion", "Church, Mocidade, family"]}
### How control worked
• Censors with the famous "blue pencil" cut newspapers, books, films and songs
• The PIDE political police used informers, torture and the Tarrafal prison camp in Cape Verde
• Elections were held, but the opposition was harassed and the results managed
• Strikes were illegal; workers and bosses were organised in state "corporations"
### A poor country
Salazar balanced the budget and kept Portugal out of the Second World War, but he distrusted industry and mass education. Most people lived from small farms; many homes had no electricity or running water.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "infant deaths per 1,000 births", "items": [{"l": "Portugal 1960", "v": 78.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Portugal 2023", "v": 2.5}]}

Women's lives were tightly limited: a married woman needed her husband's permission for some jobs and to travel abroad, and nurses and air hostesses long faced marriage restrictions.
> The Estado Novo bought stability with silence: no free press, no free parties and very little change.

# Emigration
Portugal had always sent people abroad, mostly to Brazil. After about 1960 the flow turned to Europe, where France and West Germany were booming and short of workers. Between 1960 and 1974 roughly 1.5 million Portuguese left, out of a population of under 9 million.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rural poverty", "Fear of 4 years at war", "Cross the border 'a salto'", "Factory or building job"]}
### Leaving "a salto"
The state limited legal emigration, so many people went clandestinely. They paid smugglers ("passadores") to guide them on foot over the mountains into Spain and across the Pyrenees into France. Young men also fled to avoid being sent to fight in Africa.
### Life abroad
Many first lived in shanty towns, such as the bidonville at Champigny-sur-Marne near Paris, and worked in construction, factories and cleaning. They sent money home, and built houses in their villages that you can still spot today.

# The end of empires
In 1945 European countries still ruled much of Africa and Asia. Within thirty years nearly all of it was independent. The war had weakened Britain, France and the Netherlands, colonised peoples had fought for them and expected freedom in return, and both superpowers opposed old-style empires.

### Peaceful and violent paths
Some independence came through negotiation, as in Ghana under Kwame Nkrumah. Some came through long wars: Indonesia against the Dutch, Algeria against France (1954–62), and the Portuguese colonies.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Negotiated", "items": ["Ghana, 1957", "most of French West Africa"]}, "r": {"t": "Fought for", "items": ["Algeria, 1954–62", "Angola, Mozambique"]}}
### Partition
British India split into mainly Hindu India and mainly Muslim Pakistan in August 1947. Borders drawn in weeks set off mass migration and killings: perhaps 10–20 million people moved and hundreds of thousands to a million or more died.
### A changed world

• Many new states kept colonial borders that cut across ethnic groups
• Some joined the Non-Aligned Movement (1961), refusing to pick a Cold War side
• Economic ties with former rulers often continued long after the flags changed
> In one generation, empires that took centuries to build were dismantled, and most of humanity came to live in independent states.

# Civil rights
After 1945, people who were denied equal rights because of race or sex used the language of democracy and human rights, written into the 1948 Universal Declaration, against their own governments.
### The US movement
In the American South, segregation laws kept Black citizens in separate schools, buses and restaurants, and stopped most from voting. In 1955 Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat in Montgomery, Alabama; the boycott that followed lasted over a year and made Martin Luther King Jr. a national leader.

# European integration
After two world wars started in Europe, French minister Robert Schuman proposed on 9 May 1950 to pool coal and steel, the raw materials of war, under a shared authority. If France and Germany ran these industries together, war between them would become "materially impossible".

### From six to 27

# China's rise
Under Mao Zedong (1949–76), China's economy was run by central plans and political campaigns. The Great Leap Forward caused a famine in 1959–61 that killed tens of millions; estimates range from about 15 to 45 million. The Cultural Revolution (1966–76) brought further chaos.
### Deng's reforms
From 1978 Deng Xiaoping let farmers sell surplus crops, allowed private businesses and opened Special Economic Zones to foreign investment. His slogan was that it doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice.

### The results

Shenzhen, next to Hong Kong, grew from a town of tens of thousands to a megacity of electronics and tech companies. China became the "world's factory", and since 2010 the world's second-largest economy.

# Globalisation
Globalisation means the world's economies becoming tied together through trade, money, people and information. It has happened before, but after 1945, and especially after 1990, it went further and faster than ever.
### The humble box
In 1956 American trucker Malcom McLean shipped goods in standard steel **containers**. They could move from ship to train to lorry without unpacking. Loading costs fell dramatically, ports were automated, and shipping became so cheap that distance hardly mattered.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Shipping containers, 1956", "GATT, then WTO (1995)", "Cheap flights", "Internet and mobile phones", "End of the Cold War"]}

### Supply chains
A phone may be designed in California, use chips from Taiwan, rare metals from Africa and be assembled in China or India. Cars made in Portugal, such as at Autoeuropa in Palmela, use parts from across Europe.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Winners", "items": ["cheaper goods", "growth in Asia"]}, "r": {"t": "Strains", "items": ["lost factory jobs", "fragile supply chains"]}}

> Globalisation made goods cheaper and lifted billions, but its gains and pains were spread very unevenly.

# Financial crisis
In the 2000s US banks lent heavily to homebuyers with weak finances ("subprime" mortgages). These loans were bundled into complex securities and sold worldwide, often rated as safe. When house prices fell, the losses spread through the whole financial system.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Risky home loans", "Bundled and sold", "House prices fall", "Banks fail, credit freezes"]}
### September 2008
Lehman Brothers, a major US investment bank, went bankrupt on 15 September 2008. Banks stopped trusting each other; governments rescued banks with huge sums and central banks cut interest rates to almost zero. In 2009 the world economy shrank for the first time in decades.
### The euro crisis
Countries with high debts or banking problems found that markets would only lend to them at very high interest rates. Unlike before, they could not devalue their own currencies.

In December 2019 a new coronavirus, SARS-CoV-2, was identified in Wuhan, China. It spread through the air, often before people felt ill, and by March 2020 the WHO declared a pandemic. Hospitals filled and countries closed schools, shops and borders.

# Climate change
Scientists have known since the 19th century that carbon dioxide traps heat. In 1958 Charles Keeling began measuring it on Mauna Loa in Hawaii; his curve has risen every year since. By the 1980s warming was measurable, and in 1988 the UN set up the IPCC to assess the science.

### The agreements
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1988", "l": "IPCC founded"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "Rio climate treaty"}, {"y": "1997", "l": "Kyoto Protocol"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "Paris Agreement"}]}
In Paris almost every country agreed to keep warming "well below 2 °C" and to try for 1.5 °C, each setting its own targets. The USA has left the agreement twice, most recently with effect from January 2026.

# Early computers
Before machines, a "computer" was a person, often a woman, who did calculations by hand. The Second World War needed far more calculations than people could do: code-breaking, artillery tables and the atomic bomb.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1936", "l": "Turing's ideal machine"}, {"y": "1941", "l": "Zuse's Z3, Germany"}, {"y": "1944", "l": "Colossus, Britain"}, {"y": "1945", "l": "ENIAC, USA"}, {"y": "1948", "l": "Manchester Baby"}]}
### Colossus and ENIAC
At Bletchley Park, Tommy Flowers built Colossus to break German high-command codes; it was kept secret until the 1970s. In the USA, ENIAC was built at the University of Pennsylvania to calculate shell trajectories.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "27 t", "l": "ENIAC's weight"}, {"v": "~18,000", "l": "vacuum tubes"}, {"v": "~5,000", "l": "additions per second"}]}
### Rewiring to program
ENIAC was programmed by plugging cables and setting switches, which could take days. Six women, including Kay McNulty and Jean Bartik, worked out how to program it, though they were long left out of the history books.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "ENIAC, 1945", "items": ["fills a room", "~150 kilowatts"]}, "r": {"t": "Smartphone", "items": ["fits a pocket", "millions of times faster"]}}
### The stored program
The big idea came next: store the program in memory just like data, so you can change it without rewiring. The Manchester Baby ran the first stored program in June 1948. Every computer since works this way.
> Early computers were built for war, but the idea of a programmable machine that stores its own instructions changed everything.
!? Vacuum tubes failed so often that ENIAC's engineers learned to leave it switched on, because most failures happened when it was turned on or off.

# The transistor and the chip
Vacuum tubes were big, hot and fragile. In December 1947 John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley at Bell Labs built the first **transistor**: a tiny switch made from a semiconductor. They won the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Vacuum tube, 1900s", "Transistor, 1947", "Integrated circuit, 1958", "Microprocessor, 1971"]}
### Many switches on one slice
In 1958 Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments put several components on one piece of material; months later Robert Noyce at Fairchild made a practical silicon version. This **integrated circuit**, or chip, could be printed rather than wired by hand.
### Moore's law
In 1965 Gordon Moore noticed that the number of transistors on a chip doubled regularly, later about every two years. That trend held for about 50 years.
ex: Intel 4004 (1971): 2,300 transistors | Doubling every 2 years for 50 years = 2²⁵ ≈ 33 million times | ≈ 77 billion, close to today's biggest chips

# The internet
In 1969 the US Defense Department's research agency, ARPA, linked four university computers in **ARPANET**. The first message, sent from UCLA to Stanford in October 1969, was meant to be "LOGIN"; the system crashed after "LO".
### Packets
The key idea was **packet switching**: a message is cut into small packets that travel separately and are reassembled at the end. If one route is busy or broken, packets go another way.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Message cut into packets", "Each finds its own route", "Reassembled at the end"]}
### One network of networks
Other networks appeared, and they could not talk to each other. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn designed TCP/IP, a shared set of rules. On 1 January 1983 ARPANET switched to it, and any network could join the "inter-net".
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1969", "l": "ARPANET"}, {"y": "1971", "l": "First email with @"}, {"y": "1983", "l": "TCP/IP"}, {"y": "1991", "l": "Portugal online"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "Commercial internet"}]}
### Portugal joins
The .pt domain was created in 1988. Portugal's universities connected to the internet in the early 1990s through the national research network run by FCCN, and home access spread later in the decade.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "internet users worldwide, billions", "items": [{"l": "1995", "v": 0.04}, {"l": "2005", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "2015", "v": 3.2}, {"l": "2025", "v": 6.0, "hi": true}]}
> The internet is a network of networks that works because everyone agreed to speak the same simple language, TCP/IP.
!! The internet was designed to survive a nuclear war => ARPANET was built to share expensive computers; that research only influenced it

# The World Wide Web

On 30 April 1993 CERN released the web software into the public domain. Anyone could build on it without paying. The Mosaic browser (1993), with pictures on the page, and then Netscape (1994) made the web popular.

# Search and social media
By the late 1990s the web had millions of pages and finding things was hard. Larry Page and Sergey Brin, students at Stanford, ranked pages by how many other pages linked to them, weighted by those pages' own importance. Their search engine, Google, launched in 1998.

# Mobile phones
On 3 April 1973 Motorola engineer Martin Cooper made the first handheld mobile call in New York, on a phone weighing about 1 kg. Early mobiles were car phones or expensive "bricks" for business people.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1973", "l": "First handheld call"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "GSM in Portugal"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "First text message"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "iPhone"}, {"y": "2008", "l": "Android phones"}]}
### Europe's standard
European countries agreed on one digital standard, **GSM**, so a phone could work across borders. Text messages (SMS) were added almost as an afterthought and became hugely popular.
### Portugal's fast start
Portugal's operators launched GSM in 1992, and TMN's prepaid "Mimo" card in 1995 was one of the first prepaid mobile services anywhere. With no contract, almost anyone could own a phone, and adoption soared.

# Multibanco
In 1983 Portuguese banks set up a shared company, SIBS, to run a common network of cash machines and payment terminals. Multibanco opened in 1985. Unlike in many countries, where each bank had its own machines, a card from any Portuguese bank worked in every ATM from the start.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Banks create SIBS, 1983", "Multibanco opens, 1985", "All banks, one network", "Payments added"]}
### More than cash
Portugal's ATMs soon did much more than give money. You could pay utility bills using an "entidade" and "referência", top up a mobile phone, buy concert or train tickets and pay taxes, all from the same machine.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Withdraw cash", "Pay bills by reference", "Top up phones", "Buy tickets", "Pay taxes"]}
### Why Portugal led
• Banks cooperated on one system instead of competing with many
• Late arrival meant it was built on newer technology
• The state and utilities adopted Multibanco references early
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Many countries", "items": ["bank-only ATMs", "cash and balance"]}, "r": {"t": "Multibanco", "items": ["one shared network", "bills, tickets, taxes"]}}
### MB WAY
The same network now runs MB WAY (2015), which lets people send money or pay with a phone number and is used by millions in Portugal.
> Multibanco shows how cooperation between competitors can create a service better than any could build alone.
!? Many online shops in Portugal still let you pay with a "referência Multibanco", a method almost unknown abroad.

# Cloud and data
For decades every company ran its own servers in a back room. In 2006 Amazon Web Services began renting computing power and storage over the internet, paid by the hour. Microsoft Azure (2010) and Google Cloud followed. This is **cloud computing**.

### Layers of the cloud

### Data as a resource
Every click, purchase and journey produces data. Companies use it to recommend products, set prices, detect fraud and train AI. Whoever holds the most useful data has a big advantage, which is why privacy laws such as the EU's GDPR matter.
### The physical cloud
The cloud is not in the sky. It lives in enormous buildings full of servers that need lots of electricity and water for cooling.

Portugal is attracting large data centres, notably at Sines, where submarine internet cables land and renewable power is available.
> The cloud turned computing into a utility, like electricity, rented on demand from a few giant providers.
!! The cloud is weightless and green => It runs on physical data centres that use roughly as much electricity as all of France

# The AI era
The term "artificial intelligence" was coined in 1956. For decades AI used hand-written rules and repeatedly disappointed, leading to "AI winters". The breakthrough came from **machine learning**: systems that learn patterns from huge amounts of examples instead of being told the rules.

### Why it took off in 2012
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Huge datasets from the web", "Graphics chips (GPUs)", "Better neural networks"]}
In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet easily won an image-recognition contest by running on graphics chips. In 2017 Google researchers published the **Transformer**, a design that handles language very well and scales with more data and computing power.
### Language models
Large language models learn by predicting the next word across vast amounts of text. ChatGPT, released in November 2022, reached about 100 million users in two months, then the fastest-growing app ever. Systems such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini now write, translate, summarise and code.

### Rules and recognition
• The EU AI Act (2024) is the first broad AI law, applied in stages to 2027
• AlphaFold predicted the shapes of most known proteins; its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry
• Data centres for AI are driving new demand for chips and electricity
> Modern AI learns from data rather than rules, and in a decade it went from labs to everyday tools.
!! AI understands the world like a person => Today's models find statistical patterns; they can be fluent yet confidently wrong
