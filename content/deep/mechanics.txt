# Force, mass and weight
Mass is how much matter something contains; weight is the force gravity exerts on that mass. In everyday speech they're mixed up, but engineers keep them apart.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "W = m × g", "items": [{"s": "W", "m": "weight in newtons"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass in kg"}, {"s": "g", "m": "about 9.81 m/s² on Earth"}]}
### Same mass, different weight
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "weight of a 70 kg person, N", "items": [{"l": "Moon", "v": 113.0}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 260.0}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 687.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 1736.0}]}
### Newton's second law
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = m × a", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "net force in newtons"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass"}, {"s": "a", "m": "acceleration"}]}
### Units
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1 newton", "b": "force to accelerate 1 kg by 1 m/s²"}, {"a": "1 kg weight", "b": "about 9.81 N"}, {"a": "1 kN", "b": "about the weight of 100 kg"}]}
### Why it matters
Structures and machines must carry weights (forces), so engineers convert masses to newtons before any calculation.
> Mass is in kilograms; weight is a force, in newtons.

# Levers multiply force
A lever is a rigid bar that rotates around a pivot. The turning effect of a force (its moment or torque) is force times distance from the pivot.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Torque = F × d", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "force"}, {"s": "d", "m": "perpendicular distance to pivot"}]}
### Balancing
ex: 600 N child at 1 m vs adult pushing at 3 m | 600 × 1 = 200 × 3 | Adult needs just 200 N
### Three classes of lever
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Class 1", "b": "pivot in middle: seesaw, scissors"}, {"a": "Class 2", "b": "load in middle: wheelbarrow, nutcracker"}, {"a": "Class 3", "b": "effort in middle: tweezers, your forearm"}]}
### Everyday levers
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Long spanners", "Door handles", "Crowbars", "Bottle openers", "Oars"]}
!? Archimedes is said to have boasted, "Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth", capturing the power of a long enough lever.

# Try it: balance the seesaw
A seesaw balances when the moments on each side are equal. Mass alone doesn't decide it: distance from the pivot matters just as much.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "m₁ × d₁ = m₂ × d₂", "items": [{"s": "m", "m": "mass on each side"}, {"s": "d", "m": "distance from pivot"}]}
### The challenge
ex: Right side: 6 kg × 1 m = 6 kg·m | Left options: 3 kg at 2 m, 2 kg at 3 m, 12 kg at 0.5 m | All balance
### Several masses
With more than one mass on a side, add their moments: the total on each side must be equal.
### Real seesaws
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Children shift position to balance", "Cranes use counterweights", "Balance scales", "Mobile sculptures"]}
> Mass times distance on one side must equal mass times distance on the other.

# Pulleys trade distance for force
Pulleys redirect and divide forces. With movable pulleys, the load is shared between several rope segments, so you pull with less force but over a longer distance.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fixed pulley", "b": "changes direction only, MA 1"}, {"a": "Single movable pulley", "b": "MA 2"}, {"a": "Block and tackle with 4 strands", "b": "MA 4"}]}
### Energy is conserved
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Work = force × distance", "items": [{"s": "Half the force, twice the distance", "m": "same work"}]}
ex: Lift 100 kg by 1 m with MA 4 | Pull about 245 N | over 4 m of rope
### Friction
Real pulleys lose some energy to friction in the bearings and rope bending, so actual force is a bit higher than the ideal.
### Where you see them
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Cranes", "Sailing boats", "Lifts", "Gym machines", "Flagpoles"]}
> Machines trade force for distance; they never create energy.

# Gears: speed or torque

ex: 10-tooth gear drives 20-tooth gear | Ratio 2:1 | Half the speed, twice the torque

# Balanced and unbalanced forces
Newton's first law: an object stays at rest or moves at constant velocity unless a net force acts on it.

### Free-body diagrams
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Isolate the object", "Draw every force acting on it", "Add vectors", "Find net force", "Apply F = m × a"]}

> Balanced forces don't mean no forces; they mean no net force.

# Inclined planes
A ramp lets you raise a load using less force by spreading the work over a longer distance.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F ≈ W × h ÷ L", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "push needed (no friction)"}, {"s": "W", "m": "weight"}, {"s": "h", "m": "height"}, {"s": "L", "m": "ramp length"}]}
### Example
ex: 1,000 N load raised 1 m | Ramp 4 m long | Push ≈ 250 N (plus friction)
### Steeper vs longer
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Steep ramp", "items": ["short", "more force"]}, "r": {"t": "Gentle ramp", "items": ["long", "less force"]}}
### Accessibility
Building rules limit wheelchair ramp slopes, typically to around 1:12 or gentler, so people can push themselves up comfortably.
### History
Ancient builders likely used ramps to raise stones for pyramids and temples, trading distance for force.
> A ramp makes lifting easier by making the journey longer.

# Screws and wedges
Screws and wedges are variations of the inclined plane that produce large forces.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Screw", "b": "ramp wrapped around a cylinder"}, {"a": "Wedge", "b": "two ramps back to back"}, {"a": "Pitch", "b": "distance moved per turn"}]}

ex: Screw jack with 5 mm pitch, 30 cm handle | Handle travels about 1.9 m per turn | Huge force multiplication (minus friction)
### Wedges
Axes, chisels, knives and doorstops turn a push into sideways splitting or holding forces. Thinner wedges split more easily but are weaker.

# Mechanical advantage
Mechanical advantage (MA) measures how much a machine multiplies force. The price is distance.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "MA = output force ÷ input force", "items": [{"s": "", "m": "Ideal MA = input distance ÷ output distance"}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lever, 5:1 arms", "b": "MA 5"}, {"a": "Four-strand pulley", "b": "MA 4"}, {"a": "Gear 3:1", "b": "torque × 3"}, {"a": "Ramp 4 m for 1 m rise", "b": "MA 4"}]}
### Efficiency
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Efficiency = actual MA ÷ ideal MA", "items": [{"s": "", "m": "Friction reduces actual MA"}]}
### Velocity ratio
Engineers sometimes use velocity ratio: how much farther the input moves than the output. For frictionless machines, MA equals velocity ratio.
> More force out always means more distance in.

# Tension and compression
Structures resist loads through two basic internal forces: tension (pulling apart) and compression (pushing together).
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tension", "items": ["stretched", "cables", "ties", "bottom of beams"]}, "r": {"t": "Compression", "items": ["squeezed", "columns", "arches", "top of beams"]}}
### In a beam
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Load in middle", "Beam bends", "Top squeezed (compression)", "Bottom stretched (tension)", "Neutral axis feels neither"]}
### Reinforced concrete
Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension, so steel bars are placed where tension occurs, usually near the bottom of beams. Joseph Monier patented reinforced concrete ideas in the 1860s.
### Materials by strength
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Steel", "b": "strong in both"}, {"a": "Concrete", "b": "compression only"}, {"a": "Cables", "b": "tension only"}, {"a": "Stone", "b": "compression"}]}
> Know where the tension is, and you know where the steel goes.

An arch turns downward loads into compression that flows around its curve to the supports. It's ideal for stone, which is strong in compression.

When a beam bends, stress is greatest at the top and bottom and lowest near the middle. The I-beam puts material where it works hardest.

# Strength and stiffness are different
Strength is how much stress a material withstands before failing. Stiffness is how much it resists deforming. A material can be strong but flexible, or stiff but weak.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strength", "items": ["load before breaking or yielding", "MPa"]}, "r": {"t": "Stiffness", "items": ["resistance to deformation", "Young's modulus", "GPa"]}}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rubber", "b": "weak and flexible"}, {"a": "Glass", "b": "stiff but brittle"}, {"a": "Steel", "b": "strong and stiff"}, {"a": "Nylon rope", "b": "strong but stretchy"}]}
### Toughness
A third property, toughness, is the energy absorbed before fracture. Steel is tough; glass is not.
### Design implications
• Bridges must be stiff enough not to sway uncomfortably
• Ropes must be strong but can stretch
• Aircraft need strength and stiffness at low weight
> Strong, stiff and tough are three different virtues.

# Concrete
Concrete is a mix of cement, water, sand and gravel. It's the world's most used construction material after water.

# Ductile vs brittle
Ductile materials stretch and bend significantly before breaking. Brittle materials fracture suddenly with little deformation.

# What friction is
Friction resists sliding between surfaces. At the microscopic level, even polished surfaces are rough, touching only at tiny high points.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Surfaces press together", "Microscopic peaks touch", "Bonds and interlocking", "Resistance to sliding"]}
### Classic laws
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Amontons (1699)", "b": "friction proportional to normal force"}, {"a": "Independent of area", "b": "for most solid contacts"}, {"a": "Coulomb (1785)", "b": "kinetic friction roughly independent of speed"}]}
### Leonardo first
Leonardo da Vinci studied friction around 1500, two centuries before Amontons, but his notes weren't published.
### Energy
Friction turns motion into heat. Rub your hands together and feel it.
> Friction is countless microscopic contacts resisting motion.

# Coefficient of friction
The friction force is roughly the normal force multiplied by a coefficient (μ) that depends on the two materials.

ex: 1,000 N box on wood floor, μ ≈ 0.4 | F ≈ 400 N | to keep it sliding
### Variability
Coefficients vary with surface condition, moisture, temperature and contamination. Engineers use conservative values.

# Static vs kinetic
It takes more force to start sliding than to keep sliding. Static friction is generally higher than kinetic friction.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Static", "items": ["before motion", "higher", "grips"]}, "r": {"t": "Kinetic", "items": ["during sliding", "lower", "slips"]}}
### ABS brakes
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hard braking", "Wheel about to lock", "ABS releases briefly", "Tyre keeps static grip", "Shorter stops and steering control"]}

### Stick-slip
Alternating sticking and slipping causes squeaky doors, creaking floors and violin sounds.
> Once it starts sliding, it's easier to keep it sliding.

# Pascal's principle
Pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally throughout. A small force on a small piston creates a large force on a large piston.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F₂ = F₁ × A₂ ÷ A₁", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "force"}, {"s": "A", "m": "piston area"}]}
### Example
ex: Small piston 1 cm², big piston 100 cm² | Push 100 N | Lift force 10,000 N
### The trade-off
The small piston must move 100 times farther than the large one rises. Energy is conserved.
### History
Blaise Pascal described the principle in the 1650s. Joseph Bramah patented the hydraulic press in 1795.

# Four forces
In flight, four forces act on an aircraft: lift, weight, thrust and drag.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Vertical", "items": ["lift up", "weight down"]}, "r": {"t": "Horizontal", "items": ["thrust forward", "drag back"]}}
### Steady flight
In level flight at constant speed, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. The plane isn't "pushing harder" than in cruise; forces are balanced.
### Manoeuvres
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Climb", "b": "more lift or thrust"}, {"a": "Descend", "b": "reduce thrust or lift"}, {"a": "Accelerate", "b": "thrust exceeds drag"}, {"a": "Turn", "b": "bank so lift tilts sideways"}]}
### Weight changes
Airliners burn tens of tonnes of fuel on long flights, getting lighter and able to climb to higher, more efficient altitudes.
> Steady flight is a balance of four forces.

# How wings make lift
A wing creates lift by deflecting air downward. By Newton's third law, pushing air down pushes the wing up.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Wing at angle of attack", "Air deflected downward", "Lower pressure above, higher below", "Net upward force"]}
### Two views, one phenomenon
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Newton view", "items": ["wing pushes air down", "air pushes wing up"]}, "r": {"t": "Pressure view", "items": ["faster flow above", "lower pressure", "pressure difference lifts"]}}
Both describe the same physics.
### A myth
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Air over the top must meet the air underneath at the trailing edge", "r": "Air over the top actually arrives sooner, equal transit time is false"}]}
### Angle of attack
Even a flat plate generates lift at an angle. The curved shape (camber) makes wings more efficient and gentler at stalling.
> Wings fly by pushing air down.

# The lift equation
Lift depends on air density, speed, wing area and a lift coefficient set by wing shape and angle of attack.

### Speed squared
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative lift at same angle", "items": [{"l": "100 km/h", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "200 km/h", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "300 km/h", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}]}
### Hot and high
Hot air and high altitudes mean thinner air. Planes need higher speeds and longer runways to take off from airports such as Mexico City or La Paz.
### Weight limits
On hot days at high-altitude airports, airlines sometimes reduce passengers, cargo or fuel to take off safely.
> Thin air demands more speed for the same lift.

# Stall
A stall occurs when the wing's angle of attack is too high. Airflow separates from the upper surface and lift drops sharply.

### Recovery
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lower the nose", "Reduce angle of attack", "Add power", "Level wings"]}
### Warnings
Aircraft have stall warning systems: horns, stick shakers and, in modern airliners, protections that limit angle of attack.
### Air France 447
In 2009, Air France flight 447 crashed into the Atlantic after the crew failed to recognise and recover from a high-altitude stall, a tragedy that reshaped pilot training.
> Stall depends on angle, not speed alone.

Drag resists motion through air. It rises with the square of speed, so faster travel costs disproportionately more energy.

# The four-stroke cycle
Most car engines run on the four-stroke cycle patented by Nikolaus Otto's company in 1876. Each cylinder repeats four piston strokes over two crankshaft turns.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Intake: piston down, mixture in", "Compression: piston up, mixture squeezed", "Power: spark ignites, piston pushed down", "Exhaust: piston up, gases out"]}

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Intake", "b": "valves open, air and fuel drawn in"}, {"a": "Compression", "b": "valves closed, pressure and temperature rise"}, {"a": "Power", "b": "combustion drives piston"}, {"a": "Exhaust", "b": "burnt gases pushed out"}]}
### Only one stroke makes power
Three of the four strokes absorb energy. Multiple cylinders firing in sequence and a heavy flywheel keep rotation smooth.

# Crankshaft
The crankshaft converts the pistons' up-and-down motion into rotation, just as your legs turn bicycle pedals.

# Absolute zero
Absolute zero, −273.15 °C, is the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the minimum possible motion.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "0 K", "b": "−273.15 °C"}, {"a": "273.15 K", "b": "0 °C"}, {"a": "373.15 K", "b": "100 °C"}, {"a": "Room temperature", "b": "about 293 K"}]}
### Can we reach it?
Absolute zero can be approached but never reached. Laboratories have cooled atoms to billionths of a kelvin.
### Kelvin
Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) proposed the absolute temperature scale in 1848. Kelvin degrees are the same size as Celsius degrees.
### Cold places
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Liquid nitrogen", "b": "77 K"}, {"a": "Liquid helium", "b": "4 K"}, {"a": "Space background", "b": "2.7 K"}, {"a": "Lab records", "b": "below a billionth of a kelvin"}]}
> At absolute zero, there's no heat left to take away.

# First law
The first law of thermodynamics is conservation of energy: energy can't be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "ΔU = Q − W", "items": [{"s": "ΔU", "m": "change in internal energy"}, {"s": "Q", "m": "heat added"}, {"s": "W", "m": "work done by the system"}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Heating a gas in a fixed box", "b": "all heat raises internal energy"}, {"a": "Gas expanding in an engine", "b": "heat partly becomes work"}, {"a": "Compressing air", "b": "work raises temperature"}]}
### Bicycle pump
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Push pump", "Work on air", "Air heats up", "Pump barrel gets warm"]}
### History
The principle was established in the 1840s through work by James Joule, Julius Mayer and Hermann von Helmholtz.
> You can't win: energy in equals energy out.

# Second law
The second law says heat flows naturally from hot to cold, and that every real process increases total disorder (entropy).
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hot coffee", "Heat flows to cooler room", "Coffee cools", "Room warms slightly", "Never reverses on its own"]}
### Consequences
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No engine can be 100% efficient", "Heat pumps need work to move heat uphill", "Perpetual motion is impossible", "Time has a direction"]}
### Entropy
Entropy measures how spread out energy is. Concentrated energy (fuel, a hot object) is useful; spread-out energy (warm room) is hard to use.
### Popular summary
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "First law", "b": "you can't win"}, {"a": "Second law", "b": "you can't break even"}, {"a": "Third law", "b": "you can't quit the game"}]}
> Heat runs downhill unless you pay to push it up.

# Carnot efficiency
In 1824, French engineer Sadi Carnot showed that the maximum efficiency of any heat engine depends only on the hot and cold temperatures it works between.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "η max = 1 − T cold ÷ T hot", "items": [{"s": "", "m": "Temperatures in kelvin"}]}
### Example
ex: Steam at 600 °C (873 K), cooling water at 30 °C (303 K) | 1 − 303 ÷ 873 | ≈ 65% maximum
Real power stations reach 40–45%; combined-cycle gas plants over 60%.
### Lessons
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hotter source", "b": "higher possible efficiency"}, {"a": "Colder sink", "b": "higher possible efficiency"}, {"a": "Real engines", "b": "always below Carnot limit"}]}
### Why it matters
Carnot's insight explains why power stations run hot and why combined-cycle plants reuse exhaust heat in a second cycle.
> Even a perfect engine wastes heat; the temperature gap sets the limit.

# Heat engines and heat pumps
Heat engines and heat pumps use the same physics in opposite directions.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Heat engine", "items": ["heat flows hot to cold", "produces work", "car engines, power stations"]}, "r": {"t": "Heat pump", "items": ["uses work", "moves heat cold to hot", "fridges, heat pumps, air conditioners"]}}
### Coefficient of performance
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "COP = heat delivered ÷ work input", "items": [{"s": "Typical heat pump", "m": "3–4"}]}

A heat pump doesn't create heat; it moves existing heat from outdoors. That's why it can deliver more heat energy than the electricity it uses.
### Reversible units
Air conditioners and heat pumps are often the same machine running in reverse, heating in winter and cooling in summer.
> Engines turn heat into work; heat pumps turn work into moved heat.

# Specific heat and storage
Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to warm 1 kg of a material by 1 °C. Water's is unusually high.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "specific heat, kJ/kg·°C", "items": [{"l": "Steel", "v": 0.49}, {"l": "Concrete", "v": 0.88}, {"l": "Air", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Water", "v": 4.18, "hi": true}]}
### Water as storage
ex: 200 litre tank heated by 40 °C | 200 × 4.18 × 40 | ≈ 33 MJ ≈ 9.3 kWh stored
### Thermal mass
Heavy stone and concrete absorb heat slowly and release it slowly. Thick-walled Alentejo houses stay cool in summer because their walls soak up daytime heat.
### Climate
Oceans store enormous heat, moderating coastal climates. That's why Lisbon is milder than inland Madrid.
> Water and stone are batteries for heat.

# Phase changes
Melting and boiling absorb large amounts of energy without changing temperature. This "latent heat" is released when the change reverses.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy for 1 kg of water, MJ", "items": [{"l": "Heat 0 → 100 °C", "v": 0.42}, {"l": "Melt ice", "v": 0.33}, {"l": "Boil to steam", "v": 2.26, "hi": true}]}
### Why sweating cools
Evaporating sweat absorbs latent heat from your skin. In humid air, sweat evaporates poorly, so heat feels worse.
### Steam burns
Steam at 100 °C burns worse than boiling water, because condensing on skin releases its large latent heat.

# Moisture content
Wood contains water. As it dries, it shrinks until it balances with the surrounding air's humidity.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 60.0, "unit": "% moisture", "items": [{"l": "Kiln-dried furniture", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Indoor equilibrium", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Fibre saturation", "v": 28.0}, {"l": "Green timber", "v": 50.0}]}
### Why dry wood matters
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Prevents shrinkage cracks", "Stronger", "Glues and finishes work", "Resists rot and insects"]}
### Fibre saturation point
Below about 25–30% moisture, wood begins to shrink as water leaves the cell walls. Above it, only free water in cell cavities is lost, without shrinking.

Moisture meters with pins or sensors give quick readings. Indoor joinery should be at about 8–12% before installation.
> Wet wood moves; dry wood behaves.

# Significant figures
Significant figures show how precise a measurement is. Reporting extra digits implies precision you don't have.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Rough 2 m measurement reported as 2.0000 m", "r": "Report 2 m, or measure more precisely"}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "2 m", "b": "precise to about a metre"}, {"a": "2.0 m", "b": "to about 10 cm"}, {"a": "2.00 m", "b": "to about a centimetre"}, {"a": "2.000 m", "b": "to about a millimetre"}]}
### Calculations
ex: 3.2 m × 1.47 m | Calculator: 4.704 m² | Report: 4.7 m² (two significant figures)
### Engineering practice
Results shouldn't be more precise than the least precise input. Rounding too early, though, can accumulate errors; round at the end.
> More digits don't make a measurement more accurate.

# Errors add up
When parts stack together, their tolerances accumulate. Engineers analyse this "tolerance stack-up".
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Worst case = sum of tolerances; Statistical (RSS) = √(sum of squares)", "items": [{"s": "", "m": "RSS assumes random, independent variations"}]}
### Example
ex: 10 parts, each ±0.1 mm | Worst case: ±1.0 mm | Statistical: ±0.32 mm
### Choosing a method
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Worst case", "items": ["guaranteed fit", "tighter part tolerances", "costlier"]}, "r": {"t": "Statistical", "items": ["realistic", "cheaper", "tiny chance of misfit"]}}
### Reducing stack-up
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Fewer parts in the chain", "Reference from one datum", "Adjustable features", "Tighter tolerances on key parts"]}
> Tolerances compound: design the chain, not just the parts.

# Fatigue
Fatigue is failure under repeated loading at stresses well below the material's static strength. Tiny cracks grow with each cycle until the part breaks.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Repeated loading", "Microscopic crack starts at stress concentration", "Grows a little each cycle", "Remaining section too small", "Sudden fracture"]}
### How common
Fatigue is estimated to cause the majority of mechanical failures in machines and structures.
### History
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1842", "l": "Versailles rail disaster from axle failure"}, {"y": "1850s–60s", "l": "August Wöhler studies fatigue"}, {"y": "1954", "l": "Comet crashes"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "fatigue design standard practice"}]}
### S-N curves
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Higher stress", "b": "fewer cycles to failure"}, {"a": "Lower stress", "b": "more cycles"}, {"a": "Endurance limit", "b": "some steels have a stress below which fatigue doesn't occur"}]}
### Prevention
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Smooth shapes", "Good surface finish", "Avoid notches", "Inspection for cracks", "Lower stress ranges"]}
> Small loads repeated millions of times can break what one big load cannot.

# Corrosion
Corrosion destroys metal gradually, reducing its cross-section and creating stress concentrations where cracks can start.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Uniform rusting", "Pitting", "Galvanic corrosion", "Crevice corrosion", "Stress corrosion cracking"]}
### Galvanic corrosion
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Two dissimilar metals in contact", "Electrolyte (water, salt)", "Less noble metal corrodes faster"]}
Steel bolts in aluminium, or copper touching galvanised steel, can corrode rapidly without insulation.
### Corrosion fatigue
Corrosion and fatigue together are worse than either alone: corrosion creates pits that start fatigue cracks.
### Coastal Portugal
Salt-laden air along Portugal's coast accelerates corrosion of steel structures, railings and cars.
> Corrosion and fatigue are a dangerous partnership.

> The Comet taught aviation to fear fatigue.

# Redundancy
