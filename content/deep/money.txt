== mo-basics

# What money does
Economists define money by what it does, not what it's made of. Anything that does these three jobs well enough can serve as money: shells, cigarettes, gold or numbers in a bank database.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Money", "items": ["Medium of exchange: you can pay with it", "Unit of account: prices are measured in it", "Store of value: it keeps worth over time"]}
### When a job fails
• In hyperinflation, money fails as a store of value: people spend it instantly or switch to dollars
• In prisoner-of-war camps, cigarettes became the unit of account and medium of exchange
• Bitcoin works poorly as a unit of account because its price swings so much
### Why a unit of account matters
Without a common measure, every good would need a price in every other good. With 1,000 goods, that's nearly 500,000 exchange rates; with money, just 1,000 prices.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Without money", "items": ["price of fish in bread, in shoes, in wine..."]}, "r": {"t": "With money", "items": ["one price per good, in euros"]}}
> Money is a social agreement: it works because everyone expects everyone else to accept it.

# From barter to apps
Barter requires a "double coincidence of wants": the person with what you want must also want what you have. Money removed that problem and let trade expand enormously.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 600 BC", "l": "first coins in Lydia"}, {"y": "11th c.", "l": "paper money in China"}, {"y": "1999–2002", "l": "euro launched"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "MB Way in Portugal"}]}
### Steps in the story
• Commodity money: shells, salt, cattle, metal by weight
• Coins: standard weight and stamp, so no need to weigh
• Paper notes: receipts for gold held by banks or goldsmiths
• Bank money: cheques, transfers, cards
• Digital: instant payments, mobile apps, contactless
### Did barter economies exist?
Anthropologists have found few societies that ran mainly on barter. Many communities used credit and obligations ("you owe me one") long before coins.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Credit and favours", "Commodity money", "Coins", "Paper", "Bank deposits", "Apps"]}
!? Portugal's escudo was replaced by the euro at a fixed rate of 200.482 escudos per euro; notes and coins arrived on 1 January 2002.

# Fiat money
"Fiat" means "let it be done" in Latin. Fiat money has value because a government declares it legal tender and people trust it, not because it can be exchanged for gold.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gold standard", "items": ["notes convertible into gold", "supply limited by gold"]}, "r": {"t": "Fiat money", "items": ["no backing", "supply managed by central banks"]}}
### Where value comes from
• Legal tender: it must be accepted to settle debts
• Taxes: the state demands payment in its currency
• Trust in institutions and in stable prices
### The end of gold
In August 1971, US President Nixon ended the convertibility of dollars into gold, effectively ending the Bretton Woods system. Since then, all major currencies have been fiat.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1870s", "l": "classical gold standard spreads"}, {"y": "1944", "l": "Bretton Woods"}, {"y": "1971", "l": "dollar–gold link ended"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "euro launched"}]}
### The risk
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Strength", "b": "flexible response to crises"}, {"a": "Weakness", "b": "governments can print too much"}, {"a": "Safeguard", "b": "independent central banks with inflation targets"}]}
!! Money backed by gold is safer => the gold standard caused rigid policy and worsened depressions such as the 1930s

# Most money is digital
Cash is only a small part of the money in the economy. Most money exists as bank deposits: numbers in accounts, created mainly when banks make loans.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "euro-area money (M3), approx. %", "items": [{"l": "Notes and coins", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Bank deposits and other", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}]}
### How banks create money
When a bank grants you a €100,000 mortgage, it doesn't hand over other customers' savings. It credits your account with a new deposit. New money has been created; it disappears when the loan is repaid.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bank approves loan", "New deposit created", "Money spent and moves between banks", "Loan repaid", "Money destroyed"]}
### What limits it
• Demand for loans from creditworthy borrowers
• Capital and liquidity rules for banks
• Interest rates set by the central bank
### Official confirmation
The Bank of England explained this in a widely cited 2014 article, "Money creation in the modern economy", correcting the textbook picture of banks just lending out savings.
> Every time a bank lends, it creates money; every time a loan is repaid, money disappears.

# Central banks
A central bank manages a currency: it sets key interest rates, supervises banks and acts as lender of last resort. For the euro, that's the European Central Bank (ECB) in Frankfurt.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["ECB sets rates", "Bank lending rates move", "Borrowing and spending change", "Inflation moves toward 2%"]}
### The Eurosystem
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Eurosystem", "items": ["ECB: Frankfurt", "National central banks: Banco de Portugal, Bundesbank, Banque de France and others", "Governing Council: sets policy for the euro area"]}
### The 2% target
Since its 2021 strategy review, the ECB aims for inflation of 2% over the medium term, treating deviations above and below as equally undesirable. A little inflation is preferred to zero because it gives room to cut rates and avoids deflation.

• Supervises Portuguese banks and payment systems

# Inflation
Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. It's measured with a basket of typical goods and services; in Portugal, INE publishes the consumer price index (CPI) monthly.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "Portugal consumer price inflation, % (INE)", "items": [{"l": "2015", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "2019", "v": 0.3}, {"l": "2021", "v": 1.3}, {"l": "2022", "v": 7.8, "hi": true}, {"l": "2023", "v": 4.3}, {"l": "2024", "v": 2.4}]}
### Causes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Demand-pull", "b": "too much spending chasing too few goods"}, {"a": "Cost-push", "b": "energy or food prices rise"}, {"a": "Expectations", "b": "workers and firms anticipate price rises"}, {"a": "Money growth", "b": "sustained excess money creation"}]}
### The 2021–2023 surge
Reopening after COVID, supply chain disruptions and the energy shock after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 pushed euro-area inflation above 10% in late 2022.
### Hyperinflation
• Germany, 1923: prices doubled every few days
• Hungary, 1946: the worst recorded, prices doubled roughly every 15 hours
• Zimbabwe, 2008: 100-trillion-dollar notes
> At 2% a year, prices double in about 35 years: steady, small inflation still adds up.

# The rule of 72
The rule of 72 is a quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by a yearly percentage to estimate how many years it takes something to double.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Years to double ≈ 72 / r", "items": [{"s": "r", "m": "yearly rate in percent"}, {"s": "72", "m": "works well for rates of about 2–15%"}]}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "years to double", "items": [{"l": "2% inflation", "v": 36.0}, {"l": "4% savings", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "7% stocks", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "18% card debt", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why 72?
The exact number is about 69.3 (from the natural log of 2), but 72 is close and divides easily by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12.
### It works in reverse
• To double in 10 years you need about 7.2% a year
• Prices rising 6% a year halve your cash's buying power in about 12 years
ex: 72 / 6% = 12 years | 72 / 3% = 24 years | 72 / 9% = 8 years
!? Variants exist: some use 70 for low rates and 69.3 for continuous compounding.

# Interest
Interest is the price of using someone else's money for a while. Borrowers pay it; savers and lenders earn it.

### What sets the rate
• Central bank rates: the baseline
• Risk: riskier borrowers pay more
• Time: longer loans usually cost more
• Inflation expectations: lenders want compensation

# Supply and demand
Prices are set where the quantity people want to buy meets the quantity sellers want to sell. When either side shifts, prices move.

### Examples
• Summer flights and August hotels in the Algarve cost more: demand peaks
• Strawberries are cheap in season: supply is plentiful
• Concert tickets sell out and resale prices soar when demand far exceeds seats
• Olive oil prices roughly doubled in 2023–24 after droughts cut Spanish harvests

# Inflation eats cash
Money that sits idle loses buying power every year inflation is positive. Cash under the mattress is "safe" only in nominal terms.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "buying power of €10,000 at 3% inflation, €", "items": [{"l": "Today", "v": 10000.0}, {"l": "5 years", "v": 8626.0}, {"l": "10 years", "v": 7441.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "20 years", "v": 5537.0}]}
### Real value
= Real value = amount / (1 + inflation)^years
ex: €10,000 / 1.03^10 | = €10,000 / 1.344 | ≈ €7,441
### Protecting savings
• Interest-bearing savings accounts or deposits
• Portuguese government savings products (Certificados de Aforro, Certificados do Tesouro)
• Long-term investments for money you won't need for years
### But keep some cash
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too much cash", "items": ["loses value"]}, "r": {"t": "Too little cash", "items": ["no buffer for emergencies"]}}
Keep an emergency fund in easy-access savings, and put longer-term money to work.
> Idle cash has a hidden cost: inflation.

== mo-budget

# Pay yourself first
"Pay yourself first" means moving money to savings as soon as your salary arrives, before spending on anything else. Saving what's left at month-end rarely works, because there's rarely anything left.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Save the leftovers", "items": ["spend first", "save if anything remains"]}, "r": {"t": "Pay yourself first", "items": ["save first", "spend the rest freely"]}}
### Automate it
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Salary arrives", "Automatic transfer to savings", "Bills paid", "Spend the rest guilt-free"]}
### Why it works
• Removes the daily decision and willpower
• You adapt spending to what's in the current account
• Savings grow steadily, even in small amounts
### Start small
Even 5% of take-home pay builds the habit. Increase it by one or two percentage points each year or with each raise.
!? Behavioural economists Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi's "Save More Tomorrow" scheme, which commits future raises to savings, lifted participants' savings rates from about 3.5% to over 13% in a few years.

The 50/30/20 rule, popularised by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book "All Your Worth", is a simple starting split of take-home pay.

# Needs vs wants
Needs are expenses you must pay to live and work. Wants make life nicer but are optional. Many purchases mix both.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Housing", "b": "need, but a bigger flat is a want"}, {"a": "Transport", "b": "need, but a new car is often a want"}, {"a": "Food", "b": "need, but takeaway is a want"}, {"a": "Phone", "b": "need, but the latest model is a want"}]}
### Spotting the want part
Ask: what's the cheapest way to meet this need? The difference between that and what you spend is the want.
ex: Monthly phone plan €35 | Cheapest adequate plan €12 | Want part: €23 a month = €276 a year
### Not about deprivation
• Wants are good: they make life enjoyable
• The goal is choosing wants consciously
• Cut wants you don't value to fund ones you do
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Need: rent", "Need: groceries", "Mixed: car", "Mixed: clothes", "Want: streaming", "Want: holidays"]}
> Spend freely on what you love; cut ruthlessly on what you don't.

# Emergency fund
An emergency fund is money set aside for unexpected costs or loss of income. It's the foundation of financial stability.

### What it's for
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Job loss", "Car or home repairs", "Medical or dental costs", "Urgent travel", "Broken appliances"]}
### How much
• Stable job, no dependants: around three months
• Self-employed, single income, or children: six months or more
• Count essential expenses, not total spending
### Building it
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Start with €500–1,000", "Then one month of expenses", "Then three", "Then six"]}

Keep it in an easy-access savings account at a bank covered by the deposit guarantee. Investments can fall just when you need the money.
!? Without an emergency fund, a broken car often ends up on a credit card at high interest, turning a one-off cost into months of debt.

# Mental accounting
Mental accounting, a concept developed by economist Richard Thaler, describes how people treat money differently depending on its source or label, even though money is interchangeable.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"My tax refund is free money to spend\"", "r": "\"A euro is a euro: it could pay debt or build savings\""}]}
### Common examples
• Spending a bonus freely while carrying credit card debt
• Keeping savings at 1% while paying 18% on a card
• Splurging with casino winnings ("house money")
• Treating refunds as gifts rather than your own money
### Using it to your advantage
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Separate accounts", "b": "labelled pots make goals real"}, {"a": "Savings pots", "b": "\"holiday\", \"car\", \"emergency\""}, {"a": "Windfalls", "b": "commit in advance to save half"}]}
### Nobel recognition
Thaler received the 2017 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his work in behavioural economics, including mental accounting.
> Treat every euro the same, whatever its source.

== mo-compound

# Simple vs compound
Simple interest pays only on your original amount. Compound interest pays on the original plus all interest already earned, so growth accelerates over time.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simple", "items": ["interest on original only", "straight-line growth"]}, "r": {"t": "Compound", "items": ["interest on interest", "growth curves upward"]}}
### Side by side
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "€1,000 at 5% after 30 years, €", "items": [{"l": "Simple", "v": 2500.0}, {"l": "Compound", "v": 4322.0, "hi": true}]}
### Year by year
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Year 1", "b": "€50 interest either way"}, {"a": "Year 10 compound", "b": "about €78 interest that year"}, {"a": "Year 30 compound", "b": "about €206 interest that year"}]}
With simple interest, the yearly interest stays €50 forever.
### Compounding frequency
Interest can compound yearly, monthly or daily. More frequent compounding gives slightly more; the effective annual rate (TAE in Portugal) shows the true yearly result.
> Compounding is slow at first and powerful later: time is its fuel.

# The formula
Compound growth multiplies your money by the same factor each year. The formula shows why rate and time matter so much.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "FV = PV × (1 + r)^n", "items": [{"s": "FV", "m": "future value"}, {"s": "PV", "m": "amount today"}, {"s": "r", "m": "yearly rate"}, {"s": "n", "m": "number of years"}]}
### Sensitivity
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "€10,000 after 30 years, € thousands", "items": [{"l": "3%", "v": 24.0}, {"l": "5%", "v": 43.0}, {"l": "7%", "v": 76.0, "hi": true}]}
A two-point difference in yearly return nearly doubles the final amount over 30 years.
### Working backwards
= PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
ex: Want €50,000 in 20 years at 5% | PV = 50,000 / 1.05^20 | ≈ €18,840 needed today
### Spreadsheet tip
• Excel and Google Sheets: =FV(rate, years, payment, -present value)
• Or simply: =amount*(1+rate)^years
> The exponent is where the magic lives: n matters more than you expect.

@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "First 10 years", "b": "gain of €629"}, {"a": "Years 30 to 40", "b": "gain of €2,718"}]}

> In compounding, the best years are always at the end.

# Compounding debt
Compounding works against you when you owe money. High-interest debt grows quickly if unpaid.

By the rule of 72, debt at 18% doubles in about four years. Credit card and some consumer loan rates in Portugal can exceed 15–20% TAEG.
### Why it's dangerous
• Minimum payments may barely cover interest
• Late fees add to the balance
• The longer it runs, the faster it grows

# Nominal vs real
Nominal returns are the numbers you see. Real returns subtract inflation, showing how much your buying power actually grew.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Real ≈ nominal − inflation", "items": [{"s": "Nominal", "m": "stated return"}, {"s": "Inflation", "m": "rise in prices"}, {"s": "Real", "m": "growth in buying power"}]}
### Precise version
= Real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1
ex: (1.05 / 1.03) − 1 | = 1.94% | ≈ 2% real
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "2% deposit, 2% inflation", "b": "0% real"}, {"a": "2% deposit, 7.8% inflation", "b": "about −5.4% real"}, {"a": "7% shares, 2% inflation", "b": "about 5% real"}]}
The middle case is Portugal in 2022, when inflation reached 7.8%.
### Why it matters
• Long-term goals are about what money can buy, not the number of euros
• Cash that looks safe can lose real value
• Compare investment returns with inflation, not zero
> Always ask: after inflation, did I actually get richer?

# Diversification
Diversification spreads your money across many investments so that a single failure doesn't sink you. It's often called the only free lunch in investing.

Different investments don't all fall at the same time or by the same amount. Combining them smooths overall returns.
### Home bias
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Home bias", "items": ["most money in your own country"]}, "r": {"t": "Global", "items": ["Portugal is a tiny fraction of 1% of world markets"]}}

• In crashes, many assets fall together
• Diversification reduces risk but doesn't eliminate it
• Too many overlapping funds add cost without adding diversification

# Comparative advantage
Countries gain from trade by specialising in what they're relatively best at, even if one country is better at everything. This is comparative advantage.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Portugal (Ricardo's example)", "items": ["wine: 80 workers", "cloth: 90"]}, "r": {"t": "England", "items": ["wine: 120 workers", "cloth: 100"]}}
### Ricardo's insight
In David Ricardo's 1817 example, Portugal is better at producing both wine and cloth. But it's relatively better at wine, while England's disadvantage is smaller in cloth. If Portugal makes wine and England cloth, and they trade, both have more.
### Absolute vs comparative
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Absolute advantage", "b": "produce more with the same resources"}, {"a": "Comparative advantage", "b": "lower opportunity cost"}]}
### Real history
• The 1703 Methuen Treaty between England and Portugal lowered duties on Portuguese wine and English cloth
• Port wine became an English favourite
• Some historians argue it held back Portuguese manufacturing
> Specialise in what you give up least to do, and trade for the rest.

# Tragedy of the commons
When a shared resource is open to all, each user has an incentive to take more, even though overuse harms everyone. Ecologist Garrett Hardin popularised the idea in 1968.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Shared fishing ground", "Each boat catches more", "Fish stocks fall", "Catches collapse", "Everyone loses"]}
### Classic example
In 1992, Canada closed the Grand Banks cod fishery after stocks collapsed, costing around 30,000 jobs. The cod have still not fully recovered.
### Solutions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Quotas", "Property or fishing rights", "Taxes and fees", "Community management", "International agreements"]}
### Ostrom's discovery
Economist Elinor Ostrom, who won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics, showed that many communities successfully manage commons for centuries through local rules, monitoring and sanctions.
### Portugal
Portuguese sardine catches have been strictly limited in recent years under quotas agreed with Spain after stocks fell to historic lows, and stocks have since recovered.
> Shared resources need shared rules.

# Reading a headline
Economic headlines are easy to misread. "Inflation falls" doesn't mean prices fall; it means they rise more slowly.

# Good and bad debt
Debt isn't automatically bad. It lets you use future income today. Whether that helps depends on what you buy, what it costs and whether you can comfortably repay it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Usually useful", "items": ["home", "education", "business investment"]}, "r": {"t": "Usually risky", "items": ["holidays", "gadgets", "consumer goods on credit cards"]}}
### Questions to ask
• Will what I'm buying last longer than the loan?
• Will it increase my income or reduce costs?
• Can I afford the payments if my income drops?
• What's the total cost including interest?
### Leverage cuts both ways
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Borrow to buy asset", "Asset rises: gains magnified", "Asset falls: losses magnified, debt remains"]}
### "Good" debt can go bad
• Too large a mortgage leaves no room for rate rises
• Student loans for courses with weak job prospects
• Business loans for an untested idea
> The question isn't "is debt good?" but "is this debt worth its cost and risk for me?"

# The real cost: APR
The headline interest rate rarely tells the full story. The APR, called TAEG in Portugal, combines interest and most fees into one yearly rate, so offers can be compared fairly.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "TAN", "b": "nominal annual interest rate only"}, {"a": "TAEG", "b": "annual rate including fees, insurance and charges"}, {"a": "MTIC", "b": "total amount payable by the consumer"}]}
### Example
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Loan A", "items": ["TAN 6%", "high fees", "TAEG 8.5%"]}, "r": {"t": "Loan B", "items": ["TAN 6.5%", "low fees", "TAEG 7.1%"]}}
Loan B looks dearer by its rate but is actually cheaper overall.
### What's included
• Interest
• Opening and processing fees
• Mandatory insurance linked to the loan
• Account maintenance fees required by the loan
### Your rights
EU consumer credit rules require lenders to give you a standard information sheet (in Portugal, the FIN or Ficha de Informação Normalizada) before you sign, and give a 14-day right to withdraw from consumer credit agreements.
> Always compare TAEG and the total amount payable, not the monthly payment.

# Minimum payments trap
Credit card minimum payments are designed to keep you paying interest for a long time. Paying only the minimum can take years and cost far more than the original purchase.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Balance €3,000", "Pay minimum", "Most goes to interest", "Balance shrinks slowly", "Years of payments"]}
### Why it's so slow
At 18% APR, a €3,000 balance generates about €45 of interest each month. If the minimum is little more than that, the debt barely moves.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "months to repay €3,000 at 18%", "items": [{"l": "€60 a month", "v": 94.0}, {"l": "€100 a month", "v": 41.0}, {"l": "€200 a month", "v": 18.0, "hi": true}]}
### Breaking the trap
• Pay as much above the minimum as you can
• Set up an automatic fixed payment, not the minimum
• Stop adding new charges to the card
• Consider a cheaper personal loan to clear it
### Revolving credit
In Portugal, many cards use "crédito revolving": the unpaid balance rolls over and accrues interest. Paying the full statement balance each month avoids interest entirely.
!! Paying the minimum keeps you in good standing so it's fine => it keeps you in debt for years at very high cost

# Debt-to-income ratio
The debt service-to-income ratio (DSTI) compares monthly debt payments with net monthly income. It's one of lenders' main checks on whether you can afford a new loan.

# Banks borrow and lend
Banks are intermediaries: they take deposits and borrow from markets, then lend to households and businesses at higher rates. The gap pays for costs and generates profit.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Savers deposit at 2%", "Bank lends at 5%", "Net interest margin 3%", "Covers costs, losses and profit"]}
### Maturity transformation
Banks borrow short-term (deposits you can withdraw any time) and lend long-term (30-year mortgages). This is useful for the economy but creates risk if depositors all want their money at once.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bank liabilities", "items": ["deposits", "short-term", "withdrawable"]}, "r": {"t": "Bank assets", "items": ["loans", "long-term", "hard to sell quickly"]}}
### Sources of profit
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Net interest income", "Fees and commissions", "Trading", "Asset management", "Insurance sales"]}
### When rates rise
Banks usually earn more as lending rates rise faster than deposit rates. Portuguese banks posted record profits in 2023 as Euribor jumped while deposit rates rose slowly.
> A bank's business is managing the gap between what it pays and what it earns, and the risks that come with it.

# Money creation
When a bank makes a loan, it doesn't hand out other people's savings. It creates a new deposit in the borrower's account. That's how most money in a modern economy comes into existence.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bank approves €200,000 mortgage", "Credits seller's bank account", "New money exists", "Repaid over 30 years", "Money disappears"]}
### What limits it
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Demand from creditworthy borrowers", "Capital requirements", "Liquidity rules", "Central bank interest rates", "Banks' own risk appetite"]}
### Textbook vs reality
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old textbook", "items": ["banks lend out existing savings"]}, "r": {"t": "Reality", "items": ["loans create deposits", "savings follow"]}}
### Why it matters
• Credit booms expand the money supply and can fuel bubbles
• Credit crunches shrink lending and slow the economy
• Central banks influence money creation through interest rates, not by fixing quantities
!? The Bank of England's 2014 paper "Money creation in the modern economy" helped make this mainstream understanding.

# Fractional reserves
Banks hold only a small fraction of deposits as cash or central bank reserves. The rest is tied up in loans and investments.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "where a bank's assets sit, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Loans", "v": 65.0}, {"l": "Securities", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Reserves and cash", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Other", "v": 5.0}]}
### Why it normally works
On any given day, only a small share of depositors withdraw money, and new deposits come in. Banks also hold liquid assets they can sell or borrow against.
### Liquidity rules
After 2008, the Basel III rules added the Liquidity Coverage Ratio: banks must hold enough high-quality liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows.
### When it fails
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Doubt about bank", "Depositors withdraw", "Bank sells assets at a loss", "More doubt", "Run"]}
### The euro-area reserve requirement
Euro-area banks must hold 1% of certain deposits as minimum reserves at the central bank, a requirement in place since 2012.
> Banks are safe in normal times because not everyone wants their money back at once.

# Deposit guarantee
In the EU, bank deposits are protected up to €100,000 per depositor, per bank. If a bank fails, a deposit guarantee scheme pays back covered deposits.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "€100,000", "l": "per depositor, per bank"}, {"v": "7", "l": "working days to pay out (EU target)"}, {"v": "Fundo de Garantia de Depósitos", "l": "scheme in Portugal"}]}
### What's covered
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Current and savings accounts", "b": "covered"}, {"a": "Term deposits", "b": "covered"}, {"a": "Shares and bonds", "b": "not covered"}, {"a": "Investment funds", "b": "not covered"}, {"a": "Bank's commercial paper", "b": "not covered"}]}
### Per bank, not per brand
Check whether two banks share one licence. If they do, the €100,000 limit applies once across both.
### Joint accounts
Joint accounts are usually split equally among holders, so each holder is covered up to €100,000.

When Banco Espírito Santo collapsed in 2014, deposits were protected, but many retail investors lost money on commercial paper and bonds sold to them at bank branches, which were not deposits.

# Bank runs
A bank run happens when many depositors try to withdraw money at once, fearing the bank will fail. Even a healthy bank can be brought down because its money is tied up in loans.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2007", "l": "Northern Rock queues in the UK"}, {"y": "2008", "l": "global financial crisis"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "BES resolved in Portugal"}, {"y": "2023", "l": "SVB in the US"}]}
### Silicon Valley Bank
In March 2023, depositors withdrew about $42 billion from SVB in a single day, and requested more the next morning. Concerns about losses on its bond portfolio spread quickly on social media and messaging apps. Regulators closed it on 10 March.
### Why runs are faster now
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "2007", "items": ["queues outside branches", "days"]}, "r": {"t": "2023", "items": ["mobile apps", "tweets", "hours"]}}
### Defences
• Deposit guarantees reduce the incentive to run
• Central banks lend to solvent banks in a crisis
• Liquidity rules require buffers
• Resolution regimes allow orderly failure
> Runs are about confidence: guarantees work largely by making panic unnecessary.

# Capital requirements
Capital is the bank's own money: shareholders' equity that absorbs losses before depositors or taxpayers are hurt. After 2008, regulators required much more of it.

### Basel III
The international Basel III rules, agreed after the 2008 crisis, raised the quality and quantity of capital banks must hold and added a leverage ratio, a minimum of 3% capital against total exposures regardless of risk.

EU rules since 2016 require losses to be borne by shareholders and certain creditors before public money is used. That's "bail-in" instead of "bail-out".

# The 4% rule
The 4% rule comes from US studies in the 1990s by financial planner William Bengen and the "Trinity study". It estimates how much retirees can withdraw from savings without running out.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "starting yearly income from €500,000, €", "items": [{"l": "3%", "v": 15000.0}, {"l": "4%", "v": 20000.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "5%", "v": 25000.0}]}
### How it works
Withdraw 4% of your portfolio in year one, then the same amount adjusted for inflation each year. Historically, in US data, this lasted at least 30 years in almost all periods.
### Caveats
• Based on US stock and bond returns, which were unusually strong
• Fees reduce what's sustainable
• Retirements longer than 30 years need lower rates
• Bad returns early in retirement are especially damaging
### The reverse

# What Bitcoin is
Bitcoin is a digital currency that works without a central bank or company. A network of computers maintains a public ledger of all transactions, the blockchain.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Oct 2008", "l": "Satoshi's white paper"}, {"y": "Jan 2009", "l": "first block mined"}, {"y": "2010", "l": "first purchase, two pizzas"}, {"y": "2024", "l": "US spot Bitcoin ETFs approved"}]}
### Satoshi Nakamoto
Bitcoin's creator used the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto and disappeared from public view around 2011. Their identity remains unknown.
### Key features
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No central authority", "Fixed supply", "Public ledger", "Pseudonymous addresses", "Irreversible transactions"]}
### The pizza
On 22 May 2010, programmer Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas, the first known real-world purchase. At 2025 prices, those bitcoins would be worth over a billion dollars.
### Why it appeared then
The first block contained a newspaper headline about bank bailouts from January 2009, suggesting a response to the financial crisis.
> Bitcoin's innovation was solving how strangers can agree on a ledger without trusting a central party.

# The blockchain
A blockchain is a chain of blocks, each containing transactions and a cryptographic fingerprint (hash) of the previous block. Changing any old block breaks every link after it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Block 1", "Block 2 contains hash of block 1", "Block 3 contains hash of block 2", "Tampering with block 1 breaks the chain"]}
### Why it's hard to cheat
To change a past transaction, an attacker would need to redo the work for that block and all later blocks, faster than the rest of the network combined.
### Hashes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hash", "b": "fixed-length fingerprint of data"}, {"a": "Tiny change", "b": "completely different hash"}, {"a": "One-way", "b": "can't recover data from hash"}]}
### Beyond Bitcoin
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ethereum smart contracts", "Supply chain tracking", "Digital identity", "Tokenised assets"]}
### Limits
• Public blockchains are slow and costly compared with databases
• Many "blockchain" projects didn't need one
• Data on the chain is only as good as what's entered
!! Blockchain makes any data true => it only proves data hasn't changed since being recorded, not that it was correct

# Mining
Bitcoin miners compete to add the next block by solving a puzzle: finding a number that makes the block's hash meet a target. It takes enormous computing effort; checking a solution is instant.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Collect transactions", "Guess numbers trillions of times", "Find valid hash", "Broadcast block", "Earn reward"]}
### Proof of work
The work makes attacks expensive: to rewrite history, you'd need more computing power than the rest of the network.
### Energy use
Bitcoin mining uses electricity on the scale of a mid-sized country, roughly 100–200 TWh a year by Cambridge University estimates.
### Proof of stake
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Proof of work", "items": ["computing power", "high energy"]}, "r": {"t": "Proof of stake", "items": ["coins locked as collateral", "far less energy"]}}
Ethereum switched from proof of work to proof of stake in September 2022, cutting its energy use by over 99.9%.
### Difficulty
The network adjusts difficulty about every two weeks so that blocks arrive roughly every ten minutes, however many miners join.
> Mining turns electricity into security for the ledger.

# Limited supply
Bitcoin's rules cap total supply at 21 million coins. New coins enter through mining rewards, which halve about every four years.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "21 million", "l": "maximum bitcoins"}, {"v": "3.125", "l": "BTC reward per block since 2024"}, {"v": "~2140", "l": "last bitcoin mined"}]}
### Halvings
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mining reward, BTC per block", "items": [{"l": "2009", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "2012", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "2016", "v": 12.5}, {"l": "2020", "v": 6.25}, {"l": "2024", "v": 3.125, "hi": true}]}
### Scarcity argument
Supporters compare Bitcoin to digital gold: scarce, so it can't be inflated away. Critics note scarcity alone doesn't create value; demand does.

Millions of bitcoins are believed lost forever through forgotten passwords and discarded hard drives, making the effective supply smaller.

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No cash flows to anchor value", "Speculative demand", "Leverage on exchanges", "News and regulation", "Small, concentrated holders"]}

# Present bias
Present bias is our tendency to give too much weight to rewards now compared with rewards later. It explains why saving, dieting and studying are hard: costs are immediate, benefits distant.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Choice 1", "items": ["€100 today or €110 in a week", "most take €100"]}, "r": {"t": "Choice 2", "items": ["€100 in 52 weeks or €110 in 53", "most wait"]}}
### Why it's inconsistent
In both choices, waiting one week earns €10. A perfectly consistent person would choose the same way. Economists call this pattern "hyperbolic discounting".
### Where it shows up
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Under-saving for retirement", "Credit card spending", "Procrastinating on admin", "Skipping exercise", "Delaying health checks"]}
### Strategies
• Commitment devices: lock money into savings or pensions
• Automatic transfers that happen before you can spend
• Make future rewards vivid: name savings pots after goals
• Remove instant temptations: delete saved card details
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plan when calm", "Commit in advance", "Automate", "Future self benefits"]}
> Make decisions for your future self in advance, when the present isn't shouting.

# Mental accounting
Mental accounting means treating money as belonging to separate "pots" with different rules. It can lead to choices that cost real money.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "yearly cost of keeping both, € (example)", "items": [{"l": "Card debt €3,000 at 18%", "v": 540.0}, {"l": "Savings €3,000 at 2%", "v": 60.0}]}
### The cost
ex: Pay 18% on €3,000 of card debt = €540 a year | Earn 2% on €3,000 savings = €60 a year | Net loss: €480 a year
Using the savings to clear the card would save €480 a year, then rebuild savings with the money no longer going to interest.
### Why people do it
• Savings feel "untouchable", even for a better use
• Fear of having no buffer
• Different accounts, different mental labels
### When pots help
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Harmful", "items": ["high-cost debt alongside idle savings"]}, "r": {"t": "Helpful", "items": ["labelled savings goals", "holiday fund", "emergency fund"]}}
> Money is fungible: look at the whole picture, not each pot separately.

# Loss aversion in investing
Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equal gains feel good. In investing, this leads to the "disposition effect": selling winners too early and holding losers too long.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Winning share", "items": ["sell quickly to lock in gain"]}, "r": {"t": "Losing share", "items": ["hold, hoping to break even"]}}
### The evidence
Studies of brokerage accounts, notably by economist Terrance Odean in 1998, found investors were much more likely to sell shares that had risen than ones that had fallen, and that the losers they kept did worse than the winners they sold.
### Why it hurts
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Taxes: realising gains, not losses", "Losers often keep falling", "Winners often keep rising", "Portfolio drifts to losers"]}
### Better questions
• Would I buy this share today at this price?
• Is the reason I bought it still valid?
• Am I anchored to the price I paid?
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid\"", "r": "\"The price I paid is irrelevant to what happens next\""}]}
> The market doesn't know or care what you paid.

# Herding
Herding is following what others do, assuming they know something. In markets, it amplifies booms and crashes.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Prices rise", "Media buzz", "More buyers join", "Prices rise further", "Doubt", "Selling", "Panic", "Crash"]}
### Why we herd
• Social proof: if everyone's doing it, it feels safe
• Fear of missing out (FOMO)
• Career risk: fund managers prefer being wrong together to wrong alone
• Information cascades: people copy rather than research
### Examples
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Tulip mania (1637)", "Dot-com bubble (2000)", "US housing (2006)", "Meme stocks (2021)", "Crypto booms"]}
### Buffett's advice
Warren Buffett famously advises being "fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful".
!? Social media and trading apps can accelerate herding, as in the 2021 GameStop surge driven by online forums.

# Overconfidence and trading
Most people believe they're above-average investors. Overconfidence leads to frequent trading, which typically lowers returns through costs and poor timing.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "yearly return 1991–96, % (Barber & Odean)", "items": [{"l": "Market", "v": 17.9}, {"l": "Average household", "v": 16.4}, {"l": "Most active traders", "v": 11.4, "hi": true}]}
### The study
Economists Brad Barber and Terrance Odean analysed over 66,000 US households' brokerage accounts. Their 2000 paper, "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth", found the most active fifth of traders earned 11.4% a year, against 17.9% for the market.

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Commissions and spreads", "Taxes on gains", "Buying after rises", "Selling after falls", "Chasing hot tips"]}
### Gender differences
A follow-up study found men traded 45% more than women and reduced their returns more as a result, consistent with greater overconfidence.

# Recency bias
Recency bias makes us assume recent trends will continue. After strong years, investors pile in; after crashes, they flee, often at exactly the wrong moments.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Strong year", "Investors buy at high prices", "Correction", "Panic selling at low prices", "Miss the recovery"]}
### The behaviour gap
Morningstar's annual "Mind the Gap" studies find that investors' actual returns lag the returns of the funds they own, by about one percentage point a year, because of badly timed buying and selling.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "10-year yearly returns, % (approx., Morningstar 2024)", "items": [{"l": "Funds", "v": 7.3}, {"l": "Investors in them", "v": 6.3, "hi": true}]}
### Examples
• Heavy buying of tech funds in early 2000, just before the crash
• Massive selling in March 2020, just before a rapid rebound
• Crypto buying near the 2021 peak
### Countermeasures
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Written investment plan", "Automatic monthly investing", "Rebalancing rules", "Long-term perspective", "Avoid checking daily"]}
> Last year's winner is not a forecast.

# The endowment effect
We value things more once we own them. That makes us reluctant to sell or switch, even when we should.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "median value of a mug, $ (Kahneman, Knetsch, Thaler 1990)", "items": [{"l": "Buyers would pay", "v": 2.87}, {"l": "Owners would accept", "v": 7.12, "hi": true}]}
### The experiment
Students randomly given a university mug asked for more than twice as much to sell it as other students were willing to pay to buy it. Ownership alone changed its value.
### Money examples
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Holding inherited shares", "Overpricing your house", "Keeping bad insurance", "Sticking with old bank accounts"]}
### The status quo
The endowment effect links to status quo bias: we stick with what we have. Companies exploit it with free trials, since cancelling feels like losing something.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"I'll keep it because it's mine\"", "r": "\"Would I buy this today at its market price?\""}]}
> Judge what you own as if you were deciding to buy it today.

# Nudges
A nudge changes how choices are presented without restricting them. Well-designed nudges help people act in their own long-term interest.

### Automatic enrolment
A classic 2001 study by Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea found that automatic enrolment in a US company's pension plan raised participation among new employees from about 37% to 86%.

### Save More Tomorrow
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Idea", "b": "commit future raises to savings"}, {"a": "Why it works", "b": "no pain today"}, {"a": "Result", "b": "savings rates roughly quadrupled in the first study"}]}

# Money and happiness
Does money buy happiness? Research says yes, up to a point, and much depends on how you spend it.

A joint 2023 study concluded happiness generally keeps rising with income, except for the least happy people, whose wellbeing plateaus.

== mo-history

# Tulip mania
Tulip mania in the Dutch Republic in the 1630s is often called the first speculative bubble. Rare bulbs reportedly changed hands for extraordinary prices before the market collapsed in February 1637.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1593", "l": "tulips arrive in the Netherlands"}, {"y": "1634", "l": "speculation grows"}, {"y": "Feb 1637", "l": "prices collapse"}, {"y": "1841", "l": "Mackay popularises the story"}]}
### The legend
Charles Mackay's 1841 book "Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds" told of single bulbs exchanged for houses and of economic ruin across Holland.
### The revision
Historian Anne Goldgar's 2007 research found the mania involved a relatively small group of wealthy merchants and craftsmen, and caused little wider economic damage. Many stories of ruin were moralising pamphlets.
### Why bulbs?
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Rare \"broken\" colour patterns", "Status symbol", "Slow to propagate", "Futures contracts on bulbs"]}
The prized streaked patterns were later found to be caused by a virus, making them unpredictable and scarce.
> Even the most famous bubble story was partly a bubble of exaggeration.

# The South Sea Bubble
In 1720, shares in Britain's South Sea Company, which took over government debt in exchange for trading rights, soared and then collapsed within months.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "South Sea Company share price, £ (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Jan 1720", "v": 128.0}, {"l": "Jun 1720", "v": 1050.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Dec 1720", "v": 150.0}]}
### What happened
• The company promised profits from trade with South America that never materialised
• It encouraged buying with loans and instalments
• Many other "bubble companies" floated, including one "for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is"
### Newton's loss
Isaac Newton reportedly sold early at a profit, then bought back near the top and lost around £20,000, a fortune at the time. The quote about calculating the motions of heavenly bodies but not the madness of people is attributed to him but not well documented.
### Consequences
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Bubble Act restricted new companies", "Political scandal", "Robert Walpole rose to power"]}
!? France's Mississippi Bubble, run by John Law, collapsed the same year.

# The 1929 crash
The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 ended a speculative boom and helped trigger the Great Depression, the worst economic downturn of the industrialised world.

# Hyperinflation

# Lessons from crises
Financial crises differ in details but share recurring patterns. Recognising them helps protect your money.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cheap credit", "Rising prices", "\"This time is different\"", "Leverage grows", "Shock", "Panic and crash", "Reforms"]}
### Common ingredients
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Easy credit", "Rising leverage", "Asset prices detached from income", "New-era stories", "Complex products"]}
### "This time is different"
Economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's 2009 book of that title documented eight centuries of financial crises and found the same belief before almost every one.
### Personal protection
• Diversify across assets and countries
• Avoid heavy borrowing to invest
• Keep an emergency fund
• Be sceptical when everyone's sure prices can only rise
• Hold investments you understand
> Humility and diversification have survived every crisis.

== mo-business

# Revenue, costs, profit
Every business comes down to this: money in (revenue) minus money out (costs) equals profit. Big revenue doesn't guarantee profit.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Profit = Revenue − Costs", "items": [{"s": "Revenue", "m": "sales of goods or services"}, {"s": "Costs", "m": "materials, staff, rent, interest, tax"}]}
### Layers of profit
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Net profit: after interest and tax", "Operating profit: after running costs", "Gross profit: after direct costs", "Revenue: total sales"]}
### Big but unprofitable
Many fast-growing companies have large revenues but lose money for years while investing in growth. Uber, for example, didn't post a full-year operating profit until 2023, 14 years after it was founded.
### Profit vs cash
• Profit is an accounting measure
• Cash is what's in the bank
• They can differ hugely due to timing
> Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality, as the saying goes.

# Gross and net margin
Margins show what share of each euro of sales a business keeps. Gross margin covers direct costs; net margin covers everything.

### Typical margins
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "net margin, % (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Supermarket", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Restaurant", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Luxury goods", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "Software", "v": 25.0, "hi": true}]}
### Example: a café
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Revenue", "b": "€10,000 a month"}, {"a": "Cost of coffee, milk, food", "b": "€3,000"}, {"a": "Gross margin", "b": "70%"}, {"a": "Rent, staff, energy, tax", "b": "€6,000"}, {"a": "Net margin", "b": "10%"}]}

# Fixed and variable costs
Costs behave differently as sales change. Fixed costs stay the same whatever you sell; variable costs rise with each unit.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fixed", "items": ["rent", "salaries", "insurance", "software"]}, "r": {"t": "Variable", "items": ["ingredients", "packaging", "delivery", "card fees"]}}
### Operating leverage
A business with high fixed costs and low variable costs, like a software company or airline, sees profits swing sharply with sales: great when sales grow, painful when they fall.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sales up 10%", "Fixed costs unchanged", "Profit up much more than 10%"]}
### Example
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Café fixed costs", "b": "€6,000 a month"}, {"a": "Variable cost per coffee", "b": "€0.30"}, {"a": "Price per coffee", "b": "€2.30"}, {"a": "Contribution per coffee", "b": "€2.00"}]}
### Semi-variable costs
• Energy: partly fixed, partly usage
• Staff: fixed contracts plus overtime
> Every sale above break-even goes mostly to profit when fixed costs are high.

# Break-even
The break-even point is where total revenue equals total costs. Below it, you lose money; above it, you make a profit.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Break-even units = fixed costs / contribution per unit", "items": [{"s": "Fixed costs", "m": "monthly rent, salaries"}, {"s": "Contribution", "m": "price − variable cost"}]}
### The café example
ex: Fixed costs €6,000 a month | ÷ €2 contribution per coffee | = 3,000 coffees a month, about 100 a day
### Reducing break-even
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lower fixed costs", "Raise prices", "Cut variable costs", "Sell higher-margin items"]}
### Safety margin
The margin of safety is how far sales can fall before you hit break-even. A café selling 4,000 coffees has a margin of 1,000 coffees, or 25%.
> Know your break-even before you open the doors.

# Cash flow
Profit isn't cash. A business can show profits on paper but run out of money if customers pay late, stock piles up or loans fall due.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Make sale", "Invoice sent", "Wait 60 days for payment", "Meanwhile pay staff and suppliers", "Cash runs out"]}
### Working capital
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Receivables", "b": "money customers owe you"}, {"a": "Payables", "b": "money you owe suppliers"}, {"a": "Inventory", "b": "stock waiting to be sold"}, {"a": "Cash gap", "b": "time between paying and getting paid"}]}
### Late payments
Late payment is a major problem for small firms in southern Europe. The EU's Late Payment Directive sets 60-day limits for most business-to-business payments and 30 days for public authorities, but enforcement varies.
### Managing cash

• Negotiate supplier terms
• Keep a cash buffer
• Forecast cash weekly

# Business models
A business model describes how a company creates value and gets paid for it. The same product can be sold in very different ways.

# Economies of scale
Larger producers can make each unit more cheaply. Fixed costs are spread over more units, and size brings purchasing power and specialisation.

@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Spreading fixed costs", "Bulk purchasing", "Specialised machines", "Better financing terms", "Marketing reach"]}

• Coordination problems
• Loss of motivation and accountability
### Examples
Supermarket chains negotiate lower prices from suppliers; chip factories costing tens of billions of euros only make sense at enormous volumes.
> Scale lowers costs, up to the point where complexity starts raising them.

# Competitive moats
Warren Buffett popularised the idea of an economic "moat": a durable advantage that protects a company's profits from competitors, like a moat around a castle.

• New business models

# Pooling risk
Insurance works by pooling: many people pay predictable premiums so the few who suffer big losses can be compensated. Each person swaps an uncertain, possibly ruinous loss for a known cost.

== mo-scams

# The urgency trick
Almost every scam uses urgency. Pressure stops you thinking, checking or asking someone. Legitimate organisations rarely demand instant action.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Alarming message", "\"Act now\"", "Panic", "No time to check", "You click, pay or share codes"]}
### Typical lines
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["\"Your account will be blocked\"", "\"Your parcel is held\"", "\"Tax refund expires today\"", "\"Mum, I need help now\"", "\"Investment closes tonight\""]}
### Why it works
Stress narrows attention and reduces careful reasoning. Scammers exploit fear (losing money), greed (a bargain) and love (a family member in trouble).
### Your defence
• Pause: no real emergency is solved in 60 seconds
• Contact the organisation through its official number or app
• Talk to someone you trust
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Click the link in the urgent SMS", "r": "Close it, open the official app or call the number on your card"}]}
> Urgency is the scammer's best tool; slowing down is yours.

# Phishing
Phishing uses fake emails, SMS (smishing) or calls (vishing) that pretend to come from trusted organisations, to steal passwords, card details or codes.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Fake bank alerts", "Parcel delivery fees", "Tax authority refunds", "Utility refunds", "Fake job offers"]}
### Spotting it
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sender", "b": "odd email address or unknown number"}, {"a": "Link", "b": "slightly misspelled domain"}, {"a": "Tone", "b": "urgent or threatening"}, {"a": "Request", "b": "codes, passwords, card details"}]}
### Spoofing
Scammers can make calls and SMS appear to come from real numbers, even your bank's. A familiar sender name doesn't prove anything.
### Safe habits
• Never click links in unexpected messages
• Type addresses yourself or use official apps
• Enable two-factor authentication
• Report phishing to the organisation being impersonated
!? Portuguese scams often impersonate CTT (post), the Tax Authority, Segurança Social, EDP and banks.

# Never share codes
Your PIN, online banking password and one-time codes are the keys to your money. No legitimate bank or authority will ever ask for them.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"The bank's security team needs the code we just sent you\"", "r": "That's always a scam: hang up"}]}
### Why codes matter
One-time codes (OTP) authorise payments or new devices. Sharing one lets the scammer approve a transfer or link your account to their phone.
### The fake bank call
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Call from \"bank\" about suspicious activity", "They know some of your details", "\"Read me the code to cancel\"", "Code actually approves their payment"]}
### Your rights
Under EU payment rules, banks must refund unauthorised transactions unless you acted fraudulently or with gross negligence. Sharing codes may count as gross negligence, so prevention matters.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Never share PIN", "Never share passwords", "Never share codes", "Never install remote-access apps on request"]}
> If anyone asks for your code, the answer is always no.

# Trade deficits

# Tariffs

# BATNA
BATNA stands for "best alternative to a negotiated agreement", a term from Roger Fisher and William Ury's 1981 book "Getting to Yes". It's what you'll do if this deal falls through.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Weak BATNA", "items": ["no other offers", "urgent need"]}, "r": {"t": "Strong BATNA", "items": ["other offers", "happy where you are"]}}
### Why it matters
Your BATNA sets your walk-away point. The better your alternative, the more confidently you can negotiate and the less you'll accept a bad deal.
### Improving it
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Apply to several jobs", "Build savings for flexibility", "Develop in-demand skills", "Keep your network active"]}
### The other side's BATNA
Think about their alternatives too: how hard is it for them to find someone like you? A long, costly search strengthens your position.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Know your BATNA", "Estimate theirs", "Set walk-away point", "Negotiate"]}
> The best way to get a better offer is to have another one.

# Anchoring in negotiation
The first number mentioned tends to anchor the negotiation: later offers cluster around it. Research suggests first offers often predict final outcomes.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 2000.0, "hi": 3200.0, "unit": "€ a month", "items": [{"l": "Their offer", "v": 2200.0}, {"l": "Final deal", "v": 2500.0}, {"l": "Your anchor*", "v": 2800.0}]}
### When to go first
• If you have good market data, an ambitious but justified first number can pull the result up
• If you lack information, let them go first and learn their range

### Counter-anchoring
If they anchor low, don't negotiate from their number. Restate your own range with evidence.
### Ranges
Giving a range can work, but the other side will usually focus on the bottom. Make the bottom of your range a number you'd be happy with.
!? A study by Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler found that making the first offer often led to better outcomes for the party who made it.

# Direct and indirect
Taxes come in two main forms. Direct taxes are on income and wealth; indirect taxes are on spending.

### Fairness
Indirect taxes take a larger share of income from lower earners, who spend more of what they earn. Direct taxes can be made progressive.
### Balance
Portugal relies heavily on indirect taxes and social contributions compared with some EU countries.
> Everyone pays tax, even those who earn too little to pay income tax, through IVA on purchases.

# Progressive income tax
Portugal's IRS is progressive: income is divided into brackets, and each slice is taxed at its bracket's rate. Rates rise from roughly 12–13% on the first slice to 48% on the highest.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Top slice: 48%", "Middle slices: rising rates", "First slice: lowest rate", "Minimum: income below a threshold is protected"]}
### How brackets work
Only the income inside each bracket pays that bracket's rate. Someone earning just over a threshold pays the higher rate only on the few euros above it.
### Minimum existence
The "mínimo de existência" protects low incomes so that tax doesn't push take-home pay below a minimum level.
### Solidarity surcharge
Very high incomes pay an additional solidarity surcharge on top of the highest rate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gross income", "Specific deductions", "Taxable income", "Brackets applied", "Minus deductions", "Tax due"]}
> Higher rates apply only to higher slices of income, never to all of it.

# Marginal vs average
The marginal rate is the rate on your last euro of income. The average rate is total tax divided by total income. The average is always lower.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"A raise could push me into a higher bracket and I'd take home less\"", "r": "Only the extra income is taxed at the higher rate"}]}
### Example (simplified brackets)
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "First €10,000", "b": "13%: €1,300"}, {"a": "Next €10,000", "b": "25%: €2,500"}, {"a": "Total on €20,000", "b": "€3,800"}, {"a": "Average rate", "b": "19%"}, {"a": "Marginal rate", "b": "25%"}]}
### Why the myth persists
Monthly withholding tables used to cause small jumps in net pay at some thresholds, which fed the belief. Reforms smoothed the tables, and your final tax is calculated on the annual return anyway.
### Benefits that end
The real "trap" can come from benefits or allowances that stop above certain incomes, not from the tax brackets themselves.
> A raise always increases your take-home pay from income tax's point of view.
