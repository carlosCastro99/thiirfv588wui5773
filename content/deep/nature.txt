== na-evolution

# Natural selection
Natural selection needs only three ingredients. Individuals in a population differ from one another. Some of those differences are inherited. And more young are born than can possibly survive. Put those together and change is almost guaranteed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Variation in a population", "More young than can survive", "Best-suited survive and breed", "Their traits become common"]}
### What "fittest" really means
"Survival of the fittest" was coined by the philosopher Herbert Spencer, not Darwin. In biology, fitness doesn't mean strength or speed. It means **reproductive success**: how many offspring you leave who go on to breed themselves. A drab bird that raises ten chicks is fitter than a dazzling one that raises two.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Everyday 'fit'", "items": ["strong", "fast", "healthy"]}, "r": {"t": "Biological fitness", "items": ["leaves more offspring", "suits local conditions"]}}
### Small edges add up
Selection doesn't need big advantages. A trait that gives just 1% more surviving offspring can spread through a large population in a few thousand generations. For bacteria, that can take days; for elephants, it takes tens of thousands of years.
ex: Trait starts in 1% of a population | Gives a 1% breeding edge each generation | After a few thousand generations it is close to 100%
### What selection can't do
• It has no goal or foresight: it only works on variation that already exists
• It acts on individuals but changes populations; no single animal evolves
• It can only tweak what is there, so bodies carry compromises, like our awkward back
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No goal or plan", "Works only on existing variation", "Populations change, not individuals", "Leaves imperfect compromises"]}
!! Animals evolve because they need to => Variation comes first, by chance; selection then sorts it
> Natural selection is not chance: the variation is random, but which variants survive is not.

# Darwin and Wallace
Charles Darwin had worked out natural selection by about 1838, two years after returning from his five-year voyage on HMS *Beagle*. Then he sat on it for 20 years, gathering evidence on pigeons, barnacles and plants, partly because he knew how controversial it would be.
### A letter from the Malay Archipelago
In 1858 Alfred Russel Wallace, a self-taught collector working in what is now Indonesia, sent Darwin an essay describing the very same idea. Wallace had reportedly thought of it during a bout of fever. Darwin's friends, the geologist Charles Lyell and botanist Joseph Hooker, arranged for both men's work to be read together at the Linnean Society in London on 1 July 1858.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1831", "l": "Beagle sets sail"}, {"y": "1838", "l": "Darwin's key insight"}, {"y": "1858", "l": "Joint paper read"}, {"y": "1859", "l": "Origin published"}]}
Neither man was present. Darwin was grieving the death of his baby son, and Wallace was still in Asia and didn't know it was happening. The reading made little stir at first: the society's president later said the year had produced no striking discoveries.
### Different backgrounds, one idea
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Darwin", "items": ["wealthy gentleman", "Galápagos, Beagle", "slow and cautious"]}, "r": {"t": "Wallace", "items": ["self-made collector", "Amazon, Malay islands", "quick to publish"]}}
Both men were inspired by Thomas Malthus's essay on population, which argued that numbers outgrow food supply. Both had also spent years collecting animals in the tropics and noticing how species changed from island to island.
!? *On the Origin of Species* had its whole first print run of about 1,250 copies snapped up by booksellers by publication day, 24 November 1859.
> Great ideas are often "in the air": two naturalists on opposite sides of the world reached natural selection independently.
!! Darwin came up with evolution => People had proposed that species change long before; Darwin and Wallace explained how, through natural selection

# Darwin's finches
The Galápagos Islands lie about 1,000 km off Ecuador, in the Pacific. When Darwin visited in 1835 he collected finches but didn't realise how important they were. It was the ornithologist John Gould in London who told him they were all closely related species.
### One ancestor, many niches
DNA studies suggest a single flock of seed-eating birds arrived from South America about 2 million years ago. With few competitors, their descendants spread into jobs normally done by other birds. There are now about 18 recognised species.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "One ancestor finch", "items": ["Ground finch: big seeds", "Warbler finch: insects", "Cactus finch: flowers", "Tree finch: grubs"]}
### Evolution caught in the act
Peter and Rosemary Grant studied finches on the tiny island of Daphne Major from 1973 for about 40 years. During a drought in 1977, small soft seeds ran out and only large, hard ones were left. Birds with bigger beaks survived, and the next generation had beaks about 4% deeper on average.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["1977 drought", "Only hard seeds left", "Big-beaked birds survive", "Offspring have deeper beaks"]}
When wet years returned and small seeds became plentiful again, smaller beaks came back into favour. Selection swung back and forth with the weather.
!? The Grants also watched a new finch lineage form: in 1981 a large male from another island arrived on Daphne Major, and his descendants now breed mostly among themselves.
• A beak is a tool: depth for crushing, length for probing, sharpness for picking
• Some genes, such as one called ALX1, have a big effect on beak shape
> Darwin's finches show that evolution can be measured in a human lifetime, not just across millions of years.

# DNA, the instruction book
DNA is a long, twisted ladder: the famous **double helix**, worked out in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin. Each rung is a pair of letters, and the pairing rule is strict: A always joins T, and C always joins G.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "A pairs with T", "b": "adenine and thymine"}, {"a": "C pairs with G", "b": "cytosine and guanine"}, {"a": "Gene", "b": "stretch that codes a protein"}, {"a": "Genome", "b": "the full set of DNA"}]}
### From code to protein
Reading a gene happens in two steps. First the cell copies the gene into a messenger molecule, RNA. Then a ribosome reads the RNA three letters at a time; each triplet stands for one of 20 amino acids. The chain of amino acids folds into a protein: an enzyme, a muscle fibre, a hormone.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gene in DNA", "Copied to RNA", "Read 3 letters at a time", "Protein folds up"]}
### Some numbers
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "3 billion", "l": "letter pairs in our DNA"}, {"v": "~20,000", "l": "protein-coding genes"}, {"v": "~2 m", "l": "DNA per cell, unravelled"}]}
Only about 1–2% of our DNA codes for proteins. Much of the rest helps switch genes on and off, and some has no known job. That's why a human has about the same number of genes as a mouse: what differs is largely how and when genes are used.
!? The genetic code is almost universal: the same triplets mean the same amino acids in bacteria, oak trees and people, which is why a human gene can be made to work inside a bacterium.
> DNA is a four-letter code read three letters at a time, and every living thing uses almost the same dictionary.
!! More genes means a more complex animal => Rice has more genes than we do; complexity comes mostly from gene control

# Mutations
Every time a cell divides, it copies about 3 billion letter pairs of DNA. The copying machinery proofreads itself and errors are rare, roughly one per 100 million letters or better after repair. Even so, each of us is born with about 50 to 100 new mutations that neither parent carried.
### Kinds of change
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Substitution", "b": "one letter swapped"}, {"a": "Insertion / deletion", "b": "letters added or lost"}, {"a": "Duplication", "b": "whole gene copied twice"}, {"a": "Chromosome change", "b": "big pieces rearranged"}]}
Mutations also come from outside: ultraviolet light, tobacco smoke and some chemicals damage DNA. Only mutations in eggs and sperm are passed to children; those in skin or lung cells affect just that person.
### Neutral, harmful or helpful
Most mutations land in stretches that don't matter much, or swap one letter for another that codes the same amino acid, so they're **neutral**. Some break something important and are harmful. A rare few help.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of new mutations (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Neutral", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Harmful", "v": 29.0}, {"l": "Helpful", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}]}
A famous helpful one: about 7,500 years ago, a mutation that keeps the lactase enzyme working into adulthood spread among dairy farmers in Europe. Today most northern Europeans can digest milk as adults, while most adults worldwide cannot.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Copying error", "New variant appears", "Selection favours it", "Spreads over generations"]}
> Mutations supply the raw material; natural selection decides what stays.
!! Mutations are always bad, like in films => Most do nothing, and without them there would be no evolution at all

# Common ancestors
Think of a family tree. You and your cousin don't descend from each other; you share grandparents. Humans and chimpanzees are like distant cousins: both lines branched from one ancestral population about 6–7 million years ago in Africa.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Common ancestor", "items": ["Gorillas (~9 Mya split)", "Chimps and bonobos", "Humans (~6-7 Mya)"]}
### How close are we?
Our DNA differs from a chimp's by only about 1–2% in matching stretches. Counting larger insertions and deletions, the gap is closer to 4%. That small difference still adds up to tens of millions of changes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. DNA shared with humans, %", "items": [{"l": "Chimpanzee", "v": 98.8, "t": "~99", "hi": true}, {"l": "Gorilla", "v": 98.4, "t": "~98"}, {"l": "Mouse", "v": 85.0, "t": "~85 (genes)"}]}
### Going further back
Every living thing is connected. We share an ancestor with mice about 90 million years ago, with fish over 400 million years ago, and with bananas well over a billion years ago. Biologists call the ultimate ancestor of all life **LUCA**, the Last Universal Common Ancestor, living perhaps around 4 billion years ago.
• Chimps have been evolving for just as long as we have
• Neither line is "more evolved"; each adapted to its own world
• Fossils such as *Sahelanthropus* (about 7 million years old) sit near the split
!! If we came from monkeys, why are there still monkeys? => We didn't come from today's monkeys; we and they share ancestors, like cousins
> Every species alive today is a cousin of every other, linked by common ancestors further and further back in time.

# Evidence for evolution
Evolution is one of the best-tested ideas in science because many independent lines of evidence point the same way. Any one of them could have proved it wrong; none has.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Evolution", "items": ["Fossil record", "DNA comparisons", "Shared anatomy", "Change seen today"]}
### Fossils in the right order
Fossils always appear in a consistent order in the rocks: fish before amphibians, reptiles before mammals, no rabbits beside trilobites. Transitional forms fill gaps. *Tiktaalik*, found in Arctic Canada in 2004, is a 375-million-year-old fish with a neck and sturdy fins: just what scientists predicted, in rocks of just the age they predicted.
### DNA as a family record
The tree of life built from DNA matches the tree built from bones and fossils, even though the two were worked out independently. Our DNA also carries "broken" genes, such as one for making egg yolk protein, left over from distant ancestors that laid eggs.
### Evolution happening now
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Antibiotic kills most bacteria", "A few resistant ones survive", "They multiply", "Drug stops working"]}
Antibiotic resistance is evolution we pay for. The World Health Organization ranks it among the top global health threats; drug-resistant infections were linked to over 1 million deaths worldwide in 2019 alone.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Bacteria resisting antibiotics", "Insects resisting pesticides", "Lizards changing on new islands", "Fish shrinking under heavy fishing"]}
> Fossils, DNA, anatomy and living populations tell the same story independently, which is what makes evolution so secure.
!! Evolution is "just a theory" => In science, a theory is a well-tested explanation, like the theory of gravity

# Same bones, different jobs
Look inside the front limb of almost any land vertebrate and you'll find the same plan: one upper bone, two lower bones, a cluster of wrist bones and then digits. Biologists call parts like these **homologous**: same origin, different use.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Digits (fingers)", "Wrist bones", "Two lower bones", "One upper bone"]}
### One plan, many uses
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Human arm", "b": "grasping and throwing"}, {"a": "Bat wing", "b": "long fingers hold skin"}, {"a": "Whale flipper", "b": "short bones for steering"}, {"a": "Cat leg", "b": "running and pouncing"}]}
An engineer starting from scratch would never build a wing and a flipper from the same parts. Evolution can't start from scratch; it can only modify what ancestors already had. The shared plan is a fingerprint of a shared origin in early four-legged animals about 370 million years ago.
### Same job, different bones
The reverse also happens. Bird, bat and insect wings all fly, but they're built completely differently: feathers on an arm, skin on fingers, and stiff plates of cuticle. These are **analogous** structures, which evolved separately to solve the same problem.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Homologous", "items": ["same origin", "different job", "arm and flipper"]}, "r": {"t": "Analogous", "items": ["different origin", "same job", "bird and insect wings"]}}
!? Whales still carry tiny hip bones buried in their bodies, leftovers from land-walking ancestors that lived about 50 million years ago.
> Similar bones doing different jobs reveal common ancestry; different bones doing similar jobs reveal similar problems.

# Extinction
Extinction is normal. Most species last roughly 1 to 10 million years before vanishing, and new ones arise to take their place. This steady loss is called the **background rate**. A mass extinction is different: at least three-quarters of species lost in a geologically short time.
### The Big Five
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "445 Mya", "l": "Ordovician: ice age"}, {"y": "372 Mya", "l": "Devonian: ocean crisis"}, {"y": "252 Mya", "l": "Permian: the worst"}, {"y": "201 Mya", "l": "Triassic: volcanoes"}, {"y": "66 Mya", "l": "Cretaceous: asteroid"}]}
The end-Permian "Great Dying" was the worst: about 90% of marine species and 70% of land vertebrate species disappeared, probably due to huge volcanic eruptions in Siberia that heated the planet and starved the oceans of oxygen.
### The day the dinosaurs ended
Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid about 10 km wide struck what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, leaving the Chicxulub crater, roughly 180 km across. Dust and soot blocked sunlight, plants stopped growing and food chains collapsed. About three-quarters of species died, including all dinosaurs except birds.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Asteroid strikes", "Soot blocks sunlight", "Plants stop growing", "Food chains collapse"]}
### Extinction opens doors
Each mass extinction cleared space for survivors. Small mammals that had lived in the dinosaurs' shadow spread into every niche, leading eventually to whales, bats and us.
!? Many scientists argue a sixth mass extinction is under way, driven by people: today's extinction rate is estimated at tens to hundreds of times the background rate.
> Extinction is the fate of almost every species, but mass extinctions reset the whole game of life.

# Peppered moths
The peppered moth rests on tree trunks by day, relying on camouflage to avoid birds. The usual form is pale and speckled, blending with lichen-covered bark. A rare dark form, called *carbonaria*, was first recorded near Manchester in 1848.
### Soot, birds and numbers
As coal-burning factories spread, soot killed lichens and blackened bark. Pale moths stood out; dark ones vanished into the background. By 1895 about 98% of peppered moths around Manchester were dark.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Soot darkens bark", "Pale moths easy to spot", "Birds eat pale moths", "Dark moths dominate"]}
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "dark moths near Manchester, %", "items": [{"l": "1848", "v": 1.0, "t": "rare"}, {"l": "1895", "v": 98.0, "t": "~98", "hi": true}, {"l": "2000s", "v": 5.0, "t": "few"}]}
### Clean air reverses it
Britain's Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 cut smoke pollution. Lichens returned, bark lightened, and pale moths came back. By the 2000s dark moths had become rare again in most areas.
### The gene behind it
In 2016 scientists traced the dark colour to a single mutation: a chunk of "jumping" DNA inserted into a gene called *cortex*, which dates to around 1819, just as industry took off.
!! The peppered moth story was faked => Critics questioned early experiments, but Michael Majerus's large study (2001–2007) confirmed birds eat more of the moths that stand out
> The peppered moth is natural selection you can watch: the environment changed, and within decades so did the moths.

# The biggest ever
The blue whale can reach about 30 m and 150 tonnes or more: roughly the weight of 25 elephants. The biggest dinosaurs, like *Argentinosaurus*, may have reached 35 m long but probably weighed 70–100 tonnes, so the blue whale still wins on mass.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. maximum mass, tonnes", "items": [{"l": "Blue whale", "v": 150.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Argentinosaurus", "v": 80.0, "t": "70-100"}, {"l": "African elephant", "v": 6.0}]}
### Why only in the sea
Water supports a body's weight, so a whale doesn't need massive legs. On land, a body that big would struggle to hold itself up and to shed heat. Blue whales also eat at enormous scale: a single gulp can take in water and krill weighing more than the whale's own body.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Lunge into krill swarm", "Throat pleats balloon", "Push water out", "Baleen traps krill"]}
### Numbers that stun
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~180 kg", "l": "heart weight"}, {"v": "~3.5 t", "l": "krill eaten per day"}, {"v": "~2,700 kg", "l": "newborn calf"}]}
The heart is often compared to a small car; better measurements suggest about 180 kg, more like a golf cart. Its beat can slow to about 2 per minute during a deep dive.
### A near miss
Industrial whaling killed about 360,000 blue whales in the 20th century. A worldwide ban came in 1966. Numbers have been recovering slowly, but they remain a small fraction of what they once were. Blue whales are sometimes seen off the Azores.
!? Blue whale calls can reach about 188 decibels and may travel hundreds of kilometres underwater.
> The largest animal ever lives today, made possible by water's support and a diet of tiny krill eaten by the tonne.

# The fastest
Speed records depend on how you measure. The peregrine falcon is fastest in a dive, or **stoop**, using gravity. A peregrine named Frightful, trained by a falconer, was clocked at about 390 km/h in the 1990s, though wild hunting dives are usually slower.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "top speed, km/h (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Peregrine (dive)", "v": 320.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Cheetah", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Pronghorn", "v": 88.0}, {"l": "Usain Bolt", "v": 44.0}]}
### Built to dive
The falcon folds its wings into a teardrop shape. Small bony cones in its nostrils are thought to slow the rush of air so it can keep breathing. A third, see-through eyelid sweeps the eyes clean.
### The cheetah's trade-off
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Cheetah strengths", "items": ["0-100 km/h in ~3 s", "long flexible spine", "grippy claws"]}, "r": {"t": "The cost", "items": ["sprints ~20-30 s", "overheats quickly", "loses kills to lions"]}}
A cheetah takes strides of about 7 m and has its feet off the ground for over half the time. But sprinting is exhausting. After a chase it needs to rest and cool down, often while hyenas or lions steal its meal.
!? On the ground, the fastest animal relative to its size may be a tiny Californian mite that runs about 322 body lengths a second; a cheetah manages about 16.
> Speed records depend on the rules: diving, level flight, running and swimming each have a different champion.
!! Sailfish swim at 110 km/h => That figure came from old, unreliable measurements; recent studies suggest far lower burst speeds

# Octopus intelligence
An octopus has about 500 million neurons, roughly as many as a dog. What's strange is where they are: around two-thirds sit in the eight arms, not the central brain. Each arm can taste with its suckers and handle routine tasks partly on its own while the brain sets the overall goal.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "neurons, millions (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "In the arms", "v": 330.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Central brain", "v": 170.0}]}
### Clever tricks
• Unscrewing jars, even from the inside
• Carrying coconut shells to use as portable shelters, seen in Indonesia
• Recognising individual human keepers, and squirting water at ones they dislike
• Escaping tanks through any gap larger than their beak, their only hard part
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Opens screw-top jars", "Uses coconut shells as homes", "Recognises individual people", "Squeezes through tiny gaps"]}
### A separate path to a big brain
Our last common ancestor with octopuses lived over 500 million years ago and was probably a simple worm-like creature. Octopus intelligence evolved entirely separately from ours, an example of **convergent evolution** of minds.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Octopus", "items": ["neurons in arms", "lives 1-2 years", "no parents to learn from"]}, "r": {"t": "Dog", "items": ["neurons in brain", "lives 10-15 years", "learns from others"]}}
!? Octopuses are colour-blind by usual tests, yet they match colours brilliantly; skin cells containing light-sensitive proteins may help.
Common octopus is a staple of Portuguese cooking, and Portugal is one of Europe's biggest octopus consumers. Their intelligence is one reason why proposed octopus farms, such as one planned in Spain's Canary Islands, have stirred debate.
> The octopus shows that a sharp mind can evolve along a completely different route from ours.

# Camouflage
Camouflage is a constant arms race. Predators evolve sharper eyes; prey evolves better disguises. And it works both ways: a lurking predator also needs to stay hidden from its victims.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ways to hide", "items": ["Match the background", "Break up the outline", "Look like an object", "Copy a dangerous one"]}
### Blending in and breaking up
Countershading is the most common trick: a darker back and paler belly cancel out the shadow light casts, making an animal look flat. Disruptive patterns, like a leopard's spots, break a body's outline into unrelated patches.
### Zebra stripes, solved?
Zebra stripes probably don't hide zebras from lions. Experiments with striped coats on horses found that biting horseflies struggle to land on stripes, so pest protection is now the leading explanation.
### Mimicry
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Harmless mimic", "items": ["hoverfly", "milk snake"]}, "r": {"t": "Dangerous model", "items": ["wasp", "coral snake"]}}
This is **Batesian mimicry**: harmless species copy the warning colours of dangerous ones. Predators learn to avoid the pattern, and the mimic gets a free ride.
### Chameleons and cuttlefish
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Chameleon", "b": "shifts nanocrystals in skin"}, {"a": "Cuttlefish", "b": "muscles open pigment sacs"}, {"a": "Stick insect", "b": "shaped like a twig"}]}
!! Chameleons change colour to match their background => They change mainly to signal mood, warn rivals and control temperature
!? Cuttlefish can change their pattern in under a second, faster than any chameleon.
> The best disguise isn't always invisibility: looking flat, broken up, or dangerous can work just as well.

# Migration
The Arctic tern breeds in the far north in summer, then flies south to Antarctica for the southern summer. Tracking tags fitted in 2010 showed the route isn't direct: birds zigzag with the winds across the Atlantic, covering about 70,000 km a year.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~70,000 km", "l": "flown each year"}, {"v": "30+ years", "l": "possible lifespan"}, {"v": "~2 million km", "l": "lifetime, roughly"}]}
ex: Moon round trip ≈ 770,000 km | Lifetime ≈ 30 years × 70,000 km ≈ 2.1 million km | ≈ 2.7 trips to the Moon and back
### Why bother?
Migration follows food and daylight. Terns get two summers a year and more daylight than any other animal. Many migrations trade a dangerous journey for rich feeding grounds or safe breeding sites.
### How do they find the way?
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Sun position and time of day", "Star patterns at night", "Earth's magnetic field", "Landmarks and smells"]}
### Other epic journeys
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bar-tailed godwit", "b": "13,000+ km nonstop"}, {"a": "Monarch butterfly", "b": "up to 4,000 km"}, {"a": "Humpback whale", "b": "~8,000 km each way"}, {"a": "European eel", "b": "to the Sargasso Sea"}]}
Portugal sits on a major flyway. Every autumn, huge numbers of birds pass through Sagres and the Tagus Estuary on their way to Africa, including storks, eagles and waders. The European eel spawns in the Sargasso Sea; young eels drift across the Atlantic to rivers like the Minho.
!? A bar-tailed godwit tagged in 2022 flew about 13,500 km from Alaska to Tasmania in 11 days without landing.
> Migrating animals trade the risk of a long journey for the reward of food, light and safe places to breed.

# Echolocation
Echolocation works like sonar. An animal makes a sound, the sound bounces off objects, and the echo comes back. The delay reveals distance; changes in pitch and loudness reveal size, texture and movement.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Send out a call", "Sound hits an insect", "Echo returns", "Brain builds a picture"]}
ex: Sound in air ≈ 340 m/s | Echo returns after 10 ms (there and back) | Insect is about 1.7 m away
### Bats
Most bats call at ultrasonic pitches, above the roughly 20 kHz limit of human hearing. Higher pitches give sharper detail. When closing in on prey, a bat speeds up from about 10 to as many as 200 calls a second, a burst known as the "feeding buzz".
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 200.0, "unit": "frequency, kHz", "items": [{"l": "Human limit", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Pipistrelle bat", "v": 45.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Dolphin clicks", "v": 120.0}]}
### Dolphins
Dolphins make clicks in their nasal passages and focus them through the **melon**, a fatty lump in the forehead. Echoes are picked up through the lower jaw. They can tell objects apart by shape and material, even when buried in sand.
### Arms race in the dark
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Moths hear bat calls and dive", "Tiger moths click back to jam", "Some moths have sound-soaking fur"]}
!? Some blind people echolocate by clicking their tongue, and brain scans show the echoes are processed in the visual part of the brain.
Portugal has around 27 bat species, and every one eats insects. A single pipistrelle can eat thousands of tiny insects in a night.
> Echolocation turns sound into sight, letting bats and dolphins hunt where eyes are almost useless.

# Tardigrades
Tardigrades, or water bears, are eight-legged animals about 0.5 mm long. They live almost everywhere: in moss, lichen, sea mud, hot springs and Antarctic ice. There are over 1,300 known species, and you can often find them in damp moss from a garden wall.
### The tun state
When their home dries out, a tardigrade pulls in its legs, loses almost all its water and curls into a dry ball called a **tun**. Its metabolism drops to about 0.01% of normal. In this state it can wait for years until water returns.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Habitat dries out", "Curls into a 'tun'", "Metabolism almost stops", "Revives when wet"]}
### What they've survived
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Cold", "b": "close to -272 °C, briefly"}, {"a": "Heat", "b": "about 150 °C, briefly"}, {"a": "Space", "b": "10 days in orbit, 2007"}, {"a": "Radiation", "b": "hundreds of times our limit"}]}
These records come from tuns in the lab and on a European Space Agency mission. Active tardigrades are far more fragile: about half die after a day at around 37 °C.
### Molecular armour
Tardigrades make special proteins that turn into a glass-like gel as they dry, holding cell parts in place. One protein, called Dsup, seems to shield DNA from radiation damage; human cells given it in the lab tolerated X-rays better.
!! Tardigrades are indestructible => They endure extremes only while dormant, and only for limited times; awake, they are delicate
> Tardigrades don't fight extreme conditions; they switch life off and wait them out.

# Social insects
A honeybee colony of up to about 50,000 bees behaves almost like one animal. Biologists call it a **super-organism**: individual bees are like cells, each with a job, and the colony as a whole eats, breathes, defends itself and reproduces.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Queen", "b": "lays up to ~1,500 eggs a day"}, {"a": "Workers (female)", "b": "clean, nurse, build, forage"}, {"a": "Drones (male)", "b": "mate with new queens"}]}
### The waggle dance
A forager back from good flowers dances on the comb. The angle of her straight "waggle run" relative to vertical shows the direction to fly relative to the Sun. The length of the waggle shows distance. Karl von Frisch decoded this and shared the 1973 Nobel Prize.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Forager finds flowers", "Dances on the comb", "Angle = direction", "Waggle length = distance"]}
### Jobs change with age
Young workers clean cells and feed larvae, then build comb and guard the entrance, and only in their last weeks do they forage outside. A summer worker lives about 5 to 6 weeks.
### Why help instead of breed?
In bees, ants and wasps, sisters share an unusually high share of genes, so helping raise more sisters can pass on more of a worker's genes than having her own young. This idea, **kin selection**, was developed by W. D. Hamilton in 1964.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~50,000", "l": "bees in a summer hive"}, {"v": "millions", "l": "termites in a big mound"}, {"v": "~20 quadrillion", "l": "ants on Earth (estimate)"}]}
!? Termite mounds in Africa can stand over 5 m tall, with tunnels that help air move and keep the inside at a steady temperature.
> In a colony, the individual matters less than the team: success is measured by the colony, not the insect.

# Animal communication
In the 1980s, Robert Seyfarth and Dorothy Cheney played recordings of vervet monkey alarm calls in Kenya. With no predator in sight, the monkeys still reacted correctly to each call. That showed the calls carry specific meaning, not just fear.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Leopard call", "b": "run up into a tree"}, {"a": "Eagle call", "b": "look up, dive into bushes"}, {"a": "Snake call", "b": "stand up, scan the ground"}]}
### Too low for us
Elephants communicate with rumbles as low as about 14 Hz, below the roughly 20 Hz floor of human hearing. These **infrasound** calls can travel several kilometres, and elephants may pick up vibrations through their feet. In 2024 a study suggested African elephants even use name-like calls for individuals.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.0, "hi": 100000.0, "log": true, "unit": "sound frequency, Hz", "items": [{"l": "Elephant rumble", "v": 14.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Human voice", "v": 200.0}, {"l": "Bat call", "v": 45000.0}]}
### Many channels
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Sound: songs, calls, rumbles", "Chemicals: scent and pheromones", "Sight: colours, postures, dances", "Touch and vibration"]}
Signals evolve to suit the habitat. In dense forest, sound carries better than sight; at night, smell and sound win; in open water, low whale song can travel very far.
!? Prairie dog alarm calls seem to encode a predator's type, size and even colour, according to research by Con Slobodchikoff.
> Animal signals can be surprisingly specific, carrying information about who, what and where.
!! Animals have language just like ours => Many signals carry meaning, but no animal has shown the open-ended grammar of human language

# Longest lives
In 2016 scientists used radiocarbon dating on the eye lenses of Greenland sharks. The lens core forms before birth and never changes. The largest shark tested was estimated at about 390 years old, with a wide margin of error from about 270 to 510.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "maximum known age, years (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Ocean quahog clam", "v": 507.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Greenland shark", "v": 390.0, "t": "~270-510"}, {"l": "Bowhead whale", "v": 211.0}, {"l": "Human", "v": 122.0}]}
### Why slow means old
Greenland sharks live in near-freezing water around 0–4 °C, grow about 1 cm a year, and may only reach breeding age at around 150. Cold, slow metabolism and few predators often go with long life.
### The "immortal" jellyfish
*Turritopsis dohrnii*, only about 4.5 mm across, can turn back from an adult into a polyp, its young stage, when stressed or injured. It then grows into adults again.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Polyp", "Adult medusa", "Stress or injury", "Reverts to polyp"]}
It isn't truly immortal: most are eaten or killed by disease. But in principle the cycle has no fixed end. It lives in the Mediterranean, including off southern Europe.
### Other long-lived record holders
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ming the clam: 507 years", "Glass sponges: maybe thousands", "Naked mole-rat: ~40 years", "Tortoise Jonathan: 190+ years"]}

# Photosynthesis
Photosynthesis happens inside **chloroplasts**, tiny green structures packed into leaf cells; a single leaf cell can hold dozens of them. They work in two linked stages.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "CO2 + water → sugar + O2", "items": [{"s": "CO2", "m": "carbon dioxide from air"}, {"s": "water", "m": "drawn up by roots"}, {"s": "sugar", "m": "glucose, the plant's fuel"}, {"s": "O2", "m": "oxygen, released"}]}
### Two stages
First, light energy splits water molecules. This is where the oxygen we breathe comes from: it's a by-product of breaking water, not of carbon dioxide. The energy is stored in short-lived carrier molecules. Second, in the **Calvin cycle**, that energy is used to fix carbon dioxide from the air into sugar.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Light hits chlorophyll", "Water split, O2 released", "Energy stored", "CO2 built into sugar"]}
The enzyme that grabs carbon dioxide, RuBisCO, is probably the most abundant protein on Earth. It's also slow and often grabs oxygen by mistake, which is why some plants such as maize evolved extra tricks to concentrate CO2.
### What the sugar becomes
• Fuel for the plant's own cells, burned in respiration day and night
• Cellulose, the stuff of wood, cotton and paper
• Starch stored in potatoes, rice and wheat grains
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~1-2%", "l": "of sunlight a crop stores"}, {"v": "~50%", "l": "of oxygen from ocean algae"}, {"v": "2.4 bn yrs", "l": "since oxygen built up"}]}
!! Plants get their mass from the soil => Most of a tree's dry mass is carbon taken from the air
!? Jan van Helmont grew a willow in a pot for five years in the 1600s: it gained about 74 kg while the soil lost only about 60 g.
> A tree is built mostly out of thin air, assembled with sunlight.

# Why leaves are green
Sunlight contains all the colours of the rainbow. **Chlorophyll** soaks up blue and red light strongly but absorbs less green, so more green is reflected back to our eyes. Plants actually have two main types, chlorophyll *a* and *b*, which absorb slightly different shades.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 400.0, "hi": 700.0, "unit": "wavelength of light, nm", "items": [{"l": "Blue: absorbed", "v": 440.0}, {"l": "Green: reflected", "v": 550.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Red: absorbed", "v": 670.0}]}
### Green isn't wasted
Plants do use some green light. Because it isn't absorbed straight away at the surface, it penetrates deeper into the leaf and powers chloroplasts in lower layers. One idea is that absorbing only the edges of the spectrum keeps the energy flow steadier and safer.
### The autumn show
Leaves also hold yellow and orange pigments called **carotenoids**, the same family that colours carrots. In autumn, deciduous trees break down chlorophyll to recover its valuable nitrogen before dropping their leaves. The green fades and carotenoids are unmasked.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Days shorten, nights cool", "Chlorophyll broken down", "Yellows and oranges show", "Leaf drops"]}
Reds and purples are different: they come from **anthocyanins**, which many trees make fresh in autumn, possibly as a sunscreen while nutrients are pulled back.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Chlorophyll", "b": "green; fades in autumn"}, {"a": "Carotenoids", "b": "yellow, orange; always there"}, {"a": "Anthocyanins", "b": "red, purple; made in autumn"}]}
!? Sunny days and cool (not freezing) nights give the brightest reds, which is why some autumns look more spectacular than others.
> Autumn colours aren't added; they were hiding under the green all summer.
!! Leaves change colour because they die from cold => The tree actively dismantles chlorophyll to save nutrients before shedding the leaf

# Roots
Roots do more than drink. They anchor a plant, store food (think carrots and sweet potatoes) and sense their surroundings, growing towards water and away from obstacles. Most absorption happens through **root hairs**: microscopic outgrowths that hugely increase surface area.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~600 km", "l": "roots of one rye plant"}, {"v": "~10,000 km", "l": "including root hairs"}, {"v": "~120 m", "l": "deepest known roots"}]}
!? Those rye figures come from a classic 1937 study by Howard Dittmer, who measured a single plant grown for four months in a box of soil.
### Fungal partners
About 80% of land plant species team up with **mycorrhizal** fungi. Fungal threads are much thinner than roots and reach into tiny pores, gathering phosphorus, nitrogen and water. In return the plant pays in sugar, often 10–20% of what it makes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plant gives", "items": ["sugars", "fats (some fungi)"]}, "r": {"t": "Fungus gives", "items": ["phosphorus, nitrogen", "water", "some protection"]}}
### Other partners
Peas, beans and clover host bacteria in root **nodules** that turn nitrogen gas from the air into fertiliser. That's why farmers have long rotated legumes with cereals.
### How water rises
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Root hairs absorb water", "Water enters wood vessels", "Leaves lose water vapour", "Pull draws water upwards"]}
Water evaporating from leaves pulls a continuous column of water up through the plant, like sucking on a straw. A large tree can move hundreds of litres a day this way.
> A plant is as much underground as above it, and most roots work in partnership with fungi and bacteria.

# Pollination
Pollen carries a plant's male cells. To make seeds, it has to reach the female part of a flower of the same species. Plants can't move, so they use couriers: wind, water or animals.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bee visits for nectar", "Pollen sticks to her hairs", "She visits next flower", "Pollen fertilises seeds"]}
### Adverts and rewards
Flowers advertise with colour, shape and scent, and pay with nectar or spare pollen. Bees see ultraviolet light, and many flowers have UV "landing strips" invisible to us. Red tubular flowers tend to attract birds; pale, night-scented flowers attract moths and bats.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bees", "b": "blue, yellow, UV patterns"}, {"a": "Hummingbirds", "b": "red tubes, lots of nectar"}, {"a": "Moths, bats", "b": "pale, scented at night"}, {"a": "Wind", "b": "no petals, huge pollen counts"}]}
### Some flowers cheat
Mediterranean bee orchids (*Ophrys*), common in Portugal's limestone hills, offer no nectar. Their flowers look and smell like female insects, and males try to mate with them, carrying pollen away.
### Why it matters to us
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~75%", "l": "crop types helped by animals"}, {"v": "~90%", "l": "wild flowering plants"}, {"v": "~35%", "l": "of crop volume"}]}
Almonds, apples, cherries, coffee and cocoa all depend heavily on animals; wheat, rice and maize are wind-pollinated. Wild bees, hoverflies and moths matter as much as honeybees, and many are declining because of habitat loss and pesticides.
!! Honeybees do most of the pollinating => Wild pollinators often do as much or more; there are about 2,000 bee species in Europe
> A flower is a deal: food for the animal, delivery service for the plant.

# Seed dispersal
A seed that lands under its parent faces shade and competition from a giant. Spreading seeds lets plants colonise gaps, escape seed-eating pests that gather near parents and reach new habitats.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "How seeds travel", "items": ["Wind: dandelion, maple", "Animal gut: berries", "Fur hitchhikers: burrs", "Water: coconut"]}
### Clever designs
A dandelion seed's fluffy parachute creates a stable ring of swirling air above it, a mechanism only described in 2018, which lets it float for kilometres. Maple "helicopters" spin to slow their fall so wind carries them sideways.
### Animal couriers
Fruits are bribes: sugary flesh wrapped around seeds that survive digestion. Some seeds even germinate better after passing through a gut. Jays and squirrels bury acorns to eat later; the ones they forget become oak trees. A single jay can hide thousands of acorns in one autumn.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bird eats a berry", "Seed survives the gut", "Dropped far away", "Grows with fertiliser"]}
### Exploding pods
Some plants fire their own seeds. The squirting cucumber, a common weed on Portuguese roadsides, builds up pressure inside its fruit and blasts seeds several metres when it breaks off.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Burdock burrs", "b": "inspired Velcro in the 1940s"}, {"a": "Coconut", "b": "floats for months at sea"}, {"a": "Squirting cucumber", "b": "shoots seeds metres away"}]}
!? A date palm seed about 2,000 years old, found at Masada in Israel, germinated in 2005; the tree, nicknamed Methuselah, later produced pollen.
> Plants can't move as adults, so they put all their travel into their seeds.

# Fungi are not plants
For centuries fungi were lumped in with plants because they don't move and grow from the ground. But they can't photosynthesise. Like animals, they feed on other living or dead material, digesting it outside their bodies and absorbing the results.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fungi share with animals", "items": ["eat other organisms", "chitin (as in insects)", "store glycogen"]}, "r": {"t": "Plants", "items": ["make own food", "cellulose walls", "store starch"]}}
### The hidden body
The real fungus is a **mycelium**: a network of threads called hyphae, each finer than a hair, spreading through soil or wood. A mushroom is just the fruiting body, popping up to release spores, like an apple on a hidden tree.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Mushroom: releases spores", "Stalk pushes it up", "Mycelium: threads in soil"]}
### Huge and old
A honey fungus (*Armillaria*) in Oregon's Blue Mountains covers about 9 km² and may be thousands of years old, making it one of the largest living things known.
### Everyday fungi
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Yeast: bread, beer, wine", "Penicillium: first antibiotic", "Moulds: blue cheese", "Rusts and blights on crops"]}
Only about 150,000 fungus species have been described, but estimates suggest there are 2 to 4 million.
!? Fungi and animals shared a common ancestor about a billion years ago, after the plant line had already split off.
> Fungi are a kingdom of their own, closer to us than to the plants they grow among.
!! Mushrooms are vegetables => Cooks treat them as vegetables, but biologically they're fungi, not plants

# The wood wide web
Fungal threads in soil can link the roots of several trees, even of different species, into **common mycorrhizal networks**. The nickname "wood wide web" appeared in 1997, when the journal *Nature* published a study by Suzanne Simard in Canada.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fungus links tree roots", "Network spans many trees", "Carbon and water may move", "How much? Still debated"]}
### What experiments showed
Simard's team labelled carbon in birch and Douglas fir seedlings and found some had moved from one species to the other. Later work suggested shaded seedlings might receive extra carbon and that large "mother trees" sit at the centre of networks.
### The pushback
In 2023 a review led by Justine Karst examined the field and concluded that the popular story had run ahead of the evidence. Problems include:
• Few experiments are done in mature forests rather than pots
• Carbon may move through soil, not the fungus itself
• The fungi may be serving their own interests, not the trees'
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Well supported", "items": ["fungi link many roots", "fungi trade with trees"]}, "r": {"t": "Still debated", "items": ["trees feed their young", "forest-wide sharing", "warning signals"]}}
### Two kinds of partners
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ectomycorrhizal", "b": "wrap roots: oak, pine, birch"}, {"a": "Arbuscular", "b": "enter cells: most plants"}]}
!! Trees deliberately nurture each other through fungi => Fungal networks are real, but evidence that trees "share" in this way is limited and contested
> The fungal network is real; the idea of trees caring for each other through it is an exciting hypothesis, not settled fact.

# Record trees
Hyperion was discovered in 2006 by naturalists Chris Atkins and Michael Taylor in Redwood National Park, California. It's about 116 m tall, roughly the height of a 35-storey building. Since 2022, visitors have been banned from its site because trampling damaged the ground.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "height, m (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Hyperion redwood", "v": 116.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Tallest eucalyptus", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Tallest in Europe", "v": 72.0}, {"l": "Typical oak", "v": 25.0}]}
### How tall can trees go?
A tree must lift water from its roots to its top leaves. The higher it goes, the harder this gets, and top leaves become small and water-stressed. Calculations suggest a limit of about 120–130 m for redwoods.
### The oldest
A Great Basin bristlecone pine called Methuselah, in California's White Mountains, is about 4,850 years old: it was already a young tree when the Great Pyramid of Giza was built. Bristlecones survive in cold, dry, windy conditions where slow growth makes dense, rot-resistant wood.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~2830 BC", "l": "Methuselah sprouts"}, {"y": "~2560 BC", "l": "Great Pyramid built"}, {"y": "1957", "l": "Age found by core"}, {"y": "2026", "l": "Still alive"}]}
### Records closer to home
Europe's tallest tree is thought to be the "Karri Knight", a eucalyptus about 72 m tall at Vale de Canas near Coimbra. Portugal's cork oak known as the Whistler Tree, planted in 1783, was European Tree of the Year in 2018.
!? The largest tree by volume is General Sherman, a giant sequoia of about 1,500 m³, enough wood for roughly 5 billion matches.
> The tallest trees are limited by plumbing, and the oldest by luck and harsh, slow-growing conditions.

# Decomposers
Every year, a hectare of European forest can drop several tonnes of leaves, twigs and wood. That it doesn't pile up is thanks to an army of recyclers.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Plants grow", "Leaves and bodies die", "Decomposers break down", "Nutrients back in soil"]}
### Who does the work
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Shredders", "b": "worms, woodlice, millipedes"}, {"a": "Fungi", "b": "break down tough wood"}, {"a": "Bacteria", "b": "finish the job, release nutrients"}]}
Shredders break material into small pieces, increasing surface area. Fungi are especially important for wood: white-rot fungi are among the few organisms that can break down **lignin**, the tough polymer that stiffens wood. Bacteria then process the remains into simple nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates.
### Speed depends on conditions
Warm and wet speeds things up; cold, dry or waterlogged slows it down. A leaf may vanish in months in a rainforest but last years in a cold northern forest. In waterlogged bogs, decay almost stops, building up peat.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "years (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Rainforest", "v": 0.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Temperate forest", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Boreal forest", "v": 7.0}]}
### Worms
Charles Darwin's last book, published in 1881, was about earthworms. He calculated they move tonnes of soil per hectare per year, slowly burying stones and old ruins.
!? A teaspoon of healthy soil can contain more bacterial cells than there are people on Earth.
> Death feeds life: decomposers turn yesterday's leaves into tomorrow's growth.

# Carnivorous plants
Carnivorous plants live in bogs, sands and rock where nitrogen and phosphorus are scarce. They still photosynthesise for energy; insects are their fertiliser, not their main food. About 800 species are known.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Trap types", "items": ["Snap traps: flytrap", "Pitfalls: pitchers", "Flypaper: sundews", "Suction: bladderworts"]}
### How the flytrap counts
Each lobe of a Venus flytrap has three or so trigger hairs. One touch sends an electrical signal but doesn't close the trap, avoiding false alarms from raindrops. A second touch within about 20–30 seconds snaps it shut in around a tenth of a second.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["First touch: counted", "Second touch: snap shut", "More touches: seal tight", "~5 touches: digestion"]}
The trap then seals and floods with digestive juices. Digesting an insect takes about a week to 10 days. Venus flytraps grow wild only in a small area of North and South Carolina in the USA.
### Even faster traps
Bladderworts, underwater plants, have tiny bladders under low pressure. When prey brushes a trigger, the door flips open and the prey is sucked in within about a millisecond, one of the fastest movements in the plant world.
### A Portuguese native
The dewy pine (*Drosophyllum lusitanicum*) grows on dry, sandy and rocky ground in Portugal, Spain and Morocco. Its sticky leaves catch small insects and give off a honey-like smell.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dewy pine", "b": "Portugal, Spain, Morocco"}, {"a": "Sundews", "b": "wet bogs, incl. Portugal"}, {"a": "Butterworts", "b": "greasy leaves trap gnats"}]}
!! Carnivorous plants get their energy from insects => They make energy by photosynthesis; insects supply missing nutrients
> Carnivorous plants turned the tables on insects to survive in soils too poor for other plants.

# Food chains
A food chain follows who eats whom. Each step is a **trophic level**. Plants and algae are producers; animals that eat them are primary consumers; meat-eaters that eat those are secondary consumers, and so on.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sunlight", "Grass (producer)", "Rabbit (herbivore)", "Fox (predator)"]}
### Webs, not chains
Real ecosystems are tangled **food webs**. A fox eats rabbits, mice, beetles and blackberries. Many animals switch levels: a bear eating berries is a primary consumer, but when eating salmon it's a third- or fourth-level consumer.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Phytoplankton", "Zooplankton", "Sardine", "Dolphin or tuna"]}
That ocean chain matters for Portugal: sardines eat plankton off the coast, and in turn feed dolphins, seabirds, tuna and, of course, people at the festas de Santo António.
### The forgotten level
Decomposers feed on every level, recycling the energy-poor leftovers into nutrients. Without them, nutrients would stay locked in dead bodies and producers would starve.
### Why chains are short
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy left, % of plant energy (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Plants", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Herbivores", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Predators", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Top predators", "v": 0.1, "hi": true}]}
Because so much energy is lost at each step, most chains have only 3–5 links. There simply isn't enough energy left to support another level of hunters.
!? Deep-sea hydrothermal vents host food webs that start with bacteria using chemical energy from hydrogen sulfide, not sunlight.
> Every food web is an energy budget, and each step up costs about nine-tenths of what's available.

# The 10% rule
The **10% rule** was drawn from work by the American ecologist Raymond Lindeman in 1942, who studied energy flowing through a lake in Minnesota. Real values vary from about 5% to 20%, but 10% is a good rule of thumb.
ex: Grass stores 10,000 kJ | Rabbits get about 1,000 kJ | Foxes get about 100 kJ | An eagle eating foxes would get about 10 kJ
### Where the 90% goes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Burned for moving and keeping warm", "Parts not eaten: roots, bones", "Parts not digested: droppings", "Lost as heat"]}
Warm-blooded animals waste more because they burn energy to keep their bodies warm. Cold-blooded animals like snakes and fish pass on a larger share, sometimes 10–15% or more.
### The pyramid
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Top predators: few", "Predators", "Herbivores: many", "Producers: most energy"]}
Drawn as a pyramid, each level is roughly a tenth the size of the one below. That's why big predators need huge territories: an Iberian lynx needs many rabbits, and a wolf pack can range over hundreds of km².
### What it means for our food
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Eating plants", "items": ["one step from Sun", "less land per calorie"]}, "r": {"t": "Eating meat", "items": ["extra step lost", "more land per calorie"]}}
This is why producing a calorie of beef needs far more land and crops than producing a calorie of wheat or beans.
!! Pyramids always have the most biomass at the bottom => In oceans, fast-growing plankton can weigh less at any moment than the fish feeding on them
> Energy leaks away at every step up a food chain, so the higher the level, the fewer animals it can support.

# Keystone species
A keystone is the wedge-shaped stone at the top of an arch; pull it out and the arch collapses. The ecologist Robert Paine coined the term in 1969 after removing ochre sea stars from rocky shores in Washington state. Mussels took over, crowding out dozens of other species.
### Yellowstone's wolves
Wolves were wiped out in Yellowstone National Park by the 1920s. In 1995 and 1996, 31 wolves from Canada were released. Elk numbers fell and they grazed less freely in river valleys.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Wolves return (1995)", "Elk numbers fall, wary", "Willows and aspens regrow", "Beavers and birds return"]}
This chain of effects from a top predator down is called a **trophic cascade**. Scientists still debate how strong it was: bears, human hunting, drought and beavers all played parts, and recovery has been patchy.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1926", "l": "Last wolves killed"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "First wolves released"}, {"y": "2000s", "l": "Willows recover in places"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "About 100 wolves"}]}
### Other keystones
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sea otters", "b": "eat urchins, save kelp"}, {"a": "Beavers", "b": "dams create wetlands"}, {"a": "Fig trees", "b": "fruit all year in tropics"}, {"a": "Rabbits (Iberia)", "b": "food for lynx and eagles"}]}
In Portugal, the European rabbit is a keystone: it feeds over 30 predator species, digs burrows used by others and shapes plants by grazing. Disease outbreaks among rabbits have hurt the Iberian lynx and imperial eagle.
!? Portugal still has wild wolves: about 300 Iberian wolves live mainly north of the Douro.
> Remove a keystone species and the effects ripple through the entire ecosystem.

# Biomes
A **biome** is a large region with a similar climate and similar kinds of life, wherever it is in the world. A Mediterranean hillside in Portugal looks surprisingly like one in California, Chile or South Africa, because the climate shapes plants the same way.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hot + very wet", "b": "tropical rainforest"}, {"a": "Hot + dry", "b": "desert or savanna"}, {"a": "Cold + moderate rain", "b": "taiga (boreal forest)"}, {"a": "Very cold + dry", "b": "tundra"}]}
### The big two: heat and water
Temperature sets how long the growing season lasts. Rainfall sets how much plant life the land can support. The ecologist Robert Whittaker mapped biomes on a simple chart of these two in the 1970s.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of ice-free land, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Desert & dry", "v": 33.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Grassland/savanna", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Taiga", "v": 11.0}, {"l": "Rainforest", "v": 6.0}]}
### Portugal's biome
Most of Portugal belongs to the **Mediterranean** biome: hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. Plants cope with summer drought using small, tough, often aromatic leaves: think of rosemary, rockrose and cork oak. Mediterranean-type regions cover only about 2% of Earth's land but hold roughly a sixth of all plant species.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Hot, dry summers", "Mild, wet winters", "Tough, scented leaves", "Plants adapted to fire"]}
### Biomes on mountains
Climb a mountain and you pass through biomes in miniature. In Serra da Estrela, oak woods give way to pine, then scrub, then bare, tundra-like ground near the 1,993 m summit.
!? Aquatic biomes, marine and freshwater, cover about 71% of Earth's surface, far more than all land biomes combined.
> Climate draws the map of life: similar heat and rain produce similar-looking living worlds.

# Rainforests
Tropical rainforests grow near the equator, where it's hot all year and rain falls heavily, often 2,000 mm or more per year. Constant warmth and water allow year-round growth and fierce competition for light.
### Layers of life
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Emergents: tallest giants", "Canopy: dense leafy roof", "Understorey: dim, humid", "Forest floor: under 2% light"]}
Most species live in the canopy, 30–45 m up. Some trees support hundreds of insect species, and a single tree in Peru has yielded over 40 species of ant.
### The Amazon
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~5.5 m km²", "l": "of rainforest"}, {"v": "~390 billion", "l": "trees"}, {"v": "~16,000", "l": "tree species"}]}
The Amazon spreads across nine countries, with about 60% in Brazil, the largest Portuguese-speaking nation. It recycles much of its own rain: trees release water vapour that forms "flying rivers" of moisture carrying rain to farms in southern Brazil.
### Poor soils, rich life
Rainforest soils are surprisingly poor. Nutrients are locked up in living plants and recycled quickly. Cleared land often loses fertility within a few years.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Forest cleared", "Fewer trees make rain", "Drier, more fires", "Risk of turning to savanna"]}
Roughly 17–20% of the Amazon has been cleared. Some scientists warn that beyond about 20–25% parts could flip to savanna, though the exact threshold is uncertain.
!! The Amazon is the lungs of the planet => It produces lots of oxygen but uses almost as much; its real value is storing carbon and making rain
> Rainforests pack over half of land species into a twentieth of the land, held together by heat, rain and fast recycling.

# Coral reefs
A coral is a colony of tiny animals called **polyps**, relatives of jellyfish and sea anemones. Each polyp builds a cup of limestone beneath it. Over thousands of years, these skeletons build reefs.
### A partnership
Inside the polyps' tissue live single-celled algae called zooxanthellae. They photosynthesise and pass up to 90% of their sugars to the coral. In return they get shelter, carbon dioxide and nutrients.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Algae give coral", "items": ["most of its food", "bright colours"]}, "r": {"t": "Coral gives algae", "items": ["shelter in sunlight", "CO2 and nutrients"]}}
### Bleaching
When water stays about 1 °C above the normal summer maximum for weeks, the partnership breaks down. Corals expel the algae and turn white. If heat persists, they starve.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sea warms ~1 °C", "Algae expelled", "Coral turns white", "Recovers or dies"]}
The fourth global bleaching event, starting in 2023, has affected around four-fifths of the world's reef area, the most extensive on record.
### Why so rich
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "<1%", "l": "of sea floor"}, {"v": "~25%", "l": "of marine species"}, {"v": "~2,300 km", "l": "Great Barrier Reef length"}]}
Reefs offer endless nooks for hiding, and the partnership makes them productive even in clear, nutrient-poor tropical water. They also protect coasts from waves and support fishing for hundreds of millions of people.
!? Not all corals are tropical: cold-water coral reefs grow in deep, dark Atlantic waters, including around the Azores, without any algae.
> Coral reefs are cities built by tiny animals with solar-powered tenants, and heat can evict those tenants.

# The water cycle
Earth has about 1.4 billion km³ of water, and almost all of it has been here for billions of years. The same molecules keep circulating: the water in your glass may once have passed through a dinosaur.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Sun evaporates water", "Vapour cools into clouds", "Rain or snow falls", "Rivers return it to sea"]}
### Where the water is
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of Earth's water, %", "items": [{"l": "Oceans", "v": 97.0}, {"l": "Ice and snow", "v": 1.7, "t": "~2"}, {"l": "Groundwater", "v": 1.7, "t": "~2"}, {"l": "Lakes, rivers, air", "v": 0.1, "t": "tiny", "hi": true}]}
### How long water stays
A water molecule spends on average about 9 days in the atmosphere, but around 3,000 years in the ocean, and can be locked in Antarctic ice for hundreds of thousands of years. Plants are big players too: **transpiration** from leaves returns a large share of rain on land back to the air.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Atmosphere", "b": "about 9 days"}, {"a": "Rivers", "b": "weeks to months"}, {"a": "Oceans", "b": "about 3,000 years"}, {"a": "Deep groundwater", "b": "up to millennia"}]}
### Portugal's rain
Atlantic weather systems drop most of their rain as they meet the first mountains, so the north-west is wet and the south dry. Parts of the Peneda-Gerês mountains get over 2,500 mm a year; much of the Alentejo gets around 500 mm or less.
> The Sun powers a planet-wide water pump that never stops, recycling the same water over and over.
!! Evaporation only happens from the oceans => Lakes, soil and especially plants put huge amounts of water back into the air

# Invasive species
Most species moved by people don't become a problem. But a few, freed from the predators, parasites and competitors of home, spread fast and harm local wildlife. These are **invasive species**.
### Rabbits in Australia
In 1859 Thomas Austin released 24 rabbits near Geelong for hunting. With mild winters and few predators, they multiplied into hundreds of millions, stripping vegetation and pushing native animals towards extinction. The myxoma virus, released in 1950, cut numbers dramatically, but rabbits evolved resistance.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Species arrives", "No natural enemies", "Rapid spread", "Native species suffer"]}
### The Asian hornet in Portugal
The yellow-legged Asian hornet (*Vespa velutina*) reached France in about 2004, probably in a shipment of pottery from China. It was first recorded in Portugal in 2011, in the Viana do Castelo district, and has since spread across much of the country. It hunts honeybees by hovering outside hives, and its nests can hold thousands of hornets.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~2004", "l": "Arrives in France"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "First seen in Portugal"}, {"y": "2010s", "l": "Spreads across north"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "Found in most regions"}]}
### Other invaders in Portugal

@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~60%", "l": "of extinctions: a factor"}, {"v": "$400+ bn", "l": "yearly global cost"}, {"v": "37,000+", "l": "species moved by people"}]}
These figures come from a 2023 global assessment by IPBES, the UN-backed science panel on biodiversity.

# Biodiversity
**Biodiversity** means the variety of life at every level: the genes within a species, the species in an area and the ecosystems across a landscape. About 2.2 million species have been described; estimates of the true total for plants, animals and fungi are around 8–9 million.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Ecosystems", "Species", "Genes"]}
### Why variety means stability
In long-running grassland experiments at Cedar Creek, Minnesota, led by David Tilman from the 1980s, plots with more plant species produced more and recovered faster from drought. Different species respond differently, so when some fail, others take up the slack: the **insurance effect**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Diverse system", "items": ["steady in drought", "more productive", "resists pests"]}, "r": {"t": "Monoculture", "items": ["fails together", "needs inputs", "pest outbreaks"]}}
### What we get from it
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Food", "b": "crops, fish, pollinators"}, {"a": "Medicines", "b": "aspirin from willow bark"}, {"a": "Clean water", "b": "forests and wetlands filter"}, {"a": "Climate", "b": "forests and soils store carbon"}]}
Roughly half of modern medicines derive from natural compounds or are inspired by them. Artemisinin, now a frontline malaria drug, came from sweet wormwood used in traditional Chinese medicine.
### It's declining
The WWF's Living Planet Index reported an average fall of 73% in monitored wildlife populations between 1970 and 2020. That figure is an average change in population sizes, not the share of animals lost.
!! Losing a few species makes no difference => Each loss weakens the web; ecosystems can absorb some losses, then suddenly collapse
> Biodiversity is life's insurance policy: the more variety, the better nature copes with shocks.

# A Portuguese montado
The **montado** covers over a million hectares, mostly in the Alentejo. It's a savanna-like mix of scattered cork and holm oaks above pasture, crops or scrub, shaped over centuries by farmers. Spain has the same system, called dehesa.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Cork and holm oak canopy", "Shrubs and young trees", "Pasture or cereal crops", "Grazing animals"]}
### The cork cycle
Cork is the oak's bark. It's first stripped when a tree is about 25 years old, then every 9 years, by hand with an axe. The tree isn't cut down and regrows its bark. Workers paint the last digit of the harvest year on the trunk.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cork stripped by hand", "Number painted on trunk", "Bark regrows for 9 years", "Harvested again"]}
Portugal holds about a third of the world's cork oak forest and produces around half the world's cork. The cork oak was named Portugal's national tree in 2011, and cutting one down needs official permission.
### Home for rare wildlife
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Iberian imperial eagle", "b": "back breeding since 2003"}, {"a": "Black vulture", "b": "nests in large oaks"}, {"a": "Common crane", "b": "winters on acorns"}, {"a": "Black pig", "b": "fattens on acorns"}]}
The Iberian imperial eagle, one of the world's rarest eagles, had stopped breeding in Portugal and now nests there again, with a few dozen pairs in recent years. The black Alentejo pig's acorn-fed diet gives famous cured ham.
### Under pressure
Montado is threatened by ageing trees, few young oaks surviving grazing, drought and a root disease caused by *Phytophthora*. Keeping it alive means keeping people working in it.
> The montado proves that farming and wildlife can thrive together when land is managed gently.
!? The Whistler Tree, a cork oak in Águas de Moura planted in 1783, once yielded enough cork in a single harvest for over 100,000 stoppers.

# The greenhouse effect
Sunlight arrives mostly as visible light, which passes easily through the air and warms the ground and sea. The warm surface then gives off energy as invisible **infrared** radiation. Certain gases absorb that infrared and send part of it back down, keeping heat in.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sunlight warms surface", "Surface gives off infrared", "Gases absorb some of it", "Heat sent back down"]}
### Which gases?
The two main gases in air, nitrogen and oxygen, make up about 99% of it but don't absorb infrared at all. The work is done by trace gases whose molecules can bend and stretch in step with infrared waves.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Water vapour", "b": "biggest share, follows temperature"}, {"a": "Carbon dioxide", "b": "long-lived, sets the thermostat"}, {"a": "Methane", "b": "~80x CO2 over 20 years"}, {"a": "Nitrous oxide", "b": "from fertilisers"}]}
Water vapour does more of the trapping than CO2, but it can't drive change on its own: excess rains out within days. CO2 lasts centuries, so it sets the temperature and water vapour amplifies it. Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour per °C.
### A 19th-century discovery
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1824", "l": "Fourier: air traps heat"}, {"y": "1856", "l": "Foote: CO2 warms air"}, {"y": "1859", "l": "Tyndall measures gases"}, {"y": "1896", "l": "Arrhenius: CO2 math"}]}
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "−18 °C", "l": "Earth with no greenhouse gases"}, {"v": "15 °C", "l": "Earth today"}, {"v": "464 °C", "l": "Venus"}]}
!? Venus is closer to the Sun, but its thick CO2 atmosphere makes it hotter than Mercury, which is closer still.
!! The greenhouse effect is bad => It keeps Earth livable; the problem is strengthening it by adding more gas
> A natural greenhouse effect makes Earth about 33 °C warmer and habitable; we are now turning it up.

# Rising CO₂
In 1958 the American scientist Charles David Keeling began measuring CO2 on Mauna Loa, a volcano in Hawaii far from cities. His first readings were about 315 ppm. The record, called the **Keeling Curve**, has risen every year since.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1750", "l": "About 280 ppm"}, {"y": "1958", "l": "Keeling: 315 ppm"}, {"y": "2013", "l": "Passes 400 ppm"}, {"y": "2025", "l": "About 425 ppm"}]}
ex: Today ≈ 425 ppm | Pre-industrial ≈ 280 ppm | Rise ≈ 145 ppm, about 50% more
### A breathing planet
The curve zigzags every year. Northern Hemisphere forests absorb CO2 as they grow in spring and summer, and release it in autumn and winter. Most land is in the north, so the whole planet's air "breathes" with the northern seasons.
### Older than ice
Bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice cores preserve ancient air. Over the past 800,000 years, CO2 swung between about 180 ppm in ice ages and about 300 ppm in warm periods. Today's level is far outside that range.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 150.0, "hi": 450.0, "unit": "CO2, ppm", "items": [{"l": "Ice age", "v": 180.0}, {"l": "Warm period", "v": 290.0}, {"l": "Today", "v": 425.0, "hi": true}]}
### How we know it's us
• Fossil carbon has a distinct chemical fingerprint, lacking carbon-14, and that fingerprint is growing in the air
• Oxygen is falling slightly, as expected from burning
• The rise matches the emissions we can count from fuel sales
Humans emit roughly 37–38 billion tonnes of CO2 a year from fossil fuels. About half stays in the air; oceans and land absorb the rest.
> CO2 is now about 50% higher than before industry, and the extra carbon carries the chemical signature of fossil fuels.

# Weather vs climate
**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a given moment: today's rain, tomorrow's wind. **Climate** is the statistics of weather over a long period, usually 30 years: the averages, the extremes and how often they happen.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Weather", "items": ["hours to days", "hard to predict far ahead", "today's storm"]}, "r": {"t": "Climate", "items": ["30-year averages", "predictable trends", "how often storms"]}}
### Why we can predict climate but not next month
Weather is chaotic: tiny differences grow, so forecasts lose skill after about 10 days. The meteorologist Edward Lorenz showed this in the 1960s, inspiring the phrase "butterfly effect". Climate is different: it asks about averages, which depend on the energy balance of the whole planet.
### Loading the dice
Think of weather as rolling dice. Warming doesn't decide each roll; it loads the dice so hot sixes come up more often. A single cold week still happens, but record highs now outnumber record lows by a wide margin.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Stable climate", "items": ["hot and cold records", "roughly balanced"]}, "r": {"t": "Warming climate", "items": ["hot records far more", "cold records rarer"]}}
### Portugal's changing summers
Portugal's official record high is about 47 °C, at Amareleja in the Alentejo in August 2003. Heatwaves and dry years have become more frequent, raising fire risk.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "global warming vs 1850-1900, °C", "items": [{"l": "1980s", "v": 0.4, "t": "~0.4"}, {"l": "2000s", "v": 0.8, "t": "~0.8"}, {"l": "2024", "v": 1.55, "t": "~1.55", "hi": true}]}
!! A cold winter proves global warming isn't real => One season is weather; climate shows in long-term trends across the whole globe
> Climate is what you expect; weather is what you get on the day.

# Ocean currents
Ocean currents are rivers in the sea. Surface currents are pushed mainly by winds, and steered by Earth's rotation and the shape of continents. Deep currents are driven by differences in temperature and saltiness, which change the water's density.
### The Gulf Stream
The Gulf Stream flows from the Gulf of Mexico along the US coast and across the Atlantic as the North Atlantic Drift. Off Florida it carries about 30 million m³ of water every second, more than all the world's rivers combined.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average January temperature, °C", "items": [{"l": "Lisbon (39°N)", "v": 11.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "New York (41°N)", "v": 0.5, "t": "~0"}, {"l": "Beijing (40°N)", "v": 0.1, "t": "~-3"}]}
Warmth also comes from westerly winds blowing over the ocean, which stores summer heat. Climate scientists debate how much of Europe's mildness is due to the current itself.
### The global conveyor
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Warm water flows north", "Cools, gets saltier", "Sinks near Greenland", "Deep flow south"]}
This overturning loop in the Atlantic is called the **AMOC**. A full trip of the global conveyor takes around 1,000 years. Studies suggest it has weakened, and melting Greenland ice could weaken it further; whether it might collapse this century is uncertain and actively studied.
### Upwelling off Portugal
In summer, northerly winds along Portugal's west coast push surface water offshore. Cold, nutrient-rich water rises from below to replace it. That's why the sea at Viana do Castelo or Peniche feels cold in August, and why Portuguese waters are so rich in sardines.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["North winds in summer", "Surface water pushed out", "Cold deep water rises", "Plankton and sardines thrive"]}
> Ocean currents are Earth's heating system, moving warmth from the tropics towards the poles.

# Plate tectonics
Earth's outer shell, the **lithosphere**, is cracked into about 15 major plates that ride on hotter, softer rock below. Heat from Earth's interior keeps the mantle slowly churning, and plates are pulled along, especially by their heavy sinking edges.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Divergent", "b": "plates part: new sea floor"}, {"a": "Convergent", "b": "plates meet: mountains, volcanoes"}, {"a": "Transform", "b": "plates slide: earthquakes"}]}
### A rejected idea
In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener argued that continents drift: South America and Africa fit like jigsaw pieces, with matching fossils and rocks. Geologists mocked him because he couldn't explain what moved them. In the 1960s, maps of the sea floor revealed magnetic stripes and spreading ridges, and the theory was accepted.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~300 Mya", "l": "Pangaea forms"}, {"y": "~180 Mya", "l": "Atlantic starts to open"}, {"y": "1912", "l": "Wegener's drift idea"}, {"y": "1960s", "l": "Plate tectonics proven"}]}
### Speeds
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "cm per year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Fingernail growth", "v": 3.5}, {"l": "Atlantic widening", "v": 2.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Pacific plate", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Africa vs Iberia", "v": 0.5}]}
ex: Atlantic widens ≈ 2.5 cm/yr | Over 1,000 years ≈ 25 m | Over 100 million years ≈ 2,500 km
### Visible results
• The Himalayas, still rising as India pushes into Asia
• Iceland, split by the Mid-Atlantic Ridge
• The Azores, born on the same ridge where three plates meet
!? GPS stations can now measure plate motion directly, to within millimetres per year.
> The ground beneath us is moving, too slowly to feel but fast enough to build mountains and open oceans.

# Portugal on the edge
South of Portugal runs the **Azores–Gibraltar fault zone**, the boundary between the Eurasian and African plates. Africa is pushing north-west against Iberia at only about 4–5 mm a year, but stress builds up for centuries and is released in sudden quakes.
### 1 November 1755
On All Saints' Day morning, with churches full, a huge earthquake struck. Its magnitude is estimated at about 8.5–9, and its source lay somewhere offshore, south-west of Cape St Vincent; the exact fault is still debated.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earthquake shakes Lisbon", "Tsunami floods the riverside", "Candles start fires", "Fires burn for days"]}
Tsunami waves several metres high rushed up the Tagus and hit the Algarve, Morocco and Spain. Fires burned for about five days. Estimates of deaths in Lisbon range widely, from roughly 12,000 to 50,000.
### Birth of earthquake science
The Marquês de Pombal led the rebuilding. The new Baixa had wide streets and buildings with a flexible timber frame, the **gaiola pombalina**, designed to sway without collapsing. Pombal also sent a questionnaire to every parish about the quake, an early attempt at scientific seismology.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1755", "l": "Great earthquake"}, {"y": "1909", "l": "Benavente quake"}, {"y": "1969", "l": "Offshore M ~7.9"}, {"y": "1980", "l": "Azores quake"}]}
### Today's risk
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lisbon and Algarve most at risk", "Azores: active plate junction", "Tsunami warning systems", "Seismic building codes"]}
!? The disaster shook European thought: Voltaire wrote a poem about it and mocked blind optimism in *Candide* (1759).
> Portugal sits on a slow-moving plate edge that rarely moves but, when it does, can reshape history.

# The rock cycle
Rocks look permanent, but over millions of years they're melted, worn down, buried, squeezed and lifted. The Scottish geologist James Hutton described this endless recycling in the 1780s, realising Earth must be vastly old.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Igneous: cooled magma", "Weathered to sediment", "Sedimentary: pressed", "Metamorphic: heat, squeeze"]}
### The three families
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Igneous", "b": "granite, basalt"}, {"a": "Sedimentary", "b": "limestone, sandstone, shale"}, {"a": "Metamorphic", "b": "marble, slate, schist"}]}
Granite cools slowly deep underground, so its crystals grow large enough to see. Basalt cools fast at the surface, so its crystals are tiny. Limestone often forms from the shells of sea creatures. Heat and pressure turn limestone into marble and mudstone into slate.
### Portugal is a rock museum
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Minho, Gerês", "b": "granite houses and walls"}, {"a": "Douro valley", "b": "schist under the vineyards"}, {"a": "Estremoz", "b": "fine white marble"}, {"a": "Azores, Madeira", "b": "volcanic basalt"}]}
The traditional pavement of Portuguese towns, the **calçada portuguesa**, uses white limestone and black basalt: a sedimentary and an igneous rock side by side under your feet.
### Shortcuts in the cycle
The cycle isn't a fixed loop. Any rock can be melted, any rock can be eroded, and metamorphic rock can be metamorphosed again. Plate tectonics provides the engine: subduction carries rock down, and mountain-building lifts it up.
!? Fossil sea shells are found near the top of Mount Everest, in limestone formed on an ancient ocean floor.
> Every rock is a stage in a slow cycle; the granite of the Minho was once molten, and may one day be sand.

# Layers of the Earth
Earth's radius is about 6,370 km. The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia, reached only about 12 km. Everything we know about deeper layers comes from how earthquake waves travel through the planet.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Crust: 5-70 km thick", "Mantle: to ~2,900 km", "Outer core: liquid iron", "Inner core: solid, ~5,200 °C"]}
### Reading earthquake waves
Earthquakes send out two main kinds of wave. P-waves squeeze and stretch and pass through solids and liquids. S-waves shake side to side and can't pass through liquid. A big "shadow" where S-waves never arrive revealed the liquid outer core. In 1936 the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann spotted faint echoes revealing a solid inner core.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "P-waves", "items": ["push-pull", "through solid and liquid", "arrive first"]}, "r": {"t": "S-waves", "items": ["side to side", "solids only", "arrive second"]}}
### The engine room
The inner core is solid despite its heat because the pressure is immense, over 3 million times that at the surface. Swirling liquid iron in the outer core generates Earth's **magnetic field**, which shields us from much of the solar wind and lets compasses work.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~5,200 °C", "l": "inner core"}, {"v": "~5,500 °C", "l": "Sun's surface"}, {"v": "~1%", "l": "of Earth's volume is crust"}]}
!! The mantle is a sea of molten lava => The mantle is solid rock that flows extremely slowly, like a glacier; magma forms only in pockets
> Earth is layered like an onion, and we've worked out the whole inside by listening to earthquakes.

# Ice ages
In the 1920s the Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković calculated how three slow changes in Earth's orbit alter how much summer sunlight reaches high northern latitudes. When northern summers are weak, winter snow survives, builds up and turns into ice sheets.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Orbit shape", "b": "~100,000-year cycle"}, {"a": "Axis tilt", "b": "~41,000-year cycle"}, {"a": "Axis wobble", "b": "~23,000-year cycle"}]}
### Small nudges, big feedbacks
The orbital changes alone are too small to explain ice ages. They're amplified: white ice reflects sunlight, cooling more; colder oceans absorb CO2, which fell to about 180 ppm at glacial peaks.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Weaker northern summers", "Snow survives, ice grows", "Ice reflects sunlight", "CO2 falls, colder still"]}
### The last glacial peak
About 20,000 years ago, ice sheets up to 3 km thick covered Scandinavia, Britain and Canada. Sea level was about 120 m lower, so Britain was joined to Europe and the Portuguese coast lay kilometres further west. Global average temperature was about 6 °C lower than before industry.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~120 m", "l": "lower sea level"}, {"v": "~6 °C", "l": "colder globally"}, {"v": "~3 km", "l": "thick ice sheets"}]}
Portugal had its own glaciers. In Serra da Estrela, a glacier carved the U-shaped Zêzere valley, which you can still see clearly today.
!? Before about a million years ago, ice ages came roughly every 41,000 years; why the rhythm switched to about 100,000 years is still debated.
> Ice ages are paced by Earth's orbit but powered by feedbacks from ice and CO2.

# What helps most
Not all climate actions are equal. A 2017 study by Seth Wynes and Kimberly Nicholas compared individual choices in rich countries and found a few big ones dwarf many small ones.

### Transport, homes, food
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Fly less, especially long-haul", "Electric or shared transport", "Heat pump and insulation", "Less beef, less food waste"]}
A heat pump delivers about 3–4 units of heat for every unit of electricity. Beef produces far more emissions per kilogram than poultry, and dozens of times more than beans or peas.
ex: Lisbon–New York return flight ≈ 11,000 km | ≈ 1–2 t CO2e per passenger | Comparable to months of an average European's other emissions
### Systems matter most
Individual choices help, but the biggest cuts come from changing systems: power grids, building standards, transport networks and protecting forests.

Portugal closed its last coal power station, Pego, in November 2021. Renewables now supply roughly 70% of its electricity in a typical year, led by wind, hydro and fast-growing solar.
!! Small habits like unplugging chargers make a big dent => They help a little; transport, heating and diet make far bigger differences
> Focus on the big levers: how we move, how we heat and what we eat, and support the systems that make the clean choice the easy one.

# A water planet
The ocean covers about 361 million km² and averages about 3,700 m deep. The Pacific alone is bigger than all Earth's land put together. Seen from space, Earth is unmistakably a blue planet.

# Ocean zones
Light fades rapidly with depth. Red light is absorbed in the first few metres, then orange and yellow, leaving only blue. That's why red animals in the deep look black, a perfect disguise.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Sunlight zone: 0-200 m", "Twilight zone: 200-1,000 m", "Midnight zone: 1,000-4,000 m", "Abyss: 4,000-6,000 m", "Hadal: trenches, 6,000 m+"]}
### The twilight zone
Not enough light reaches 200–1,000 m for photosynthesis, but enough for animals to see silhouettes against the faint glow above. Many fish here have huge upward-facing eyes. The **bristlemouth**, a fish the size of a finger, may be the most numerous vertebrate on Earth.
### The biggest migration on Earth
Every night, billions of tonnes of small animals rise from the twilight zone to feed near the surface, then sink back before dawn to hide from predators. This **daily vertical migration** is so large it shows up on ships' sonar as a moving "false bottom".
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Day: hide at 500 m", "Dusk: swim upwards", "Night: feed near surface", "Dawn: sink back down"]}
### Life in the dark
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Temperature", "b": "about 2-4 °C below 1,000 m"}, {"a": "Food", "b": "'marine snow' from above"}, {"a": "Light", "b": "only from living things"}]}
Deep-sea animals depend on **marine snow**: flakes of dead plankton, droppings and mucus drifting down from above. A flake may take weeks to reach the bottom.
!? The twilight zone may hold more fish by weight than the rest of the ocean combined, though estimates vary enormously.
> The ocean is layered by light, and each layer has its own way of life.

# The deepest point
The Mariana Trench curves for about 2,500 km through the western Pacific, near Guam. It's a **subduction zone**: the old, heavy Pacific plate dives beneath the smaller Mariana plate, dragging the seafloor down into a deep gash.

Even at the bottom, there's life: shrimp-like amphipods, sea cucumbers and microbes. The deepest fish filmed, a snailfish, was seen at about 8,300 m in 2022. Fish probably can't live much deeper, because the chemistry that protects their proteins from pressure reaches its limit.

# Pressure
Air presses on you with a weight of about 1 atmosphere at sea level. Water is much denser, so every 10 m of seawater adds roughly another atmosphere. At 10 m you feel twice the surface pressure; at 100 m, eleven times.
ex: Depth 10,900 m ÷ 10 m per atm ≈ 1,090 atm | + 1 atm of air ≈ 1,091 atm | ≈ 1.1 tonnes pressing on every square centimetre

### What happens to air
Gases squeeze. A balloon taken to 10 m shrinks to half its size; at 30 m, to a quarter. That's why scuba divers must rise slowly: gas dissolved in their blood under pressure can form bubbles if released too fast, causing **decompression sickness**.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "volume of a balloon of air, %", "items": [{"l": "Surface", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "10 m", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "30 m", "v": 25.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "90 m", "v": 10.0}]}
### How deep animals cope
Deep-sea animals have no air spaces to crush. Their bodies are mostly water, which barely compresses. Many fish carry a molecule called **TMAO** that stabilises proteins against pressure; it's also what gives fish their "fishy" smell when they decay.
!? Cuvier's beaked whales hold the record for deep-diving mammals: nearly 3,000 m, and one dive lasted almost 4 hours.
> Water pressure grows by about one atmosphere every 10 m, so deep-sea life is built without air spaces to crush.
!! Deep-sea fish explode when brought up => They don't explode, but any gas inside expands and soft tissues sag, which is why they look so strange on deck

# Bioluminescence
Animals make light with a chemical reaction: an enzyme called **luciferase** helps a molecule called luciferin react with oxygen, releasing energy as light with very little heat. Some animals make the chemicals themselves; others, like the anglerfish, host glowing bacteria.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Luciferin + oxygen", "Luciferase speeds reaction", "Energy released as light", "Almost no heat"]}
### Why glow?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lure prey", "b": "anglerfish's glowing rod"}, {"a": "Hide", "b": "belly lights cancel shadow"}, {"a": "Startle", "b": "vampire squid's glowing cloud"}, {"a": "Find mates", "b": "species-specific flashes"}]}
Hiding with light sounds odd, but it works. Seen from below, a fish's dark shape stands out against faint sunlight. Hatchetfish and some squid shine dim blue lights on their bellies to match the glow above, a trick called **counter-illumination**.
### Mostly blue
Blue-green light travels farthest in seawater, so most deep-sea glow is blue, and most deep-sea animals can only see blue. A few dragonfish produce red light, invisible to their prey, like a secret searchlight.
### On Portuguese beaches
On some summer nights, waves on Portuguese beaches sparkle blue. This is usually caused by dinoflagellates such as *Noctiluca*, single-celled plankton that flash when disturbed.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~76%", "l": "of deep animals glow"}, {"v": "~94", "l": "times light evolved"}, {"v": "2008", "l": "Nobel for jellyfish GFP"}]}
!? A glowing protein from a jellyfish, GFP, is now used in labs worldwide to tag cells and genes; its discoverers won the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
> In the dark ocean, light is a language: for hunting, hiding and finding a partner.

# Phytoplankton
Phytoplankton are microscopic algae and bacteria drifting in sunlit water. Most are far too small to see individually, but together they can colour the sea green, visible from space during spring blooms.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Diatoms", "b": "glass shells of silica"}, {"a": "Coccolithophores", "b": "chalk plates; made Dover cliffs"}, {"a": "Dinoflagellates", "b": "spin with two whip tails"}, {"a": "Cyanobacteria", "b": "tiniest, most numerous"}]}
### Small but mighty
Phytoplankton make up less than 1% of the weight of all photosynthesisers on Earth, yet do roughly half of all photosynthesis. The secret is speed: they can divide every day or so and are eaten almost as fast as they grow.

### Feeding the ocean

### The carbon pump
When phytoplankton die or are eaten, some of their carbon sinks to the deep sea in droppings and marine snow, where it can stay for centuries. This **biological pump** removes billions of tonnes of carbon from the surface each year.
The cyanobacterium *Prochlorococcus*, discovered only in the 1980s, is probably the most abundant photosynthetic organism on Earth.
!! Rainforests make most of our oxygen => Ocean phytoplankton produce about half, and forests use much of what they make
> The ocean's tiniest plants do about half the planet's photosynthesis.

# The ocean as a heat sink
Water can store far more heat than air. The top few metres of the ocean hold as much heat as the entire atmosphere. Since the 1970s, the ocean has soaked up over 90% of the extra energy trapped by greenhouse gases, slowing the warming we feel on land.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "where extra heat has gone, %", "items": [{"l": "Ocean", "v": 91.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Land", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Melting ice", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Atmosphere", "v": 1.0}]}
### The cost of a cooler sky
Warmer water expands. Together with melting ice, this has raised global sea level by about 20 cm since 1900, and the rate is now about 4 mm a year. Hotter seas also bring marine heatwaves that bleach corals and kill kelp forests.
### Acidification
CO2 dissolving in seawater forms carbonic acid. Average surface pH has fallen from about 8.2 to 8.1. The sea is still alkaline, but less so.
ex: pH is logarithmic | Drop of 0.1 = 10^0.1 ≈ 1.26 | About 26–30% more hydrogen ions (acidity)
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["CO2 dissolves in sea", "Forms carbonic acid", "Fewer carbonate ions", "Shells harder to build"]}
Oysters, mussels, corals and tiny sea snails called pteropods need carbonate to build shells. Oyster hatcheries in the US Pacific Northwest have already had mass die-offs linked to acidic water.
!? The ocean's heat uptake is so large that if it all went into the lower atmosphere instead, air temperatures would have risen by tens of degrees.
> The ocean has protected us from the worst of warming so far, but it's paying for it with heat and acid.

# Hydrothermal vents
In 1977 scientists in the submersible *Alvin* explored the Galápagos Rift, expecting bare rock. They found shimmering warm water and dense communities of giant tube worms, clams and crabs around it, 2,500 m down. It was one of biology's great surprises.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Seawater seeps into crust", "Heated by magma", "Gathers minerals", "Gushes out, up to ~400 °C"]}
### Life without sunlight
The base of these food webs is bacteria that get energy from chemicals, mainly hydrogen sulfide, a process called **chemosynthesis**. The water is far hotter than 100 °C but doesn't boil because of the enormous pressure.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Photosynthesis", "items": ["energy from sunlight", "plants and algae"]}, "r": {"t": "Chemosynthesis", "items": ["energy from chemicals", "vent bacteria"]}}
### Giant tube worms
*Riftia* tube worms grow up to about 2 m long and have no mouth or gut. Inside them live billions of bacteria that turn vent chemicals into food. The worm's red plume is coloured by haemoglobin, which carries oxygen and sulfide to its bacterial partners.
### Vents near Portugal
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lucky Strike", "b": "Azores, ~1,700 m deep"}, {"a": "Menez Gwen", "b": "Azores, ~850 m deep"}, {"a": "Rainbow", "b": "Azores, ~2,300 m deep"}]}
These vent fields lie on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge within Portugal's waters. Black smokers there pour out metal-rich water, attracting interest in seabed mining; Portugal has backed a pause until the effects are better understood.
!? Some scientists think life on Earth may have begun at hydrothermal vents, where chemical energy and minerals were concentrated.
> Hydrothermal vents showed that life doesn't need the Sun: chemical energy from Earth itself can feed whole ecosystems.

# Plastic in the sea
Estimates of how much plastic reaches the ocean each year vary from around 1 to over 10 million tonnes. It comes from rivers, beaches, fishing and wastewater. Only about 9% of all plastic ever made has been recycled.
### Where it ends up

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch, a zone where currents gather floating debris, covers about 1.6 million km². It isn't a solid island but a thin soup of fragments; by weight, almost half is lost or dumped fishing gear.

Sunlight and waves break plastic into ever smaller pieces. Pieces under 5 mm are called **microplastics**. They've been found in Arctic ice, deep-sea sediments, Mariana Trench animals, seafood, table salt and human blood.

# The age of dinosaurs
Geologists call the age of dinosaurs the **Mesozoic** era, which ran from about 252 to 66 million years ago. It began after the worst mass extinction in Earth's history and ended with an asteroid.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "252 Ma", "l": "Triassic begins"}, {"y": "230 Ma", "l": "First dinosaurs"}, {"y": "201 Ma", "l": "Jurassic begins"}, {"y": "145 Ma", "l": "Cretaceous begins"}, {"y": "66 Ma", "l": "Asteroid strikes"}]}
### A slow start
The earliest known dinosaurs, such as Eoraptor and Herrerasaurus from Argentina, were small to medium two-legged animals. For their first 30 million years they shared the land with crocodile relatives that were often bigger and more common. A mass extinction about 201 million years ago wiped out many of those rivals, and dinosaurs took over.
### The end
About 66 million years ago an asteroid roughly 10 km wide hit what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, leaving the Chicxulub crater, about 180 km across. Dust and soot blocked sunlight, food chains collapsed, and all dinosaurs died out except the birds.
ex: Non-bird dinosaurs existed ≈ 165 million years | Homo sapiens so far ≈ 0.3 million years | Dinosaurs lasted about 550 times longer
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "time on Earth, million years", "items": [{"l": "Non-bird dinosaurs", "v": 164.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mammoths", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Homo sapiens", "v": 0.3}]}
!? During the Mesozoic the supercontinent Pangaea split apart and the Atlantic Ocean opened, slowly separating Portugal from North America.
> Dinosaurs ruled for over 160 million years; our species has been around for about 0.3 million.

# T. rex and Stegosaurus
"Prehistoric" squashes a vast span of time into one word. Stegosaurus lived in the Late Jurassic, about 155 to 150 million years ago. Tyrannosaurus rex lived at the very end of the Cretaceous, about 68 to 66 million years ago.

### Different worlds
In Stegosaurus's time there were no flowering plants; the landscape was ferns, cycads and conifers. By T. rex's time flowers, and many modern insects that pollinate them, had spread across the land. Stegosaurus's predator was Allosaurus, not T. rex.

### Getting a feel for it
A similar trick works with human history. Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid of Giza. Deep time is so long that even "ancient" things are spread far apart.
!? Stegosaurus fossils have also been found in Portugal, in the Late Jurassic rocks around Lourinhã.
!! All dinosaurs lived together => Different dinosaurs were separated by tens of millions of years
> T. rex is closer in time to your smartphone than to Stegosaurus.

# Birds are dinosaurs
Scientists classify animals by shared ancestry, like a family tree. Birds sit inside the dinosaur tree, on the branch of **theropods**, the mostly meat-eating two-legged dinosaurs that also include T. rex and Velociraptor.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Dinosaurs", "items": ["Ornithischians: Stegosaurus", "Sauropods: Diplodocus", "Theropods: T. rex", "Theropods: birds"]}
### The evidence
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Wishbone (furcula)", "Hollow, air-filled bones", "Feathers and quill knobs", "Three forward toes", "Sitting on nests to brood"]}
In 1861, just two years after Darwin's Origin of Species, quarry workers in Germany found **Archaeopteryx**, about 150 million years old, with feathered wings but also teeth, claws and a bony tail. In the 1960s the American palaeontologist John Ostrom noticed how much the small predator Deinonychus resembled birds. Since the 1990s, feathered dinosaur fossils from China have settled the question.
### Who's related to whom
T. rex and a sparrow share a common ancestor within the theropods. To find the ancestor T. rex shares with Stegosaurus, you have to go back to the very base of the dinosaur family tree.
!? There are about 11,000 species of birds today, nearly twice as many as there are species of mammals, so dinosaurs are arguably still thriving.
> Dinosaurs didn't all die out: about 11,000 species of them are flying around today.

# Feathers
In 1996 Chinese scientists described **Sinosauropteryx**, a small predator from Liaoning province covered in simple fuzzy filaments. It was the first non-bird dinosaur found with feathers. Since then dozens of feathered species have turned up.
### Colours from fossils
Feathers contain tiny pigment packets called **melanosomes**. Their shape reveals their colour: sausage shapes for black and grey, rounder ones for reddish brown. In 2010 scientists used this to show Sinosauropteryx had a ginger-and-white striped tail and Anchiornis had black-and-white wings with a reddish crest.

### Feathers came before flight

Early feathers were for warmth and display, not flight. Yutyrannus, a 9 m relative of T. rex described in 2012, was covered in long filaments. Some plant-eating dinosaurs also had fuzz, so feathers may go back to the earliest dinosaurs.

# Giants
**Sauropods** were long-necked plant-eaters with small heads, pillar-like legs and long tails. They include Brachiosaurus, Diplodocus and the true giants of the Cretaceous, the titanosaurs.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "body mass, tonnes (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Argentinosaurus", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "T. rex", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "African elephant", "v": 6.0}]}
### How big, really?
Argentinosaurus lived about 95 million years ago in what is now Argentina. It is known from only a few bones, including huge vertebrae, so its size is estimated by comparison with better-known relatives. Estimates run from about 30 to 35 m long and 60 to 75 tonnes. The blue whale is heavier, but water supports its weight.
### How they managed
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Air-filled vertebrae", "b": "strong but light"}, {"a": "Bird-like air sacs", "b": "efficient breathing"}, {"a": "Long neck", "b": "graze without walking"}, {"a": "No chewing", "b": "swallow, digest in gut"}]}
Sauropods had a bird-like breathing system with air sacs that reached into their bones, making their skeletons lighter. By not chewing, they could keep their heads small and their necks long.
### Small eggs, fast growth
Their eggs were only about the size of a football. Babies grew enormously fast, gaining perhaps several kilograms a day at their peak.
!? Portugal had its own sauropod, Lusotitan, a Brachiosaurus relative from the Late Jurassic rocks around Lourinhã.
> Air-filled bones and bird-like breathing let sauropods become the largest animals ever to walk on land.

# Not dinosaurs
"Dinosaur" has a precise scientific meaning. Dinosaurs share features of the hips and legs: their legs were held straight under the body, like a horse, not sprawled out sideways like a lizard. The hip socket has a hole right through it.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Pterosaurs", "b": "flying reptiles, wings of skin"}, {"a": "Plesiosaurs", "b": "long-necked sea reptiles"}, {"a": "Ichthyosaurs", "b": "dolphin-shaped sea reptiles"}, {"a": "Mosasaurs", "b": "giant sea lizards"}]}
### Pterosaurs
Pterosaurs were the first vertebrates to fly with flapping wings, about 80 million years before birds. Their wings were skin membranes stretched from a hugely long fourth finger. Quetzalcoatlus had a wingspan of about 10 to 11 m, like a small plane. They were close cousins of dinosaurs but on a separate branch.
### Sea monsters
Marine reptiles evolved from land reptiles that returned to the sea. Mosasaurs are actually lizards, related to monitor lizards and snakes. Many of the first ichthyosaur and plesiosaur skeletons were found in the early 1800s by the English fossil hunter Mary Anning.

!! Dimetrodon, the sail-backed beast, was a dinosaur => It lived 40 million years before them and is closer to mammals
> Pterosaurs ruled the air and marine reptiles the sea, but only land animals with upright legs were dinosaurs.

# How fossils form
Most dead animals are eaten, rot or are scattered within weeks. To become a fossil, a body usually needs to be buried quickly, out of reach of scavengers and oxygen.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Animal dies near water", "Quickly buried in mud", "Soft parts rot", "Minerals fill bones", "Rock eroded: exposed"]}
### Turning to stone
Groundwater carrying dissolved minerals such as silica or calcite seeps through buried bone. The minerals fill the tiny spaces and can gradually replace the original material. The bone keeps its shape but becomes rock, a process called **permineralisation**.
### Kinds of fossil

### Why so few
Hard parts fossilise far better than soft ones, and lakes, rivers and seabeds are better than forests or mountains. Then the rock has to survive millions of years of heat and pressure, be lifted to the surface, and be found before it erodes away. Only a tiny fraction of all animals that ever lived became fossils, and an even tinier fraction have been found.
Rare sites with exceptional preservation, such as the Solnhofen limestone in Germany or volcanic ash beds in China, keep feathers and even skin.
!! Fossils are the original bones => Most dinosaur fossils are rock that has copied the bone's shape
> A fossil is usually a rock copy of a body that was buried fast and lucky enough to survive.

# Dating fossils
There are two kinds of dating. **Relative dating** puts rocks in order: in undisturbed layers, the lower ones are older. The Danish scientist Nicolas Steno set out this principle in 1669. **Absolute dating** gives an age in years.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Sandstone: younger", "Volcanic ash: 70 Ma", "Fossil layer: in between", "Volcanic ash: 72 Ma", "Shale: older"]}
### Radioactive clocks
Some elements in volcanic rock are radioactive and decay at a fixed rate. Each has a **half-life**, the time for half of it to decay. By measuring how much has turned into its "daughter" element, scientists work out when the rock cooled.
ex: Uranium-238 half-life ≈ 4.5 billion years | Rock with 1% decayed | Age ≈ 65 million years
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Uranium to lead", "b": "half-life 4.5 billion years"}, {"a": "Potassium to argon", "b": "half-life 1.25 billion yrs"}, {"a": "Carbon-14", "b": "only up to ~50,000 years"}]}
### The sandwich
Fossils themselves rarely contain the right minerals. So scientists date volcanic ash layers above and below, and the fossil must lie between those ages. **Index fossils**, such as ammonites that lived for short, known periods, help match layers between distant sites.
!! Dinosaurs are carbon dated => Carbon-14 is useless beyond about 50,000 years; dinosaurs need uranium or argon
> Rock layers give the order; radioactive clocks in volcanic ash give the dates.

# Discovering dinosaurs
People found dinosaur bones for centuries without knowing what they were; some were taken for giants or dragons. Scientific study began in England in the 1820s.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1824", "l": "Megalosaurus"}, {"y": "1842", "l": "'Dinosauria' coined"}, {"y": "1854", "l": "Crystal Palace models"}, {"y": "1877", "l": "Bone Wars begin"}]}
### Naming the group
The anatomist Richard Owen noticed that Megalosaurus, Iguanodon and Hylaeosaurus shared features no living reptile had. In 1842 he grouped them as **Dinosauria**, from Greek deinos, "terribly great", and sauros, "lizard". Owen meant "awe-inspiringly big" as much as "terrible".
### The Bone Wars
In the late 1800s two American palaeontologists, Othniel Marsh and Edward Cope, raced to find new species in the American West. Their bitter rivalry included bribery and smashing fossils to keep them from each other. Together they named well over 100 species, including Stegosaurus and Triceratops.
### Still discovering
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1842", "l": "'Dinosauria' coined"}, {"v": "1,000+", "l": "species named so far"}, {"v": "~50", "l": "new species a year"}]}
Today about 50 new species are named each year, many from China, Argentina and the USA. The total is now well over 1,000. Better imaging, such as CT scans, also reveals details like brain shape and inner ear inside old fossils.
!? Many experts think we know only a small share of the dinosaur species that ever lived.
> We are living through a golden age of dinosaur discovery, with about one new species a week.

== na-birds

# Built for flight
Flight is the most energy-hungry way to move. Almost everything about a bird's body is shaped by two needs: keep weight down and deliver huge amounts of power.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hollow bones", "b": "internal struts, light"}, {"a": "Keel on breastbone", "b": "anchors flight muscles"}, {"a": "Beak, no teeth", "b": "lighter head"}, {"a": "Fused skeleton", "b": "rigid frame for flight"}]}
### Muscle power
The main flight muscles can make up around a quarter of a flying bird's body mass. They attach to a deep ridge on the breastbone called the **keel**. The downstroke muscle is the breast meat you eat on a chicken.
### One-way breathing
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Mammal lungs", "items": ["air in and out", "stale air mixes in", "less oxygen extracted"]}, "r": {"t": "Bird lungs", "items": ["air flows one way", "air sacs as bellows", "fresh air in both breaths"]}}
Birds have about nine air sacs that act as bellows, pushing air through the lungs in one direction during both breathing in and breathing out. That lets them extract oxygen where mammals would struggle.
### High flyers
Bar-headed geese migrate across the Himalayas, reaching altitudes of over 6,000 m. In 1973 a Rüppell's vulture collided with a plane over Ivory Coast at about 11,300 m.
!! Bird skeletons are unusually light => They weigh about the same share of body mass as a mammal's; the bones are hollow but denser
> Birds fly on hollow, strutted bones, a keel of muscle and lungs that breathe in one direction.

# Feathers
Feathers are the one feature only birds have today. They are made of **keratin**, the protein family in your nails and hair, though birds use a different, stiffer type.
### How a feather is built
A central shaft, the **rachis**, carries rows of branches called barbs. Each barb has smaller barbules with tiny hooks that lock onto their neighbours like Velcro. When a feather splits, preening zips it back together.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Rachis: central shaft", "Barbs: side branches", "Barbules: smaller branches", "Hooklets: zip it together"]}
### Different jobs
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Flight feathers", "b": "stiff, give lift and steering"}, {"a": "Contour feathers", "b": "streamline the body"}, {"a": "Down", "b": "fluffy insulation"}, {"a": "Bristles", "b": "protect eyes and nostrils"}]}
Waterbirds spread oil from a gland near the tail to keep feathers waterproof. Feathers wear out, so most birds replace them at least once a year in a **moult**.
### Colour tricks
Some colours are pigments, like the pink of flamingos, which comes from carotenoids in their food. Others are structural: the blue of a jay or kingfisher comes from microscopic structures that scatter light. Grind a blue feather and the blue disappears.
!? A ruby-throated hummingbird has about 1,000 feathers; a swan can have about 25,000.
> Feathers are keratin built like a zip, specialised for flight, warmth, waterproofing and show.

# Bird intelligence
"Bird brain" used to be an insult. Bird brains are small, but in parrots and songbirds the neurons are packed very densely. A 2016 study found some parrots and crows have as many neurons in their forebrain as some monkeys.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "New Caledonian crows", "b": "craft hooks from twigs"}, {"a": "Magpies", "b": "recognise themselves in mirrors"}, {"a": "Scrub jays", "b": "re-hide food if watched"}, {"a": "African grey parrots", "b": "learn words and counting"}]}
### Tools and planning
In 2002 a captive New Caledonian crow called Betty, at Oxford, bent a straight piece of wire into a hook to lift a food bucket from a tube. Wild crows of the same species make hooked tools from twigs and leaves and keep them for later use.
### Remembering faces
In Seattle, researchers trapped crows while wearing a particular mask. For years afterwards, crows scolded anyone wearing that mask, including birds that had never been caught. They had learned from the others.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Person in mask traps crow", "Crows remember the face", "Others learn by watching", "Whole flock scolds mask"]}
### A different design
Bird brains are organised differently from ours, with nerve cells in clusters rather than layers. Similar intelligence evolved twice, in mammals and in birds, from different wiring.
!! Small brain means low intelligence => Corvid and parrot brains pack neurons densely and rival apes on some tasks
> Crows and parrots pack primate-like numbers of neurons into small brains, and use them to plan, make tools and remember.

# Bird song
About half of all bird species are **songbirds**, which learn their songs. Parrots and hummingbirds learn sounds too. Among mammals, vocal learning is rare: humans, whales, dolphins, some bats and elephants.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chick hears adult song", "Memorises template", "Practises 'subsong'", "Matches template", "Crystallised song"]}
### Like learning to talk
A young songbird has a sensitive period for listening, much as children absorb language easily when young. It then practises with quiet, rambling **subsong**, like a baby's babbling, gradually refining it. A bird raised without hearing an adult sings an abnormal song. A gene called FOXP2 is involved in vocal learning in both birds and people.
### Dialects
Because songs are copied, they drift from place to place. In the 1960s, the American biologist Peter Marler showed that white-crowned sparrows around San Francisco Bay had distinct local dialects. Chaffinches in Europe also have regional variations.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Song", "items": ["learned, complex", "mostly in breeding season", "attract mates, hold land"]}, "r": {"t": "Call", "items": ["short, often innate", "all year", "alarm, contact"]}}
### Two voices
Birds sing with a **syrinx**, an organ where the windpipe splits into two. Each side can work separately, so some birds sing two notes at once.
!? In temperate regions mostly males sing, but in many tropical species females sing too, and some pairs sing duets.
> Songbirds learn their songs from adults during a sensitive period, so local dialects spread like accents.

# Migration
An estimated 2 billion small birds fly from Europe to Africa each autumn. They follow seasonal food: insects vanish in a European winter but are plentiful south of the Sahara.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "one-way distance, km (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Swallow", "v": 10000.0}, {"l": "Godwit nonstop", "v": 13500.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Arctic tern", "v": 35000.0}]}
### Record breakers
In 2022 a young bar-tailed godwit flew about 13,500 km nonstop from Alaska to Tasmania in 11 days. Arctic terns travel from the Arctic to Antarctica and back each year, about 70,000 km on a wandering route.
### The navigation toolkit
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sun", "b": "compass plus internal clock"}, {"a": "Stars", "b": "spin around the Pole Star"}, {"a": "Magnetic field", "b": "sensed by eyes or beak"}, {"a": "Landmarks and smell", "b": "coasts, rivers, odours"}]}
In the 1960s Stephen Emlen raised indigo buntings in a planetarium and showed they learn north by watching which point the night sky rotates around. Birds may sense Earth's magnetic field using a protein called cryptochrome in their eyes, though how exactly is still debated.
### Portugal on the flyway
Portugal sits on a major route. In autumn, Sagres and Cabo de São Vicente see thousands of raptors and storks heading south, and the Tagus estuary hosts tens of thousands of wintering wading birds.
!? Caged migratory birds become restless at night during migration season, fluttering in the direction they would fly. Scientists call it Zugunruhe, "migration restlessness".
> Migrating birds combine a sun clock, star map, magnetic sense and memory to cross continents.

# Storks in Portugal
The white stork is one of Portugal's most visible birds: tall, white with black wing feathers, a red bill and red legs. Storks are almost silent; instead of singing they clatter their bills loudly at the nest.
### A changing migration
Traditionally, nearly all European white storks flew to Africa south of the Sahara for the winter, soaring on rising warm air over the Strait of Gibraltar. Since the 1980s, more and more stay in Iberia all year.

### Why they stay
• Open landfill sites provide food all year; GPS-tracked storks in Portugal visit them daily
• Rice fields host the invasive red swamp crayfish, an easy meal
• Milder winters make staying less risky
Portugal's breeding population has grown many times over since the 1980s, to over 10,000 pairs. EU rules phasing out open landfills could change their habits again.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Landfill food all year", "No need to migrate", "Better survival", "Population grows"]}
### Nests on high
Stork nests are huge piles of sticks reused and enlarged each year, sometimes over a metre wide. They crown church towers, chimneys and electricity pylons, where power companies often fit platforms to keep nests away from live wires.
!? The legend of storks delivering babies comes from northern European folklore, where storks returning in spring were seen as bringers of new life.
> Portugal's storks increasingly skip Africa, staying all year thanks to landfills, rice fields and milder winters.

# The fastest and the biggest
Birds range from a bee-sized hummingbird to flightless giants, and from slow fliers to the fastest animal on Earth.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2.7 m", "l": "ostrich height"}, {"v": "~2 g", "l": "bee hummingbird weight"}, {"v": "3.5 m", "l": "wandering albatross wings"}]}
### The ostrich
Male ostriches can weigh around 150 kg. Their long legs have only two toes, and they can sprint at about 70 km/h and keep up 50 km/h for long distances. An ostrich egg weighs about 1.4 kg, about as much as two dozen hen's eggs.
### The bee hummingbird
Found only in Cuba, the bee hummingbird is about 5 to 6 cm long and weighs about 2 g, less than a 1-cent euro coin (2.3 g). Its nest is the size of a small cup.
### The fastest
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "top speed, km/h (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Peregrine dive", "v": 320.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Common swift", "v": 110.0}, {"l": "Ostrich run", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Usain Bolt", "v": 45.0}]}
The peregrine falcon dives on prey at over 300 km/h, the fastest speed of any animal. Peregrines breed on cliffs along the Portuguese coast and sometimes on city buildings.
!? Common swifts, which nest in Lisbon's old buildings each summer, can stay airborne for about 10 months without landing, even sleeping on the wing.
> Birds span from a 2 g hummingbird to a 150 kg ostrich, and include the fastest animal alive.

# Hummingbirds
There are about 360 species of hummingbird, all living in the Americas. They are built for hovering in front of flowers to drink nectar.
### How they hover
Most birds get lift only on the downstroke. Hummingbirds rotate their wings at the shoulder, tracing a figure of eight, so they get lift on the upstroke too, about a quarter of the total. That lets them hover, and fly sideways and backwards.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "wingbeats per second (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Small hummingbird", "v": 80.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Large hummingbird", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Pigeon", "v": 7.0}]}
### Living fast
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1,200", "l": "heartbeats a minute"}, {"v": "~250", "l": "breaths a minute"}, {"v": "360", "l": "species"}]}
Hummingbirds have the highest metabolism of any warm-blooded animal relative to size. They feed every few minutes during the day. At night many go into **torpor**: body temperature drops steeply and metabolism falls by up to about 95%, saving energy until morning.
### A European past
Fossils about 30 million years old found in Germany show that birds very close to modern hummingbirds once lived in Europe. Later they disappeared from the Old World.
!? The ruby-throated hummingbird, weighing about 3 g, crosses the Gulf of Mexico nonstop, about 800 km, on its migration.
> Hummingbirds hover by getting lift on both wing strokes, powered by the fastest metabolism of any bird.

# Penguins
There are 18 species of penguin, all in the Southern Hemisphere. The Galápagos penguin is the only one that reaches just north of the equator.
### Flying underwater
Penguins' wings became stiff flippers. Unlike flying birds, they have solid, heavy bones, which help them dive. Dark backs and white bellies hide them from predators above and below.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "deepest recorded dive, m (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Emperor penguin", "v": 560.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "King penguin", "v": 340.0}, {"l": "Gentoo penguin", "v": 210.0}]}
### The emperor's winter
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Female lays egg, leaves", "Male holds egg on feet", "Huddle through winter", "Chick hatches, female back"]}
Emperor penguins breed on sea ice in the Antarctic winter. After the female lays a single egg, she passes it to the male and heads to sea to feed. He balances it on his feet under a fold of skin for about two months, in temperatures that can fall below −40 °C with wind chill. Thousands of males huddle, slowly rotating so each takes a turn on the cold outside.
ex: Male fasts about 4 months | Starts at about 38 kg | Can lose up to about 40% of his body weight
### A warming threat
Emperors need stable sea ice from April to December. Recent years of record-low Antarctic sea ice have caused colonies to fail, and scientists warn many could vanish by 2100.
!? If the female is late, the male can feed the newly hatched chick a protein-rich secretion from his oesophagus.
> Penguins traded flight in air for flight in water, and emperors raise chicks through the harshest winter on Earth.

Birdwatching needs little equipment and works anywhere, from a balcony in Porto to a beach in the Algarve. The key is learning to look systematically.

== na-insects

# Insect numbers
Scientists have named about a million insect species, more than all other animals put together. Most estimates of the true total land around 5 million, though some go far higher. Every year roughly 10,000 new insect species are described, many from tropical forests where few people have looked.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "described species, approx.", "items": [{"l": "Insects", "v": 1000000.0, "t": "~1 million", "hi": true}, {"l": "Other animals", "v": 500000.0, "t": "~0.5 million"}, {"l": "Plants", "v": 380000.0, "t": "~380,000"}]}
### Beetles lead the way
About 400,000 beetle species are known, roughly a quarter of all described animals. A famous quip, attributed to the biologist J. B. S. Haldane, says the creator must have had "an inordinate fondness for beetles".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Beetles", "b": "~400,000 species"}, {"a": "Butterflies, moths", "b": "~160,000 species"}, {"a": "Flies", "b": "~150,000 species"}, {"a": "Bees, wasps, ants", "b": "~150,000 species"}]}
### Why so many?
• Small size: one tree can host hundreds of species, each using a different part
• Wings: insects were the first animals to fly, over 300 million years ago
• Fast generations: some breed many times a year, so evolution moves quickly
• Metamorphosis: young and adults eat different food and don't compete
ex: About 10 quintillion insects alive | About 8 billion people | Over 1 billion insects per person
!? Insects have survived all five mass extinctions, including the asteroid that ended the dinosaurs 66 million years ago.
> By species count, Earth is a planet of insects, and beetles above all.

# Body plan
Every insect is built on the same pattern. The **head** carries the eyes, a pair of antennae and the mouthparts. The **thorax** is the engine room, holding all six legs and the wings. The **abdomen** contains most of the gut, the heart and the reproductive organs.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Head: eyes, antennae, mouth", "Thorax: 6 legs, wings", "Abdomen: gut, eggs, breathing"]}
### Armour on the outside
Insects have no internal skeleton. Instead they wear an **exoskeleton** made largely of chitin, a tough material that protects them and stops them drying out. Because it can't stretch, a growing insect must moult: it splits its old skin and pumps itself up before the new one hardens.
### Breathing through holes
Insects have no lungs. Air enters through small holes along the sides called spiracles, then travels down branching tubes straight to the tissues. This system works well over short distances, one reason insects stay small today. About 300 million years ago, when the air held more oxygen, dragonfly-like insects reached wingspans of around 70 cm.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Insect", "items": ["6 legs", "3 body parts", "antennae, wings"]}, "r": {"t": "Spider", "items": ["8 legs", "2 body parts", "no antennae or wings"]}}
• Flies have only one pair of wings; the second pair became tiny balancing knobs
• Beetles' front wings became hard cases that protect the flying wings beneath
• Fleas, lice and silverfish have no wings at all
!! Spiders, woodlice and centipedes are insects => They are arthropods, cousins of insects; insects are only those with six legs and three body parts
> Count the legs: six legs on a three-part body means an insect.

# Metamorphosis
Most insect species go through **complete metamorphosis**: egg, larva, pupa, adult. Beetles, flies, bees, ants, butterflies and moths all do it. The larva is an eating machine; a caterpillar can end up thousands of times heavier than when it hatched.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Egg on a leaf", "Caterpillar eats, grows", "Chrysalis rebuilds body", "Butterfly lays eggs"]}
### Soup, but not quite
Inside the chrysalis, digestive enzymes break down much of the caterpillar's tissue. But some parts survive. Small clusters of cells called **imaginal discs** have waited, dormant, since the egg. They now use the soup as raw material to build wings, legs, antennae and adult eyes. Hormones decide the timing: when a hormone that keeps the insect "young" drops, the next moult becomes the change into a pupa.
### The other way to grow up
Grasshoppers, dragonflies, cockroaches and bugs use **incomplete metamorphosis**. Their young, called nymphs, look like small wingless adults and grow through a series of moults.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Complete", "items": ["egg, larva, pupa, adult", "young look different", "butterflies, beetles"]}, "r": {"t": "Incomplete", "items": ["egg, nymph, adult", "young look similar", "grasshoppers"]}}
Complete metamorphosis has a big advantage: larvae and adults live different lives and don't compete for food. It may help explain why the groups that use it make up the large majority of insect species.
!? In a 2008 experiment, caterpillars trained to avoid a smell still avoided it as adult moths, so some memory survives the rebuild.
> A chrysalis isn't resting: it's a construction site that turns an eating body into a flying, breeding one.

# Pollinators
Many plants can't move their pollen themselves, so they pay animals to do it with nectar and spare pollen. A 2007 global study found that about three-quarters of leading food crops benefit from animal pollination. Insects do most of this work: bees above all, but also hoverflies, butterflies, moths, beetles and wasps.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bee visits for nectar", "Pollen sticks to hairs", "Pollen lands on next flower", "Seed and fruit form"]}
### Which foods depend on it?
The world's staple grains, such as wheat, rice and maize, are pollinated by the wind. So pollinators supply a smaller share of our calories, but a large share of our vitamins and variety.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Need insects", "items": ["apples, cherries", "almonds, melons", "coffee, cocoa"]}, "r": {"t": "Wind or self", "items": ["wheat, rice", "maize, barley", "oats"]}}
### Wild bees matter too
Honeybees get the attention, but Europe has about 2,000 species of wild bee. Many are better at certain crops. Bumblebees, for example, shake tomato flowers with a fast buzz that releases pollen honeybees can't reach.
### What it's worth
In the EU, about €15 billion of farm output a year is linked directly to insect pollinators. In Portugal, apples from Alcobaça, cherries from Fundão and the fast-growing almond orchards of the Alentejo all rely on them.

!! Honeybees do all the pollinating => Wild bees, hoverflies and other insects do much of it, and some crops depend on them
> Pollinators don't feed us bread, but they give us most of our colour and flavour.

# Bees
A honeybee colony is a family. In summer it may hold 20,000 to 60,000 workers, all daughters of one queen. The queen can lay up to about 2,000 eggs a day. Drones are males; their only role is to mate with a queen from another colony, and they are pushed out in autumn.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Honeybee colony", "items": ["Queen: lays the eggs", "Workers: all female", "Drones: males, mate"]}
### A career that changes with age
A worker's job depends on how old she is, as her glands develop and then change:
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Days 1-3: clean cells", "Days 3-12: feed larvae", "Days 12-18: build wax", "Day 21+: guard, forage"]}
Summer workers live only about five to six weeks; the last weeks are spent flying. Bees born in autumn can live for months, keeping the queen warm through winter.
### Queen or worker?
Queens and workers come from the same kind of egg. The difference is food: a larva fed lots of **royal jelly** becomes a queen. Drones grow from unfertilised eggs.
### The waggle dance
A forager who finds good flowers returns and dances. The angle of her waggle run shows the direction relative to the Sun; its length shows the distance. Karl von Frisch decoded this and shared the 1973 Nobel Prize.
ex: One forager makes about 1/12 teaspoon of honey in her life, roughly 0.6 g | A 500 g jar ÷ 0.6 g | The lifetime work of about 800 bees
!? The Asian hornet, which hunts honeybees, was first detected in Portugal around Viana do Castelo in 2011 and has since spread across much of the country.
> A colony works like one big body, and each bee's job is set by her age.

# Ants
About 14,000 ant species are known. All live in colonies, from a few dozen workers to many millions. Like bees, most workers are sterile females, and like bees they evolved from wasp ancestors.
### Farmers and builders
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Aphid farmers", "b": "guard aphids, drink honeydew"}, {"a": "Leafcutters", "b": "grow fungus on cut leaves"}, {"a": "Army ants", "b": "build bridges from bodies"}, {"a": "Fire ants", "b": "form rafts in floods"}]}
Leafcutter ants of the Americas don't eat the leaves they carry. They chew them into a compost that feeds a fungus, and the fungus feeds the colony. They began farming about 50 million years ago, long before humans did.
### How many ants?
A 2022 study estimated about 20 quadrillion ants on Earth. Their combined dry carbon mass, about 12 million tonnes, is more than all wild birds and wild mammals combined, and about a fifth of the mass of all humans.
ex: About 20 quadrillion ants | About 8 billion people | About 2.5 million ants per person

### A supercolony in Europe
The Argentine ant, brought by human trade, forms a single giant colony along about 6,000 km of southern Europe's coast, from Italy to Portugal. Ants from distant nests treat each other as nestmates.

# Insect senses
Insects live in a sensory world very different from ours. A honeybee's eyes are tuned to ultraviolet, blue and green. She can't see red, but she sees ultraviolet "landing strips" on many flowers that guide her to the nectar.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Human colour", "items": ["red, green, blue", "no ultraviolet"]}, "r": {"t": "Bee colour", "items": ["ultraviolet, blue, green", "no red"]}}
### Smell at a distance
Male moths have feathery antennae covered in thousands of sensors. In some species they can find a female from several kilometres away, following a few molecules of her scent. The first such sex scent, or **pheromone**, was identified in silk moths in 1959.
### Fast eyes
An insect's **compound eye** is made of many small units, each pointing in a slightly different direction. A dragonfly has up to about 30,000. Flies' eyes refresh much faster than ours, so to a fly a swatting hand looks slow.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "flashes per second seen as separate", "items": [{"l": "Human", "v": 60.0, "t": "~60"}, {"l": "Housefly", "v": 250.0, "t": "~250", "hi": true}]}
### Senses in odd places
• Butterflies and flies taste with their feet
• Crickets hear through ears on their front legs
• Mosquitoes track the carbon dioxide in your breath from tens of metres
!? Because they see ultraviolet, bees see sunflowers with a dark bullseye in the middle, invisible to us.
> Insects don't see a dimmer version of our world; they sense a different one.

# Insect decline
In 2017 a team studying nature reserves in Germany reported that the weight of flying insects caught in traps had fallen by more than 75% in 27 years. The study made headlines and triggered many more.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "flying insect biomass, Germany (%)", "items": [{"l": "1989", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "2016", "v": 24.0, "t": "<25", "hi": true}]}
### A mixed but worrying picture
A 2020 review of many long-term studies found land insects declining by roughly 9% per decade on average, though with large differences between places. Some freshwater insects have increased, helped by cleaner rivers. Data are scarce in the tropics, where most insects live, so the global picture is still uncertain.
### The main causes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lost meadows, hedges and wetlands", "Pesticides and fertilisers", "Light pollution at night", "Hotter, drier climate"]}
These causes add up. A field sprayed more often, next to a road lit all night, in a hotter summer, offers far less food and shelter than the same place 50 years ago.
### What is being done
In 2018 the EU banned outdoor use of three neonicotinoid insecticides harmful to bees. The EU Nature Restoration Law of 2024 includes a goal to reverse pollinator decline by 2030.
!? Many older drivers recall cleaning dead insects off windscreens after a summer drive; today far fewer get squashed. Scientists call this the "windscreen phenomenon".
!! All insects are disappearing => Many species are declining, some are stable and a few are increasing; the overall trend is still a warning
> Insects are declining from many causes at once, so they need many small fixes at once.

# Mosquitoes
There are about 3,500 mosquito species, but only a few hundred bite humans. Only females bite: they need the protein in blood to make eggs. Both sexes otherwise drink plant nectar. A female finds you by your breath, your body heat and the smell of your skin.

### Why they're so deadly
A mosquito bite itself is harmless. The danger is what it carries. While feeding, a female can pick up a parasite or virus from one person and pass it to the next.

### Portugal's story
Malaria was once common in Portugal's rice fields and river valleys. The WHO declared the country malaria-free in 1973. In 2012 Madeira had a dengue outbreak with over 2,000 cases, spread by an invasive mosquito. The Asian tiger mosquito has been found in mainland Portugal since 2017.

# Help insects at home
Small spaces add up. Gardens, balconies and roadside verges together cover a large area, and insects need only patches of food and shelter linked together.
### Food: plant the right flowers
Native plants suit native insects. In Portugal, rosemary, thyme, French lavender, heather and borage are bee favourites. Aim for something in flower from early spring to late autumn.

### Shelter: leave some mess
About seven in ten wild bee species nest in the ground, in bare, sunny soil. Others use hollow stems or holes in dead wood. Leave stems standing over winter, keep a log pile and don't rake every leaf.

== na-mammals

# What makes a mammal
There are about 6,500 living mammal species, from 2-gram shrews to 150-tonne blue whales. The name comes from the Latin mamma, meaning breast; Carl Linnaeus chose it in 1758.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Hair or fur", "Milk from mammary glands", "Three middle ear bones", "A single lower jaw bone"]}
### Clues in the ear
The three tiny ear bones are the hammer, anvil and stirrup. Fossils show that two of them began as bones in the jaw joint of our reptile-like ancestors. Over millions of years they shrank and moved into the ear, giving mammals unusually sharp hearing.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~320 Ma", "l": "Mammal line splits off"}, {"y": "~200 Ma", "l": "First true mammals"}, {"y": "66 Ma", "l": "Dinosaurs wiped out"}, {"y": "After", "l": "Mammals diversify"}]}
### Other mammal features
• Warm-blooded, keeping a steady body temperature (birds do this too)
• A diaphragm, a muscle that helps fill the lungs
• Different teeth for cutting, tearing and grinding
• Long care of the young
For more than 100 million years, mammals were mostly small, night-active animals living alongside the dinosaurs. When an asteroid wiped out the large dinosaurs 66 million years ago, mammals spread into the empty roles.
!! Whales and dolphins have no hair, so they aren't mammals => They breathe air, make milk, and many have a few bristles, especially when young
> Milk and hair are the mammal trademarks, but the three ear bones are the deepest clue to our ancestry.

# Three groups
All mammals make milk, but they raise their young in three very different ways.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Mammals", "items": ["Placentals: ~6,000", "Marsupials: ~380", "Monotremes: 5"]}
### Placentals
Most mammals, including humans, are placentals. The placenta is an organ that links mother and baby, passing food and oxygen in and waste out, so young can grow for a long time inside. Pregnancy lasts from about 16 days in hamsters to about 22 months in elephants.
### Marsupials
Marsupials give birth very early. A newborn red kangaroo weighs under 1 gram, about the size of a jelly bean. It crawls through its mother's fur into the pouch, fixes onto a teat and grows there for months. Most marsupials live in Australia and nearby, but about 100 species of opossum live in the Americas.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Kangaroo", "items": ["~1 month pregnancy", "~1 g at birth", "months in pouch"]}, "r": {"t": "Human", "items": ["~9 months pregnancy", "~3.4 kg at birth", "no pouch"]}}
### Monotremes
Only five monotremes survive: the platypus and four species of echidna, all in Australia and New Guinea. They lay leathery eggs, yet still feed their young on milk.
!! Marsupials have no placenta => They do have a simple, short-lived one; it just isn't used for long
> Mammals share milk, but they split three ways on how to get the baby out: early, late or in an egg.

# The platypus
When a dried platypus skin reached Britain in 1799, the naturalist George Shaw wondered if someone had sewn a duck's bill onto a mammal. It was real, and it remains one of the strangest animals alive.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lays eggs, but makes milk", "Bill senses electric fields", "Males have venom spurs", "No stomach"]}
### Hunting with a sixth sense
A platypus hunts at the bottom of rivers with its eyes, ears and nostrils shut. Its soft, rubbery bill carries about 40,000 electric sensors. They pick up the tiny electric signals from the muscles of shrimps, worms and insect larvae hidden in the mud.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Dives, closes eyes and ears", "Sweeps bill side to side", "Senses prey's electric field", "Snaps prey from the mud"]}
### Milk without nipples
The female lays one to three small eggs in a burrow and keeps them warm for about 10 days. The young lick milk that seeps from patches of skin on her belly, because she has no teats.
### Venom
Males have a sharp spur on each hind ankle that delivers venom. It isn't deadly to people, but the pain can last for days or weeks. Males use it mainly against rival males in the breeding season.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Like a bird", "b": "lays eggs, has a bill"}, {"a": "Like a reptile", "b": "egg-laying, venom"}, {"a": "Like a mammal", "b": "fur, milk, ear bones"}]}
!? Under ultraviolet light, platypus fur glows a blue-green colour, something scientists only reported in 2020.
!! The platypus is half bird, half mammal => It's fully a mammal; it just kept old features that most mammals lost
> The platypus shows what early mammals were like: furry milk-makers that still laid eggs.

# Bats
There are about 1,400 bat species. Their wings are their hands: thin skin stretched between very long finger bones, which is why the group is called Chiroptera, "hand-wing". Gliding mammals such as flying squirrels can only fall slowly; bats truly fly, flapping and steering.
### Seeing with sound
Most insect-eating bats **echolocate**. They make very high-pitched calls, usually beyond human hearing, and listen to the echoes to find moths in the dark.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bat calls out", "Sound hits a moth", "Echo returns", "Bat closes in, calls faster"]}
### What bats do for us
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Insect-eaters", "b": "eat mosquitoes, crop pests"}, {"a": "Nectar bats", "b": "pollinate agave, durian"}, {"a": "Fruit bats", "b": "spread seeds of trees"}]}
A small pipistrelle can eat hundreds of insects in one night. In the United States, bats' pest control has been estimated to be worth billions of dollars a year to farmers.
### Bats in Portugal
Portugal has about 27 bat species, all legally protected. In the old university library at Coimbra and the palace library at Mafra, small bats live behind the shelves. At night they hunt the insects that would otherwise eat the books.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~1,400", "l": "bat species worldwide"}, {"v": "~1 in 5", "l": "mammal species is a bat"}, {"v": "40+ years", "l": "oldest wild bat recorded"}]}
!! Bats are blind => All bats can see, and fruit bats have large eyes; echolocation adds to sight
> Bats are flying hands, and they keep the night sky's insects in check.

# Whales evolved from land
Whales, dolphins and porpoises descend from four-legged, hoofed mammals. Their closest living relatives on land are hippos. Fossils found since the 1980s, mostly in Pakistan, India and Egypt, show the move into water step by step.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~50 Ma", "l": "Pakicetus: on land"}, {"y": "~49 Ma", "l": "Ambulocetus: wades"}, {"y": "~47 Ma", "l": "Rodhocetus: swims"}, {"y": "~40 Ma", "l": "Basilosaurus: tiny legs"}]}
### Clues in living whales
• Many whales still have small hip bones floating in their bodies
• Dolphin embryos briefly grow hind-limb buds, which then disappear
• Whales swim by moving the tail up and down, like a running mammal's spine, not side to side like a fish
• They breathe air and feed their young on milk

### Going big
Life in water supports a heavy body, and rich polar seas offer huge amounts of krill. The blue whale, up to about 30 m long, is the largest animal known to have lived.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "length, metres", "items": [{"l": "Pakicetus", "v": 1.5, "t": "~1-2"}, {"l": "Basilosaurus", "v": 18.0, "t": "~18"}, {"l": "Blue whale", "v": 30.0, "t": "~30", "hi": true}]}
Whales and dolphins are common off Portugal, especially around the Azores and Madeira, which were once major whaling sites and are now known for whale watching.

> Whales are mammals that went back to the sea, and their bodies still carry the legs they left behind.

# Elephants
There are three living elephant species: the African savanna elephant, the African forest elephant and the Asian elephant. They are the largest land animals, and among the most socially complex.
### Family life
Elephant herds are led by an old female, the **matriarch**. Her memory of water holes and dangers helps the family survive droughts. Pregnancy lasts about 22 months, the longest of any mammal, and calves stay close to their mothers for years.

### Talking through the ground
Elephants make deep rumbles, many below the range of human hearing. These can carry several kilometres. Elephants may also pick up vibrations through their feet and trunk.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 100.0, "unit": "sound pitch, Hz", "items": [{"l": "Rumble", "v": 14.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Human limit", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Male voice", "v": 100.0}]}
### Minds and mourning
In 2006 an Asian elephant at the Bronx Zoo passed the mirror test: she touched a mark on her head that she could only see in a mirror. # Hibernation
Staying warm in winter costs a lot of energy, just when food is scarce. Hibernation is a way out: the animal turns its body thermostat down and lives on stored fat.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Autumn: eat, store fat", "Winter: cool and slow", "Brief warm-ups", "Spring: wake, feed"]}
### How deep it goes
A hibernating hedgehog's heart can slow from around 190 to about 20 beats a minute, and its body can cool from 35 °C to a few degrees above the air. Breathing may pause for minutes at a time.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "heart beats per minute (hedgehog)", "items": [{"l": "Active", "v": 190.0, "t": "~190"}, {"l": "Hibernating", "v": 20.0, "t": "~20", "hi": true}]}
### The costly wake-ups
Hibernators don't sleep straight through. Every week or two they warm up for a few hours, perhaps to repair their bodies, then cool down again. These wake-ups use most of the winter's fat. That's why disturbing a hibernating animal can kill it.
### Iberian bats in caves
In Portugal, bats such as horseshoe bats and Schreiber's bats gather in caves and old mines that stay cold and steady through winter. A few sites hold thousands of bats, so they are protected and often closed to visitors in winter. Hedgehogs and garden dormice also hibernate in Portugal.

!? The Arctic ground squirrel can let its body fall below 0 °C during hibernation without freezing.
> Hibernation is a planned energy shutdown, and every unplanned wake-up costs fuel the animal can't replace.

# The Iberian lynx
The Iberian lynx lives only in Spain and Portugal. It weighs about 10 to 13 kg, has tufted ears and a short black-tipped tail, and eats mainly rabbits, which make up around 80 to 90% of its diet.
### Brink of extinction
The rabbit crashed twice: myxomatosis arrived in the 1950s and a deadly viral disease in the late 1980s. With habitat loss, hunting and traffic, lynx numbers collapsed. A 2002 count found fewer than 100, in just two small areas of Andalusia.

# Primates
There are about 500 primate species: lemurs, lorises, monkeys and apes, including us. Most live in tropical forests. Early primates lived in trees, and many of our features are tree-climbing tools.

== na-reptiles

# Cold-blooded?
"Cold-blooded" is a misleading nickname. A lizard basking on a sunny wall can have a body temperature of 35–40 °C, warmer than yours. The better word is **ectotherm**: an animal whose body heat comes mostly from outside, not from burning food.
Mammals and birds are endotherms. We keep a steady temperature by running our metabolism hard all day, which costs a lot of food. An ectotherm of the same size needs only about a tenth as much energy, so a snake can live for weeks on one meal.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Endotherm", "items": ["makes its own heat", "eats a lot", "active in the cold"]}, "r": {"t": "Ectotherm", "items": ["borrows outside heat", "eats little", "slow when cold"]}}
### Managing heat with behaviour
Ectotherms control their temperature by moving, not by shivering or sweating.
• Basking on warm rocks in the morning, often flattened to catch more sun
• Shuttling between sun and shade to avoid overheating at midday
• Turning darker to absorb more heat, paler to reflect it
• Hiding underground or in crevices on cold nights
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cold, stiff at dawn", "Bask in the sun", "Warm: hunt and feed", "Midday: seek shade"]}
In winter, Portuguese lizards, snakes and tortoises slow right down in a state called brumation, tucked into burrows until spring warms them again.
!! Cold-blooded animals have cold blood => Their blood is as warm as their surroundings allow, and a basking lizard can be warmer than you
> Ectotherms trade speed in the cold for a tiny food bill: they let the Sun do the heating.

# Reptiles vs amphibians
The big difference is how each group deals with water. Amphibians never fully left it behind; reptiles cut the cord around 310 million years ago with a new invention, the **amniotic egg**.
That egg has a shell and inner membranes that hold a private pond for the embryo, so it can be laid on dry land. Reptile skin, covered in keratin scales (the same protein as your nails), also stops the body drying out.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Reptiles", "items": ["dry, scaly skin", "shelled eggs on land", "lungs from birth"]}, "r": {"t": "Amphibians", "items": ["moist, glandular skin", "jelly eggs in water", "gills, then lungs"]}}
### Who's in each group
There are about 12,000 reptile species and about 8,800 amphibian species, and new ones are described every year.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Amphibians", "items": ["Frogs and toads", "Salamanders, newts", "Caecilians (legless)"]}
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Reptiles", "items": ["Lizards and snakes", "Turtles", "Crocodilians", "Tuatara (New Zealand)"]}
Amphibian skin is full of glands that keep it slimy, and many also make toxins. That moist skin works for breathing, but it means most amphibians must stay near water or damp ground.
!? By family tree, birds are reptiles too: they descend from dinosaurs, so a sparrow is more closely related to a crocodile than a crocodile is to a lizard.
> The shelled egg freed reptiles from water; amphibians still need it to breed and to keep their skin alive.

# A double life
The name comes from Greek: *amphi* (both) and *bios* (life). A frog spends its first stage as a fully aquatic animal and its second as an air-breathing land animal. The switch between the two is **metamorphosis**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Eggs in water", "Tadpole with gills", "Legs grow, lungs form", "Tail is absorbed", "Froglet on land"]}
### A body rebuilt
Metamorphosis is driven by thyroid hormone. It is not just growing legs: almost every organ is remodelled.
• Gills disappear and lungs take over
• The long gut of a plant-eating tadpole shortens for a meat-eating adult
• The tail is broken down and its material recycled, not dropped
• Eyes move and change to see well in air
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tadpole", "items": ["gills", "eats algae", "swims with tail"]}, "r": {"t": "Adult frog", "items": ["lungs and skin", "eats insects", "jumps on land"]}}
For a common frog in Portugal, the whole change takes roughly two to four months in spring, depending on temperature.
### Exceptions to the rule
Not every amphibian follows the script. The Mexican axolotl stays a gilled larva for life and breeds in that form. Some tropical frogs skip the tadpole stage, with tiny froglets hatching straight from eggs laid on land.
!? Common midwife toad fathers, found in Portugal, wrap strings of eggs around their back legs and carry them for weeks before releasing them into water.
> An amphibian lives two lives in one body, and rebuilds that body completely to switch from water to land.

# Breathing through skin
Breathing is just gas exchange: oxygen in, carbon dioxide out. Any thin, wet surface with blood behind it can do the job. Amphibian skin fits that description, so many species take in a large share of their oxygen through it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Oxygen dissolves in mucus", "Crosses thin skin", "Enters tiny blood vessels", "Carried round the body"]}
### All skin, no lungs
The largest family of salamanders, the lungless salamanders, has no lungs at all. They breathe entirely through their skin and the lining of their mouth. In cold, oxygen-rich water, a frog hibernating at the bottom of a pond can get by on skin breathing alone.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lungs: mainly in active adults", "Skin: all the time, wet or dry", "Mouth lining: pumped by the throat", "Gills: in larvae and some adults"]}
### The price of a permeable skin
A skin that lets oxygen in also lets other things in. Pesticides, heavy metals and other pollutants pass straight through, and amphibians can dry out fast in hot, dry air.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Benefit", "items": ["extra oxygen supply", "drinks through skin"]}, "r": {"t": "Cost", "items": ["absorbs pollutants", "dries out easily"]}}
This sensitivity makes frogs and newts good **indicator species**. Healthy, varied amphibian life in a pond is a sign of clean water.
!? The Titicaca water frog has huge folds of baggy skin to increase its surface area for breathing in the cold, thin-aired waters of the Andes.
> A skin that breathes is a skin that absorbs, which is why amphibians are early warnings of a polluted environment.

# Chameleons
For a long time people thought chameleons shuffled pigments around to match their background. Research published in 2015 found something stranger: a layer of skin cells packed with tiny **guanine nanocrystals** arranged in a grid.
When the chameleon is relaxed, the crystals sit close together and reflect blue light, which mixes with yellow pigment to give green. When it is excited, the grid stretches, the crystals spread apart and reflect longer wavelengths: yellow, orange or red.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Relaxed", "items": ["crystals packed tight", "reflect blue", "skin looks green"]}, "r": {"t": "Excited", "items": ["crystals spread out", "reflect red/yellow", "skin flares bright"]}}
### Why change colour?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Signalling", "b": "bright colours win fights, mates"}, {"a": "Temperature", "b": "dark to warm up, pale to cool"}, {"a": "Camouflage", "b": "mostly from resting colours"}]}
### The tongue catapult
The tongue is fired by elastic tissue that is wound up like a spring and released at once. In small species it accelerates faster than a fighter jet's pilot could survive, and it can reach more than one and a half times the body length. A sticky, suction-cup tip holds the prey.
Their eyes swivel independently, giving almost all-round vision, then lock together for depth perception just before the strike.
!? The Mediterranean chameleon lives wild in Portugal, mainly in coastal pine woods and dunes of the Algarve.
!! Chameleons change colour to match their background => They change mostly to signal mood and control temperature
> A chameleon's colour is light bouncing off tunable crystals, and it's mainly a message, not a disguise.

# Snakes
A snake's flickering tongue doesn't taste or sting; it collects scent. Each flick picks up chemical particles from the air and the ground. The tongue tips are then pressed to two openings in the roof of the mouth leading to the **vomeronasal organ** (also called Jacobson's organ), which analyses them.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tongue flicks out", "Picks up scent particles", "Tips touch mouth roof", "Vomeronasal organ reads it"]}
### Why a forked tongue?
The fork lets a snake sample two points at once. If the scent is stronger on the left tip, the trail goes left. It's smell in stereo, perfect for following prey that has passed by minutes earlier.
### Seeing heat
Pit vipers, such as rattlesnakes, and many pythons and boas have heat-sensing pits on their faces. A thin membrane inside each pit detects infrared radiation, letting them "see" a warm mouse in total darkness and strike accurately.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Forked tongue", "b": "tracks scent trails"}, {"a": "Heat pits", "b": "spot warm prey in the dark"}, {"a": "Jaw bones", "b": "feel ground vibrations"}, {"a": "Eyes", "b": "mostly detect movement"}]}
Snakes have no outer ears or eardrums, but they pick up vibrations through their jawbones and hear low sounds reasonably well.
In Portugal there are about ten native snake species. Only the two vipers, Lataste's viper and Seoane's viper, have medically significant venom, and bites are rare.
!! Snakes use their tongues to sting => The tongue is harmless; it's a scent collector
> A snake explores the world mainly through chemistry: its tongue is a nose that works in stereo.

# Turtles
Turtles are one of the oldest surviving reptile designs. Fossils show shelled turtles more than 210 million years ago, so they watched the dinosaurs rise and fall. The shell is part of the skeleton: ribs and backbone fused with bony plates, so a turtle can never leave it.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Hatch on a beach", "Decades at sea", "Return to home beach", "Lay eggs in the sand"]}
### Finding the way home
Female sea turtles often return to nest on the beach where they hatched, sometimes after 20–30 years and thousands of kilometres. The best explanation is that they remember the magnetic "signature" of their home coast, the unique strength and angle of Earth's magnetic field there.
### Temperature decides sex
Turtles have no sex chromosomes like ours. Instead, the temperature of the sand in the middle part of incubation decides. Cooler nests produce mostly males; warmer nests mostly females.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 26.0, "hi": 32.0, "unit": "nest sand temperature, °C", "items": [{"l": "Mostly males", "v": 27.0}, {"l": "About 50:50", "v": 29.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mostly females", "v": 31.0}]}
This makes turtles vulnerable to climate change. On some warm beaches in northern Australia, more than 99% of young green turtles studied in 2018 were female.
!? Loggerhead turtles that hatch in Florida drift across the Atlantic as juveniles, and many spend years feeding in the waters around the Azores and Madeira.
> For a turtle, sand temperature is destiny: a degree or two can tip a whole generation male or female.

# Crocodiles
Crocodiles look like giant lizards, but their family tree says otherwise. Crocodilians and birds are the two surviving branches of the **archosaurs**, the "ruling reptiles" that also included the dinosaurs and pterosaurs.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Archosaurs", "items": ["Crocodilians", "Pterosaurs (extinct)", "Dinosaurs, incl. birds"]}
### Family resemblances
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Four-chambered heart", "b": "like birds (and mammals)"}, {"a": "Gizzard with stones", "b": "grinds food, as in birds"}, {"a": "Nests and care", "b": "guard eggs and young"}, {"a": "Calls from the egg", "b": "chicks chirp before hatching"}]}
### A careful mother
A Nile crocodile mother guards her nest for about three months. When the young are ready, they squeak from inside their eggs. She digs them out and carries them, a few at a time, in her mouth to the water.
That gentleness is impressive given what the jaws can do. Saltwater crocodiles have one of the strongest bites ever measured, around 16,000 newtons, yet the mother controls it so precisely that hatchlings ride unharmed.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "bite force, newtons (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Human", "v": 800.0}, {"l": "Lion", "v": 4500.0}, {"l": "Saltwater croc", "v": 16000.0, "hi": true}]}
Like turtles, crocodilians have temperature-dependent sex. There are about 27 species of crocodiles, alligators, caimans and gharials alive today.
!? Crocodilians survived the asteroid impact 66 million years ago that wiped out the non-bird dinosaurs, their close cousins.
> A crocodile is a bird's cousin in armour: nests, chirping chicks and devoted mothers included.

# Amphibians in trouble
A major global assessment published in 2023 found that about 41% of amphibian species are threatened with extinction. That is a higher share than mammals, birds or reptiles.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of species threatened (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Amphibians", "v": 41.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mammals", "v": 27.0}, {"l": "Reptiles", "v": 21.0}, {"l": "Birds", "v": 13.0}]}
### The chytrid fungus
The amphibian chytrid fungus (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*) infects the skin, the very organ amphibians use to breathe and balance salts. Infected animals' hearts can stop. Spread around the world partly by trade in live amphibians, it has been linked to declines in more than 500 species, with around 90 thought to be extinct.
A related fungus, *Bsal*, appeared in the Netherlands and Belgium in the 2010s and kills fire salamanders. Europe is watching closely for its spread, including into Iberia.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Habitat loss and drained ponds", "Climate change and drought", "Disease: chytrid fungi", "Pollution and pesticides", "Roads and invasive species"]}
### Why they matter
Amphibians eat vast numbers of insects, including mosquitoes, and are food for birds, snakes and mammals. When they vanish, food webs shift.
!? Since the early 2000s, climate change has overtaken disease as the main driver pushing amphibian species into higher threat categories.
> Amphibians are the most threatened vertebrates on Earth, hit by habitat loss, heat and a skin-eating fungus at once.

# Portugal's species
Portugal is small but rich in reptiles and amphibians, thanks to its mix of wet Atlantic north and dry Mediterranean south. Several species live only in the Iberian Peninsula.

== na-weather

# What drives weather
Weather is the atmosphere trying, and failing, to even out the Sun's uneven heating. Near the equator, sunlight hits the ground almost straight on. Near the poles, the same beam arrives at a low angle and is spread over a much larger area, so each square metre gets less energy.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sun heats Earth unevenly", "Warm air rises, cool sinks", "Pressure differences form", "Winds and currents move heat"]}
### Land versus sea
Water needs about four times more energy than dry soil to warm by the same amount, and the Sun's heat mixes down into the sea. So land heats and cools quickly, while the ocean changes slowly.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Land", "items": ["heats fast by day", "cools fast at night"]}, "r": {"t": "Sea", "items": ["warms slowly", "stores heat for months"]}}
That's why inland Alentejo summers reach 40 °C while Lisbon stays milder, and why coastal places have gentler winters. On a hot day the land warms faster than the sea, air rises over the land and cooler sea air rushes in: a **sea breeze**.
### Where weather happens
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Stratosphere: ozone, calm", "Tropopause: the lid", "Troposphere: all weather", "Surface: land and ocean"]}
Almost all weather happens in the troposphere, the lowest layer, about 8 km thick at the poles and 16–18 km at the equator. Ocean currents, like the Gulf Stream, carry a large share of that heat too, which is one reason western Europe is milder than Canada at the same latitude.
> Every breeze and storm is part of one giant effort to move heat from the tropics towards the poles.

# High and low pressure
Air pressure is the weight of the air above you. At sea level it averages about 1013 hectopascals (hPa), roughly the weight of 10 tonnes on every square metre.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "High pressure", "items": ["air sinks and warms", "clouds evaporate", "calm, dry, sunny"]}, "r": {"t": "Low pressure", "items": ["air rises and cools", "clouds form", "wind and rain"]}}
### Why sinking air means sunshine
In a high, air slowly sinks from aloft. As it descends it is squeezed and warms, so any water droplets evaporate and clouds dissolve. In a low, air converges at the surface and is forced upward, where it cools and its water vapour condenses into clouds and rain.
### The Azores High
The Azores High is a semi-permanent area of high pressure over the Atlantic. In summer it grows and shifts north, sitting like a shield that steers Atlantic storms away from Portugal. That's why July and August are so dry. In winter it weakens and moves south, opening the door to rain-bearing lows.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 860.0, "hi": 1090.0, "unit": "sea-level pressure, hPa", "items": [{"l": "Typhoon Tip 1979", "v": 870.0}, {"l": "Average", "v": 1013.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Siberia record", "v": 1084.0}]}
A barometer that falls steadily over a few hours usually means a low is approaching. A rising barometer suggests clearing skies.
!? The lowest sea-level pressure ever recorded, 870 hPa, was in the eye of Typhoon Tip over the Pacific in 1979.
> Sinking air brings clear skies and rising air brings clouds, so pressure maps are really maps of up and down motion.

# How clouds form
Air always contains some invisible water vapour. How much it can hold depends on temperature: warm air can hold far more than cold air. Cool the air enough and it reaches its **dew point**, where the vapour starts turning back into liquid.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Warm, moist air rises", "Expands and cools", "Reaches the dew point", "Vapour condenses on dust", "Droplets form a cloud"]}
### Why rising air cools
Pressure falls with height. As a bubble of air rises, it expands, and expanding air cools. Dry air cools by nearly 10 °C for every kilometre it climbs. Once clouds form, the cooling slows because condensation releases heat.
### What lifts the air
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Air is lifted by", "items": ["Sun-warmed ground", "Mountains in its path", "Weather fronts", "Converging winds"]}
### Seeds for droplets
Water vapour struggles to condense on nothing. It needs tiny particles called **condensation nuclei**: sea salt, dust, pollen, smoke or pollution. Over the ocean, salt from breaking waves seeds many of the clouds that reach the Portuguese coast.
ex: Typical cumulus: about 1 km³ of air | holds about 0.5 g of water per m³ | 1,000,000,000 m³ × 0.5 g = about 500 tonnes of water
!? A fluffy fair-weather cloud can hold hundreds of tonnes of water; it floats because the droplets are tiny and spread through a vast volume of rising air.
> A cloud is simply rising air that has cooled past its dew point, with water condensing on specks of dust and salt.

# Cloud types
Clouds were first given their Latin names in 1802 by Luke Howard, an English chemist and amateur meteorologist. His system is still used today, combining a few root words.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Cumulus", "b": "heap: puffy, flat-based"}, {"a": "Stratus", "b": "layer: grey sheet"}, {"a": "Cirrus", "b": "curl of hair: wispy ice"}, {"a": "Nimbus", "b": "rain-bearing"}]}
### Sorted by height
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["High (above 6 km): cirrus", "Middle (2–6 km): alto- clouds", "Low (below 2 km): stratus", "Towering: cumulonimbus"]}
The ten main cloud types combine these roots: nimbostratus is a thick rain layer, altocumulus is a mid-level heap, cirrostratus is a thin high veil that can make a halo around the Sun.
### Reading the sky
• Small cumulus on a summer afternoon: fair weather
• Cirrus thickening into a veil: a warm front and rain may arrive within a day or so
• Cumulus growing tall by early afternoon: showers or thunder later
• Low stratus and drizzle: typical of a grey Atlantic morning in the north
The cumulonimbus is the giant. Its top can reach 12–16 km and spread out against the tropopause into a flat anvil shape.
!? Cirrus clouds are made entirely of ice crystals, even on the hottest summer day, because at their height the air is around −40 °C.
> Cloud names are just Latin descriptions of shape and height, and together they forecast the next few hours.

# Why it rains
A typical cloud droplet is about 0.02 mm across, so light that the gentlest updraught keeps it afloat. A raindrop is around 2 mm across. To make one raindrop you need roughly a million cloud droplets.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "0.02 mm", "l": "cloud droplet"}, {"v": "2 mm", "l": "raindrop"}, {"v": "1,000,000", "l": "droplets per raindrop"}]}
ex: Raindrop is 100× wider than a droplet | volume grows with width³ | 100 × 100 × 100 = 1,000,000 droplets
### Two ways to grow a raindrop
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Warm clouds", "items": ["droplets collide", "merge and grow", "common in tropics"]}, "r": {"t": "Cold clouds", "items": ["ice steals vapour", "snowflakes grow", "melt as they fall"]}}
In cold clouds, ice crystals and super-cooled droplets (liquid below 0 °C) sit side by side. Water vapour prefers to freeze onto ice, so the crystals grow quickly while the droplets shrink. Once heavy enough, they fall. In Europe, most rain actually starts as snow high up and melts on the way down.
### Not teardrops
Small raindrops are almost perfect spheres. Larger ones are flattened by air resistance into a shape like a burger bun, and drops bigger than about 5 mm usually break apart.
!! Raindrops are shaped like teardrops => They are spheres or flattened buns; the teardrop shape is a cartoon invention
> Rain falls only when a million tiny droplets have joined forces, through collisions or by feeding ice crystals.

# Thunder and lightning
Inside a thunderstorm, strong updraughts hurl ice crystals upward while soft hail pellets fall. When they collide, electric charge is transferred. Light crystals carry positive charge to the top of the cloud; heavier pellets leave the lower cloud negative.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ice and hail collide", "Charges separate", "Huge voltage builds", "Spark jumps: lightning", "Air explodes: thunder"]}
### Hotter than the Sun's surface
When the voltage is high enough, a channel a few centimetres wide opens and a huge current flows. The air inside heats to around 30,000 °C, about five times hotter than the Sun's surface, in a few millionths of a second. The air expands so violently that it sends out a shock wave, which we hear as thunder.
### How far away is it?
Light reaches you almost instantly, but sound travels about 340 metres per second.
ex: See flash, count 9 seconds to thunder | 9 ÷ 3 = 3 | storm is about 3 km away
= distance (km) ≈ seconds ÷ 3
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "30,000 °C", "l": "lightning channel"}, {"v": "~45", "l": "flashes per second, world"}, {"v": "3 s", "l": "per km of distance"}]}
If you can hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck. Shelter in a building or a car, not under a lone tree.
!! Lightning never strikes the same place twice => Tall structures are struck again and again; the Empire State Building is hit about 20 times a year
> Lightning is a giant spark that heats air hotter than the Sun's surface, and thunder is that air exploding outward.

# Wind direction
Air flows from high to low pressure, so you might expect wind to blow straight into a low. It doesn't, because Earth spins underneath the moving air. The result is the **Coriolis effect**: in the Northern Hemisphere, moving air is steered to the right; in the Southern, to the left.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Air pushed to the low", "Earth's spin bends it right", "Wind circles the low", "Anticlockwise spiral"]}
### Around highs and lows
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low (N. Hemisphere)", "items": ["anticlockwise", "air spirals in, rises"]}, "r": {"t": "High (N. Hemisphere)", "items": ["clockwise", "air spirals out, sinks"]}}
Above the friction of the ground, the push towards the low and the Coriolis turn balance out, so winds blow almost along the lines of equal pressure (isobars) on a weather map. Near the surface, friction slows the wind and lets it cut in slightly towards the low.
### A handy rule
In the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind and the low pressure is on your left. This is Buys Ballot's law, from a 19th-century Dutch meteorologist.
The Coriolis effect only matters over large distances and long times. It shapes trade winds, storms and ocean gyres, but it is far too weak to steer water in a basin.
!! Sinks drain in opposite directions in each hemisphere => A sink is too small; its swirl depends on its shape and how it was filled
> Earth's rotation turns the straight rush from high to low into great spinning systems: anticlockwise lows and clockwise highs in the north.

# Hurricanes
A hurricane is a heat engine. Warm sea water evaporates, the moist air rises, and as the vapour condenses into cloud it releases huge amounts of heat. That heat makes the air rise faster, drawing in more moist air at the surface.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sea above 26.5 °C", "Warm moist air rises", "Condensation frees heat", "Pressure drops, winds rise", "Coriolis sets it spinning"]}
### The recipe
• Ocean at least 26.5 °C down to about 50 m deep
• Moist air through a deep layer
• Little change in wind with height, so the storm isn't torn apart
• At least about 5° from the equator, where the Coriolis effect can start the spin
### Names and strength
They are hurricanes in the Atlantic and northeast Pacific, typhoons in the northwest Pacific and cyclones in the Indian Ocean. The Saffir–Simpson scale ranks them by sustained wind speed.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "minimum sustained wind, km/h", "items": [{"l": "Category 1", "v": 119.0}, {"l": "Category 3", "v": 178.0}, {"l": "Category 5", "v": 252.0, "hi": true}]}
### Storms reaching Portugal
Leslie made landfall near Figueira da Foz on 13 October 2018 as a post-tropical storm, with gusts near 180 km/h. It brought down thousands of trees and left hundreds of thousands of homes without power. A year earlier, Ophelia passed offshore and its winds fanned the deadly wildfires of 15 October 2017.
!? Warming seas mean the eastern Atlantic near Iberia now reaches temperatures that let tropical storms survive further east and north than in the past.
> Hurricanes are engines fuelled by warm ocean water, which is why they weaken fast over land or cool seas.

# The Nortada
On a summer afternoon on Portugal's west coast, a cool, strong wind often blows from the north. This is the **Nortada**, and it's caused by two pressure systems pulling in the same direction.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Azores High to the west", "Heat low over Iberia", "Steep pressure difference", "Strong north wind on coast"]}
### Why the afternoon?
The interior of Iberia bakes in summer, creating a shallow "thermal low" over the land. The difference between this low and the Azores High out at sea is greatest when the land is hottest, so the Nortada usually builds from late morning and peaks in the afternoon, often 30–50 km/h.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Morning", "items": ["light wind", "calm sea"]}, "r": {"t": "Afternoon", "items": ["strong N wind", "sand blowing"]}}
### Cold water, rich fish
The Nortada pushes surface water along the coast and, because of Earth's rotation, away from it. Cold, nutrient-rich water rises from below to replace it. This is called **upwelling**.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["N wind along coast", "Surface water pushed out", "Cold water wells up", "Plankton and sardines"]}
Upwelling explains why the sea at Viana do Castelo, Nazaré or Ericeira can feel 5 °C colder in August than on the Algarve's south coast. It also feeds the plankton that sustain Portugal's sardine fishery.
!? Guincho, Viana do Castelo and the Óbidos lagoon are popular kitesurfing spots largely because the Nortada is so reliable on summer afternoons.
> The Nortada is the Azores High and a sun-baked Iberia pulling together, and it chills the west-coast sea as well as its beaches.

# Forecasts
A modern forecast begins with a snapshot of the whole atmosphere. Satellites, weather balloons, aircraft, ships, buoys and thousands of ground stations feed in millions of observations every few hours. Supercomputers then solve the physics equations of moving air, step by step, into the future.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Millions of observations", "Build a 3D starting state", "Solve physics on a grid", "Run 50 slightly varied runs", "Forecast with odds"]}
### Why not perfect?
The atmosphere is chaotic: tiny errors in the starting state grow over time. The meteorologist Edward Lorenz showed this in the 1960s, giving us the "butterfly effect". Useful daily detail runs out after about 10–14 days.
To deal with this, centres run an **ensemble**: dozens of forecasts with slightly different starting points. If they agree, confidence is high; if they spread out, the forecast is uncertain. That's where "60% chance of rain" comes from.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1950", "l": "First computer forecast"}, {"y": "1975", "l": "ECMWF founded"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "Ensembles go live"}, {"y": "2025", "l": "AI models operational"}]}
### Who makes Portugal's forecasts
Portugal's weather service is IPMA. It uses its own models plus those of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), which Portugal helps fund. Since 2023, machine-learning models trained on decades of weather data have matched or beaten traditional ones on many measures, and ECMWF began running one operationally in 2025.
!? Forecast skill has improved by roughly a day per decade: a 5-day forecast today is about as good as a 1-day forecast was around 1980.
> Forecasts are physics simulations started from millions of observations, run many times to measure their own uncertainty.

== na-volcanoes

# What a volcano is
Deep below the surface, rock is hot but mostly solid. It melts into **magma** when pressure drops, when water is added, or when extra heat arrives from below. Magma is lighter than the rock around it, so it slowly rises, collecting in a magma chamber a few kilometres down.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rock melts deep down", "Light magma rises", "Fills a magma chamber", "Pressure forces it up", "Erupts at the vent"]}
### What comes out
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lava", "b": "magma that reaches the surface"}, {"a": "Ash and pumice", "b": "shattered, frothy rock"}, {"a": "Volcanic bombs", "b": "blobs flung out, cooled in air"}, {"a": "Gases", "b": "steam, CO2, sulphur dioxide"}]}
Lava erupts at about 700–1,200 °C. The gases are a big part of the story: water vapour dissolved in magma behaves like the fizz in a sealed bottle, waiting to escape.
### Anatomy of a volcano
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Eruption column and ash cloud", "Crater and vent", "Conduit (the pipe)", "Magma chamber"]}
There are about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes on land, and somewhere around 50 erupt each year. Many more lie hidden on the seafloor.
!! Lava is the deadliest part of an eruption => Most volcanic deaths come from pyroclastic flows (fast avalanches of hot gas and ash) and mudflows
> A volcano is a leak in the planet's lid, where buoyant, gas-filled magma finally reaches the air.

# Where volcanoes are
Volcanoes aren't scattered randomly. Mapped on a globe, they trace the edges of the tectonic plates, plus a few lonely hotspots.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Volcanoes form at", "items": ["Subduction zones", "Spreading ridges", "Hotspots"]}
### The Ring of Fire
Around the Pacific, ocean plates dive (subduct) beneath other plates. As the sinking slab heats up, it releases water, which lowers the melting point of the rock above it. The result is a horseshoe of about 40,000 km of volcanoes and earthquakes from New Zealand through Japan and Alaska to the Andes.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "40,000 km", "l": "length of Ring of Fire"}, {"v": "~75%", "l": "of active land volcanoes"}, {"v": "~90%", "l": "of world's earthquakes"}]}
### Spreading ridges
Where plates pull apart, as along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the mantle rises and melts. Most of Earth's lava erupts here, quietly, under the sea. Iceland is a rare spot where the ridge rises above the waves.
### Hotspots
Hawaii is thousands of kilometres from a plate edge. A plume of hot mantle rises beneath it, and as the Pacific plate slides over at about 7–10 cm a year, it builds a conveyor belt of islands: young and active in the southeast, old and eroded to the northwest.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plume stays still", "Plate slides over it", "New island over plume", "Older islands move off"]}
Portugal has both kinds of island volcanoes: the Azores sit near the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, while Madeira was built by a hotspot.
> Volcanoes mark where plates sink, spread or sit above a plume of hot mantle.

# Explosive or gentle
Whether a volcano oozes or explodes depends mostly on how sticky its magma is, and that depends on **silica**. More silica means longer chains of molecules and thicker, stiffer magma.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Basalt", "items": ["about 50% silica", "1,100–1,200 °C", "runny: gas escapes"]}, "r": {"t": "Rhyolite/andesite", "items": ["60–75% silica", "cooler, stiffer", "sticky: gas trapped"]}}
### The fizzy-drink effect
Magma holds dissolved gas under pressure, like an unopened bottle of fizzy drink. In runny basalt, bubbles rise and pop gently, giving lava fountains and rivers. In sticky magma, bubbles can't escape. As the magma rises and pressure falls, they expand violently and shatter the magma into ash.
### Measuring the bang
The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) runs from 0 to 8. Each step up means about ten times more material thrown out.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 8.0, "unit": "Volcanic Explosivity Index", "items": [{"l": "Kīlauea", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "St Helens", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Tambora", "v": 7.0}]}
Mount St Helens in the USA blew out its side on 18 May 1980, killing 57 people and removing about 400 m from its summit. Vesuvius in 79 CE was a similar size.
!? Iceland's Eyjafjallajökull in 2010 was only a modest eruption, but ice meltwater shattered its magma into fine ash that closed much of Europe's airspace for days.
> Runny magma lets its gas go; sticky magma traps it until it explodes.

# Pompeii
In 79 CE, Vesuvius had been quiet for centuries. Many Romans didn't even know it was a volcano. Then it erupted for about a day, and two towns on the Bay of Naples vanished.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Eruption column 30 km high", "Pumice rains for hours", "Roofs collapse", "Hot ash surges sweep in", "Towns buried"]}
### A letter from the scene
Pliny the Younger watched from across the bay and later described it in two letters to the historian Tacitus. His uncle, Pliny the Elder, commander of the Roman fleet, died trying to rescue people. Volcanologists still call this kind of tall, explosive column a **Plinian eruption**.
### Two towns, two fates
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pompeii", "items": ["buried by pumice, ash", "about 4–6 m deep", "bodies left hollows"]}, "r": {"t": "Herculaneum", "items": ["hit by hot surges", "about 20 m deep", "wood, scrolls charred"]}}
At Pompeii, ash hardened around victims' bodies. In 1863 the archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli poured plaster into the hollows they left, creating the famous casts. Herculaneum preserved wooden furniture, food and a library of carbonised papyrus scrolls. Since 2023, researchers have used X-ray scans and AI to begin reading text from scrolls that cannot be unrolled.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "79 CE", "l": "Vesuvius erupts"}, {"y": "1748", "l": "Pompeii digs begin"}, {"y": "1863", "l": "First plaster casts"}, {"y": "1944", "l": "Last eruption"}]}
!? Most people in Pompeii probably escaped. Remains of over a thousand victims have been found, out of a population thought to be at least 10,000.
!? An inscription found in 2018 suggests the eruption happened in October, not on 24 August as long believed.
> Pompeii is a city frozen in a single day, and Vesuvius, which still has hundreds of thousands of people living on its slopes, is still active.

# The Azores
The Azores sit on a **triple junction**, where the North American, Eurasian and Nubian (African) plates meet. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs just west of the central islands, and a slow, messy rift cuts through the archipelago. Add a possible mantle plume underneath, and you get nine volcanic islands and frequent earthquakes.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Azores triple junction", "items": ["North American plate", "Eurasian plate", "Nubian (African) plate"]}
### Capelinhos, 1957–58
On 27 September 1957, the sea began to boil off the western tip of Faial. For about 13 months, an underwater eruption grew into a new volcano, joined Faial and added roughly 2.4 km² of land. Ash buried farms and houses, and the lighthouse was half swallowed.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "13 months", "l": "length of eruption"}, {"v": "~2.4 km²", "l": "new land added"}, {"v": "1958", "l": "US law opens emigration"}]}
The disaster triggered a wave of emigration. A US law of 1958, co-sponsored by Senator John F. Kennedy, allowed thousands of Azoreans to settle in America. Today the buried lighthouse houses an underground interpretation centre.
### Living with volcanoes
• Furnas, on São Miguel, cooks its famous stew (cozido) in pots buried in hot volcanic ground
• Geothermal power plants on São Miguel supply roughly 40% of the island's electricity
• Pico, Portugal's highest mountain, last erupted in the 1700s
• CIVISA monitors the islands' earthquakes and volcanoes around the clock
!? Much of the land Capelinhos added has since been eroded by Atlantic waves, a reminder of how fast new volcanic land can disappear.
> The Azores are Portugal's living geology lab, where three plates meet and new land can still rise from the sea.

# How earthquakes happen
Tectonic plates move about 2–10 cm a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. But along a fault, the rocks on either side are jammed together by friction. The plates keep moving, so the rock bends and stores energy like a drawn bow.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Plates keep moving", "Fault stays locked", "Strain energy builds", "Sudden slip: quake"]}
When the stress overcomes friction, the fault slips in seconds, sometimes by several metres. The stored energy radiates out as **seismic waves**. This idea, called elastic rebound, came from studying the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.
### The waves
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "P waves", "b": "push-pull, fastest, ~6 km/s"}, {"a": "S waves", "b": "side-to-side, ~3.5 km/s"}, {"a": "Surface waves", "b": "slowest, most damaging"}]}
The point underground where the slip begins is the **focus** (or hypocentre); the point on the surface directly above it is the **epicentre**. The gap between P and S wave arrivals tells seismologists how far away a quake was.
### Portugal's quake history
Portugal lies near the boundary between the Eurasian and Nubian plates, which runs from the Azores to the Strait of Gibraltar. On 1 November 1755 an offshore earthquake, estimated at magnitude 8.5–9, followed by a tsunami and fires, destroyed much of Lisbon and killed tens of thousands of people. Smaller quakes are felt in the Algarve and Lisbon regions every few years.
!? The 1755 Lisbon earthquake helped launch modern seismology: the Marquis of Pombal sent a questionnaire to every parish asking what people had seen.
> An earthquake is the sudden release of strain that slowly built up while a stuck fault waited to slip.

# Measuring earthquakes
The first famous scale came from Charles Richter in 1935. Today seismologists use the **moment magnitude** scale, which measures the real size of the event: how big an area of fault slipped, how far, and how stiff the rock was.
### Why the numbers are deceptive
The scale is logarithmic. Each step up means the ground shakes about ten times harder on a seismograph and releases about 32 times more energy.
ex: Magnitude 5 to 6 | ×32 energy | Magnitude 5 to 7 | 32 × 32 ≈ ×1,000 energy
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy released (M5 = 1)", "items": [{"l": "Magnitude 5", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Magnitude 6", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "Magnitude 7", "v": 1000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Magnitude versus intensity
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Magnitude", "items": ["one number per quake", "energy at the source"]}, "r": {"t": "Intensity", "items": ["varies by place", "what people feel"]}}
A magnitude 6 under a city can do more harm than a magnitude 8 far out at sea. Intensity scales, like the European Macroseismic Scale used in Portugal, rate shaking from I (not felt) to XII (complete destruction).
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "9.5", "l": "Chile 1960, biggest yet"}, {"v": "~8.5–9", "l": "Lisbon 1755, estimated"}, {"v": "~15", "l": "magnitude 7+ per year"}]}
!! A magnitude 8 is twice as strong as a magnitude 4 => It releases about a million times more energy
> On the magnitude scale, every two steps up means roughly a thousand times more energy.

# Tsunamis
A tsunami is not a giant surfing wave. When an undersea earthquake suddenly lifts or drops the seafloor, it moves the entire column of water above it, from the bottom to the surface. That creates waves with wavelengths of hundreds of kilometres.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Seafloor jolts up", "Whole water column lifts", "Waves race outward", "Slow down near shore", "Pile up into a wall"]}
### Jet speed in deep water
A tsunami's speed depends only on the depth of the water.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = √(g × d)", "items": [{"s": "v", "m": "speed, m/s"}, {"s": "g", "m": "gravity, 9.8 m/s²"}, {"s": "d", "m": "water depth, m"}]}
ex: Ocean 4,000 m deep | √(9.8 × 4,000) ≈ 200 m/s | about 700 km/h, as fast as a jet
In deep water the wave may be less than a metre tall, so ships barely notice. As it reaches shallow water it slows, the back catches up with the front, and the water heaps up, sometimes 10–30 m high.
### Not just one wave
Tsunamis come as a series of waves, often 10 to 60 minutes apart, and the first is often not the biggest. They can also be caused by landslides, volcanic eruptions or meteorite impacts.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1755", "l": "Lisbon and Algarve hit"}, {"y": "2004", "l": "Indian Ocean, ~230,000 die"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Japan, Tōhoku"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "Tonga eruption tsunami"}]}
!? The 1755 tsunami reached Lisbon's riverside about 40 minutes after the earthquake, catching people who had fled to the open quays for safety.
> A tsunami moves the whole ocean, not just its surface, which is why it travels so fast and hits so hard.

# Warning signs
Official warning systems help, but for a tsunami generated close to shore, nature's own warnings may be all you get. Near Portugal, a big offshore quake could send waves to the Algarve in 15–30 minutes.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Strong shaking for over 20 s", "Sea suddenly pulls back", "Loud roar from the ocean", "Official alert or siren"]}
### Why the sea retreats
If the trough of the wave arrives first, the water is drawn out before the crest hits. Reefs, rocks and fish are left exposed, and curious people walk out to look. This is the worst possible reaction: the wave may arrive within minutes.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Go and look at the sea", "r": "Leave the beach at once"}, {"w": "Wait for an official alert", "r": "Shaking or drawback is the alert"}]}
### What to do
• Move inland or uphill immediately, on foot if roads are jammed
• Aim for ground at least 30 m above sea level, or 2–3 km inland
• If you can't get away, go to an upper floor of a solid concrete building
• Stay away until authorities say it's safe; later waves can be bigger
Since the 2010s, Portuguese coastal towns such as Cascais, Lisbon and several in the Algarve have put up blue tsunami signs marking risk zones and evacuation routes. IPMA monitors earthquakes and issues tsunami alerts.
!? In 2004, a 10-year-old British girl on a Thai beach recognised the retreating sea from a school geography lesson and warned her family; around a hundred people were evacuated safely.
> If the ground shakes hard or the sea draws back, don't wait: get to high ground.

# Volcanoes and climate
The ash from a big eruption falls out within weeks. The climate effect comes from **sulphur dioxide**. Blasted into the stratosphere, above the weather, it forms tiny droplets of sulphuric acid that reflect sunlight back to space for one to three years.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Huge eruption", "Sulphur into stratosphere", "Forms reflective haze", "Less sunlight reaches us", "Global cooling"]}
### The year without a summer
Tambora, in Indonesia, erupted in April 1815, the largest eruption in recorded history. It killed tens of thousands locally. The next year, northern hemisphere summers were cold and wet: frosts in June in New England, crop failures across Europe, food riots and famine.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1783", "l": "Laki: haze over Europe"}, {"y": "1815", "l": "Tambora erupts"}, {"y": "1816", "l": "Year without a summer"}, {"y": "1991", "l": "Pinatubo cools ~0.5 °C"}]}
That gloomy summer of 1816, Mary Shelley, stuck indoors by storms at Lake Geneva, began writing *Frankenstein*.
### Do volcanoes cause global warming?
Volcanoes release CO2, but far less than people do. All the world's volcanoes together emit well under 1 gigatonne a year; human activity emits around 37 gigatonnes of CO2 from fossil fuels alone.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "CO2 per year, gigatonnes (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "All volcanoes", "v": 0.4}, {"l": "Humans (fossil)", "v": 37.0, "hi": true}]}
!! Volcanoes emit more CO2 than humans => Humans emit roughly 100 times more each year
> Big eruptions cool the planet for a few years by veiling the Sun with sulphur haze; they don't drive long-term warming.

# Weathering and erosion
Rock seems permanent, but given time it always loses. **Weathering** breaks rock down where it sits; **erosion** carries the pieces away. Together they wear down every mountain and feed every beach.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Weathering", "items": ["Physical: ice, heat", "Chemical: acid, rust", "Biological: roots, lichens"]}
### How rock breaks
• Freeze–thaw: water seeps into cracks and expands by about 9% when it freezes, wedging rock apart
• Chemical: slightly acidic rain dissolves limestone and turns minerals in granite into clay
• Biological: tree roots prise open cracks; lichens release acids
Granite landscapes in Portugal, like Monsanto or the Serra da Estrela, show this well: weathering along cracks leaves huge rounded boulders stacked on top of each other.
### How pieces move
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Rock uplifted", "Weathered into bits", "Carried by water, ice", "Dropped as sediment", "Pressed into new rock"]}
Rivers do most of the transport, but glaciers, wind, waves and gravity (landslides) all help.
ex: Mountain wears down 0.1 mm a year | over 10 million years | 0.1 mm × 10,000,000 = 1,000 m, a whole mountain gone
!? The Colorado River has carved the Grand Canyon, about 1.8 km deep, in roughly the last 5–6 million years.
> Tiny losses every year, multiplied by millions of years, are enough to erase mountains.

# Mountains rise
Most great mountain ranges are made by collisions. When two continents meet, neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples, folds and thickens, pushing rock up into peaks and down into deep roots.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plates converge", "Ocean floor subducts", "Continents collide", "Crust folds and stacks", "Mountains rise"]}
### The Himalayas
India broke away from the southern supercontinent and raced north. Around 50 million years ago it hit Asia. It is still pushing north by about 4–5 cm a year, and the Himalayas are still rising by a few millimetres a year, though erosion and earthquakes take some back.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "8,849 m", "l": "Everest (2020 survey)"}, {"v": "~5 cm/yr", "l": "India moving north"}, {"v": "50 My", "l": "since the collision"}]}
### Kinds of mountains
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fold mountains", "b": "Himalayas, Alps, Pyrenees"}, {"a": "Volcanic", "b": "Pico, Fuji, Etna"}, {"a": "Fault-block", "b": "crust lifted along faults"}]}
Mountains also float, in a way. Thick crust sits deeper in the denser mantle beneath, like an iceberg. As the tops erode, the roots slowly bob up, so mountains keep rising for a while even after collision stops.
The Pyrenees formed when the Iberian plate pushed into Europe. The same squeeze helped lift Portugal's Serra da Estrela, part of a chain of ranges running across central Iberia.
!? The rock at the summit of Everest is marine limestone, full of fossils of sea creatures that lived on an ocean floor.
> Mountains are the crumple zone of colliding plates, and the collision that built the Himalayas is still going on.

# River journey
Geographers divide a river into three stretches. Each has its own job: cutting down, cutting sideways, or dropping its load.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Upper: steep, V-valleys", "Middle: bends, wider valley", "Lower: floodplain, estuary"]}
### Upper course
Near the source, the river is small but steep. It carries big boulders that grind its bed, cutting a narrow V-shaped valley. Waterfalls and rapids form where hard rock resists. Many northern Portuguese rivers, like the Paiva, have these wild, rocky stretches.
### Middle and lower course
Further down, more tributaries join, so the river carries more water. It starts to swing side to side, widening its valley. Near the sea, the land is flat; the river spreads over a floodplain, and when it slows at the mouth it drops its sediment in a delta or an estuary like the Tejo's.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Near the source", "items": ["steep and narrow", "big boulders", "cuts downward"]}, "r": {"t": "Near the mouth", "items": ["wide and deep", "fine silt and mud", "drops sediment"]}}
### Load and discharge
Rivers move rock in three ways: rolling boulders along the bed, carrying sand and silt suspended in the water, and dissolving minerals invisibly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rain and springs", "Tributaries join", "Discharge grows", "Sediment dropped at sea"]}
!! Rivers flow fastest in their steep upper course => Average speed often increases downstream: there's more water and less friction, even if it looks calmer
> A river is a conveyor belt that cuts in the hills and builds in the lowlands.

# Meanders
Even a straight river doesn't stay straight. Any small bend makes water swing to the outside, and that starts a feedback loop.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Outside of bend", "items": ["fast, deep water", "erosion", "river cliff"]}, "r": {"t": "Inside of bend", "items": ["slow, shallow", "deposition", "sandy beach (point bar)"]}}
### How a bend grows
As water rounds a curve, it piles up against the outer bank and spirals down and across to the inner bank, carrying sediment with it. The outer bank is eaten away; the inner bank builds up. The bend becomes a loop, and the whole meander slowly migrates downstream.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Small bend", "Outer bank erodes", "Inner bank builds", "Loop's neck narrows", "Flood cuts through"]}
### The oxbow lake
Eventually two bends almost touch. In a flood, the river takes the short cut across the narrow neck. Sediment seals off the old loop, leaving a curved lake: an **oxbow lake**. Over time, it fills with plants and silt and becomes a marsh.
ex: River winds 15 km to cover 10 km | sinuosity = 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5 | above about 1.5, a river counts as meandering
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Bend grows", "Neck cut off", "Oxbow lake left", "Lake silts up"]}
!? The word "meander" comes from the Büyük Menderes River in Turkey, known to the ancient Greeks as the Maeander for its winding course.
> Erosion on the outside and deposition on the inside make river bends grow until they're cut off as oxbow lakes.

# Glaciers
A glacier is a river of ice. Snow piles up faster than it melts, gets compressed into dense ice, and then flows downhill under its own weight, typically tens of metres to a few hundred metres a year.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Snow builds up", "Pressed into ice", "Ice flows downhill", "Rock frozen in", "Valley ground into U"]}
### How ice carves
• Plucking: ice freezes onto rock and tears blocks away as it moves
• Abrasion: rocks embedded in the ice scrape the valley like sandpaper
• Transport: ice carries everything from sand to house-sized boulders
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "River valley", "items": ["V-shaped", "narrow floor", "winding"]}, "r": {"t": "Glacial valley", "items": ["U-shaped", "wide flat floor", "straightened"]}}
### What glaciers leave behind
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Moraine", "b": "ridge of dumped rubble"}, {"a": "Cirque", "b": "armchair hollow, often a lake"}, {"a": "Erratic", "b": "boulder far from home"}, {"a": "Hanging valley", "b": "side valley left high up"}]}
### Ice in Portugal
About 20,000 years ago, at the peak of the last ice age, an ice cap covered the high plateau of the Serra da Estrela, and glaciers flowed down its valleys. The Zêzere valley above Manteigas is a textbook U-shaped glacial valley, and moraines and glacier-polished rocks can be seen along its trails. Lakes such as Lagoa Comprida sit in hollows carved by ice.
!? At the height of the last ice age, so much water was locked in ice that sea level was about 120 m lower, and Portugal's coastline lay many kilometres further west.
> Glaciers turn V-shaped river valleys into broad U-shapes, and the Serra da Estrela still wears their marks.

# Caves
Rain picks up carbon dioxide from the air and, even more, from soil. That makes a weak acid, carbonic acid. Limestone is made of calcium carbonate, which this acid slowly dissolves.
= water + carbon dioxide → carbonic acid, which dissolves limestone
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rain soaks through soil", "Picks up CO2: weak acid", "Dissolves limestone cracks", "Cracks widen into caves"]}
### Decorating the cave
When water seeps into an air-filled cave, it releases some of its CO2, just like a fizzy drink going flat. It can no longer hold all its dissolved calcium carbonate, so it leaves a thin film of crystal behind. Drip by drip, formations grow.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Stalactite", "b": "hangs from the ceiling"}, {"a": "Stalagmite", "b": "grows up from the ground"}, {"a": "Column", "b": "the two join up"}, {"a": "Flowstone", "b": "sheets down walls"}]}
ex: Typical growth 0.1 mm a year | 1 m = 1,000 mm | 1,000 ÷ 0.1 = 10,000 years
### Caves in Portugal
The limestone Serras de Aire e Candeeiros, in central Portugal, are riddled with caves. The Grutas de Mira de Aire, discovered in 1947, are the country's largest known cave system, with over 11 km of passages. Landscapes like this, with sinkholes, underground rivers and few surface streams, are called **karst**.
!? A memory trick: stalac**t**ites hold **t**ight to the ceiling; stalag**m**ites **m**ight reach them one day.
> Caves are carved by a weak acid in rainwater and decorated by the same water giving back its stone.

# Coasts
Waves are the sculptors of the coast. They hurl water and pebbles at cliffs and squeeze air into cracks, which then explodes outward as the wave retreats. Softer rock and lines of weakness give way first.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Crack in headland", "Waves widen to a cave", "Cave breaks through: arch", "Arch roof falls: stack", "Stack wears to stump"]}
### The Algarve's showcase
The western Algarve's golden cliffs are made of relatively soft limestone and sandstone, laid down in a shallow sea millions of years ago. Around Lagos, Ponta da Piedade is a maze of arches, stacks and grottoes. Near Lagoa, the Benagil sea cave has a hole in its roof where part of the ceiling collapsed.
### Erosion and deposition
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Eroding coasts", "items": ["cliffs and caves", "arches, stacks", "Algarve west"]}, "r": {"t": "Building coasts", "items": ["beaches, dunes", "spits, barrier isles", "Ria Formosa"]}}
Where waves lose energy, they drop sand. The Ria Formosa lagoon near Faro is protected by a chain of sandy barrier islands, and the Tróia peninsula is a long sand spit built across the mouth of the Sado.
### Cliffs are unstable
Cliffs retreat in sudden collapses, not gradually. In 2009, part of a cliff at Praia Maria Luísa near Albufeira fell onto the beach and killed five people. That's why Portuguese beaches have signs telling you to keep away from the base and edge of cliffs.
!? Sea arches are temporary: most collapse within a few centuries or less, and the stacks standing off the Algarve today were once arches.
> Waves turn headlands into caves, arches, stacks and stumps, and dump the eroded sand on calmer shores.

# Deserts
A desert is defined by dryness, not heat or sand. The usual rule of thumb is less than about 250 mm of precipitation a year, or more water evaporating than falling. By that measure, about a third of Earth's land is arid or semi-arid.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 2600.0, "unit": "rain per year, mm (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Atacama", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Desert limit", "v": 250.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 700.0}, {"l": "Gerês", "v": 2500.0}]}
### Hot and cold
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hot deserts", "items": ["Sahara, Arabian", "blocked by high pressure"]}, "r": {"t": "Cold deserts", "items": ["Antarctica, Gobi", "air too cold for rain"]}}
Most hot deserts sit around 20–30° from the equator, under belts of sinking, dry air. Others are in the "rain shadow" of mountains or next to cold ocean currents, like the Atacama in Chile, where some weather stations have recorded almost no rain for years.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "area, million km²", "items": [{"l": "Antarctica", "v": 14.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Arctic", "v": 13.9}, {"l": "Sahara", "v": 9.2}, {"l": "Arabian", "v": 2.3}]}
### Mostly not sand
Only about a fifth of the world's deserts are sand seas (ergs). The rest is bare rock, gravel plains, salt flats and mountains. Desert temperatures can swing by 30 °C between day and night, because dry air and clear skies let heat escape fast.
!? Parts of southern Portugal's interior are classified as at risk of desertification, as drought, heat and soil erosion combine.
> A desert is any place starved of precipitation, which is why ice-covered Antarctica is the biggest of all.

# Portugal's rivers
Portugal's great rivers mostly start in Spain and flow west to the Atlantic. That means water management is shared: the 1998 Albufeira Convention sets how much water Spain must let through on shared rivers.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "total length, km (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Tejo (Tagus)", "v": 1007.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Douro", "v": 897.0}, {"l": "Guadiana", "v": 744.0}, {"l": "Mondego", "v": 250.0, "t": "~250"}]}
### The Tejo
The Tagus rises in the mountains east of Madrid and runs about 1,000 km to Lisbon. Only about the last quarter is in Portugal. Its estuary is one of the largest in Europe and makes Lisbon one of the world's great natural harbours. The Mondego is the longest river entirely within Portugal.
### The Douro
The Douro cuts a deep, steep valley through hard schist and granite. Over centuries, people built terraces on the slopes to grow vines. In 1756, the Marquis of Pombal set boundaries for where port wine grapes could be grown, making the Douro the world's oldest legally demarcated wine region.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1756", "l": "Douro demarcated"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "Albufeira Convention"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Alto Douro: UNESCO"}, {"y": "2002", "l": "Alqueva dam closes"}]}
### Dams and reservoirs
Most big Portuguese rivers are now chains of dams, providing hydropower and irrigation. The Alqueva reservoir on the Guadiana, about 250 km², is one of the largest artificial lakes in Western Europe.
!? Before dams were built, the Douro was so wild that port wine was taken downstream to Porto on flat-bottomed rabelo boats through dangerous rapids.
> Portugal's rivers are borrowed from Spain, shaped its wine and its capital, and are now tamed by dams.

# Highest points
Portugal's highest mountain isn't on the mainland at all. **Mount Pico**, on the Azores island of the same name, is a volcano rising to 2,351 m. It also rises from a seafloor more than 1,000 m below sea level, so its full height is far greater than what you see.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "height above sea level, m", "items": [{"l": "Torre (Estrela)", "v": 1993.0}, {"l": "Pico (Azores)", "v": 2351.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Teide (Spain)", "v": 3715.0}, {"l": "Mont Blanc", "v": 4806.0}]}
### Climbing Pico
Pico is a young volcano that last erupted in the early 1700s. Hikers register before climbing and usually take about 3–4 hours to reach the summit, where a small cone called Piquinho still releases warm volcanic gas. On clear days you can see São Jorge and Faial.
### Torre and the Serra da Estrela
On the mainland, the top of the Serra da Estrela, called Torre, reaches 1,993 m. The name "Torre" (tower) comes from a stone monument built on the summit in the early 19th century, said to have been raised so the top would reach a round 2,000 m.

== na-fungi

# Neither plant nor animal
Early naturalists, from Aristotle to Linnaeus in the 18th century, filed fungi under plants. Only in 1969 did the American ecologist Robert Whittaker give them a kingdom of their own, and DNA studies later confirmed that fungi are closer to animals.
### Eating from the outside
A fungus grows into its food, then pours out digestive enzymes. These break big molecules such as cellulose and proteins into small ones that the fungus absorbs through its cell walls. In effect it lives inside its own stomach.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Grow into food", "Release enzymes", "Food broken down outside", "Absorb the nutrients"]}
### Three ways of life
• Decomposers feed on dead wood, leaves and dung
• Parasites attack living hosts: chestnut blight, athlete's foot, the "zombie-ant" fungus
• Partners trade with plants or algae, as in mycorrhiza and lichens
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "fungal species (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Named so far", "v": 150000.0, "t": "~150,000"}, {"l": "Estimated total", "v": 2500000.0, "t": "2–4 million", "hi": true}]}
Fungi cause most plant diseases and some serious human infections, but most species are harmless to us, and many are essential to ecosystems.
!? Some fungi are hunters: the oyster mushroom can paralyse and digest tiny roundworms in the wood it grows on.
> Fungi are nature's great absorbers: they digest the world from the outside in.

# Mycelium, the hidden body
Each hypha is a tube a few thousandths of a millimetre wide, far thinner than a human hair. Because it grows only at the tip, it can push its way into wood and between soil grains. A single gram of forest soil can hold tens to hundreds of metres of hyphae.
### A network that adapts
When one part of a mycelium finds food, it thickens the threads leading there and lets unproductive ones die back. In lab experiments, wood-rotting fungi given scattered food sources built networks that balanced efficiency and resilience, rather like well-designed transport systems.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hypha", "items": ["one thread", "grows at its tip"]}, "r": {"t": "Mycelium", "items": ["web of hyphae", "the whole fungus"]}}
### Long-lived and hard to see
A mycelium can live for years or centuries, producing mushrooms only when conditions suit. Fairy rings, circles of mushrooms in grass, form as a mycelium grows outward from a centre, using up nutrients behind it. Some rings in Europe are thought to be hundreds of years old.
### Mycelium as a material
Grown on farm waste, mycelium binds loose material into light, solid shapes, and some fungi can be grown as food.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Compostable packaging", "Insulation panels", "Leather-like fabrics", "Meat alternatives"]}
!! A mushroom is the whole fungus => It is a short-lived fruiting body; the mycelium is the real, long-lived organism
> The real fungus is the hidden web, not the mushroom you see.

# Mushrooms are fruit
Mushrooms often spring up after autumn rain because they grow mostly by swelling, not by making new cells. The mycelium prepares a tiny knot of tissue underground, then pumps water into it. That is why mushrooms can even push up through tarmac.
### Launching spores
Many mushrooms launch their spores with a drop of water that forms beside each spore. When the drop suddenly merges with a film on the spore, the shift in weight flicks it away with an acceleration thousands of times that of gravity. It travels only a fraction of a millimetre, just enough to fall clear of the gill and drift away.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Water drop forms", "Drop merges with spore", "Spore is flicked off", "Falls and drifts away"]}
### Many shapes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Gills", "b": "field mushroom, parasol"}, {"a": "Pores", "b": "ceps and other boletes"}, {"a": "Puffballs", "b": "spores puff out when hit"}, {"a": "Truffles", "b": "underground, found by smell"}]}
Truffles grow underground and cannot use the wind. Instead they give off strong smells that attract animals, which dig them up, eat them and spread the spores in their droppings. That is why pigs and trained dogs can find them.
### Spore prints
Placing a cap gills-down on paper overnight leaves a spore print. Its colour, from white or pink to brown or black, is one of many features experts use in identification, never on its own.
!? Some mushrooms make their own wind: water evaporating from the cap cools the air beneath it, creating gentle currents that lift spores even on still days.
> A mushroom is a temporary launch pad, built fast to send spores out into the world.

# Breaking down wood
Wood is mostly cellulose fibres, glued and armoured with lignin. Many organisms can digest cellulose, but lignin is a jumble of chemical rings that few enzymes can attack. White-rot fungi produce powerful oxidising enzymes that tear it apart, which is why rotten logs eventually crumble.
### A coal mystery
Much of the world's coal formed about 360 to 300 million years ago, in the Carboniferous period. One hypothesis says this happened partly because fungi able to digest lignin had not yet evolved, so dead trees piled up. Other scientists argue that vast swamps, climate and moving continents explain it better. The debate continues.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "No fungi ate wood, so coal formed", "r": "Fungi may be part of it; swamps mattered"}]}
### Decay at work
• Shelf fungi on dead trunks are the fruiting bodies of wood-rotters
• Rotting wood left in forests shelters beetles, birds and bats
• Compost heaps rely on fungi to break down woody stems and paper
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fungi soften the wood", "Beetles tunnel in", "Wood crumbles to soil", "Seedlings take root"]}
### When decay hits our homes
The same skills cause trouble indoors. Dry rot, Serpula lacrymans, can spread through damp timber in old buildings and destroy floors and roofs. Keeping wood dry is the best defence, because fungi need moisture to grow.
!? Some white-rot fungi can also break down pollutants such as certain dyes and pesticides, and are being tested to clean up contaminated soil.
> Fungi are the recyclers that can fully unlock wood, returning a tree's carbon and nutrients to the cycle.

# Root partners
Fossils from Scotland's Rhynie chert, about 400 million years old, show fungi inside the cells of some of the earliest land plants. Many scientists think this partnership helped plants leave the water: the first plants had no true roots, and fungi did much of the job.
### How the trade works
Fungal threads are far thinner than roots and reach into the tiniest pores of soil. They release acids and enzymes that free phosphorus locked in minerals. In exchange, a plant may pass a sizeable share of its sugars, by some estimates up to about a fifth, to its fungal partners.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Leaves make sugar", "Sugar flows to roots", "Fungus takes its share", "Brings back nutrients"]}
### Orchids, truffles and pines
• Orchid seeds are as fine as dust and cannot sprout without a fungus feeding them
• Truffles are the fruiting bodies of fungi partnered with oak and hazel roots
• Pines planted in new continents often grew poorly until the right fungi arrived
### What about the "wood wide web"?
Fungal networks can link several trees at once. But claims that trees use them to feed their young or send warnings have been questioned: a 2023 review found the evidence weaker than popular books suggest. The partnership is solid; a forest-wide "internet" remains a hypothesis.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Trees chat through fungi", "r": "Links exist; sharing is debated"}]}
> Most plants on Earth grow with fungal partners underground, a trade at least 400 million years old.

# Lichens: a partnership
In 1867 the Swiss botanist Simon Schwendener proposed that lichens were partnerships, an idea many experts at first rejected. In 2016 researchers found that many lichens also contain a third partner, a yeast, in their outer layer. A lichen is better thought of as a tiny ecosystem.
### Pioneers
About 20,000 lichen species are known. They survive drying out completely and spring back to life with rain. On bare rock they slowly release acids that help break it down, starting the first soil. Some grow a millimetre or less a year, and some crusts are thousands of years old.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Lichen on bare rock", "Acids weaken the rock", "Dust and dead lichen", "First thin soil"]}
### Pollution detectors
Many lichens absorb water and nutrients straight from the air, so they also take in pollutants. Shaggy, bushy lichens disappear first where sulphur dioxide is high; tough, crusty ones hang on. In many European cities lichens have returned as coal smoke declined.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "clean air (illus.)", "items": [{"l": "Crusty", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Leafy", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Shrubby, hairy", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}]}
### Useful lichens
• "Reindeer moss" is a lichen and a key winter food for reindeer
• Litmus, the classic acid–alkali indicator, comes from lichens
• Lichens were long used to dye wool, including traditional Scottish tweed
!? The grey-green beard-like tufts hanging from trees in clean, damp hills, such as those of northern Portugal, are lichens of the genus Usnea.
> A lichen is a partnership so close that it behaves like one organism and survives where neither partner could alone.

# Yeast, the tiny workhorse
A yeast cell is about five thousandths of a millimetre across, and a small block of fresh baker's yeast contains billions of them. They multiply by budding: a small bulge grows on the cell, receives a copy of its DNA and pinches off.
### Lager's surprise ancestor
Lager yeast is a hybrid of brewer's yeast and a cold-loving wild species. That second parent was a mystery until 2011, when scientists found it on southern beech trees in Patagonia. How it reached Europe's breweries is still debated.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Brewer's yeast", "Meets cold-loving wild yeast", "Hybrid forms", "Lager yeast"]}
### A model for our own cells
Yeast cells have a nucleus and many of the same genes as human cells, but they grow fast and are easy to study. In 1996 yeast became the first organism with a nucleus to have its whole genome read. Yeast research helped win Nobel Prizes for discoveries about cell division in 2001 and about how cells recycle their parts in 2016.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1850s–60s", "l": "Pasteur: living yeast"}, {"y": "1996", "l": "Yeast genome read"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Nobel: cell division"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Nobel: cell recycling"}]}
### Yeast factories
• Engineered yeast makes a large share of the world's insulin
• A hepatitis B vaccine has been produced in yeast since the 1980s
• Yeast also makes flavours, enzymes and some biofuels
!? Yeast is so like us that some human genes can replace their yeast versions and keep the cells alive.
> Yeast is both one of humanity's oldest helpers and one of its most modern tools.

# Medicines from moulds
In the soil, fungi compete with bacteria for food, and many produce substances that kill or slow their rivals. Alexander Fleming noticed this in 1928, when a mould contaminating a dish of bacteria cleared a ring around itself. Turning it into a medicine took Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their Oxford team until the early 1940s.
### Finding better moulds
Early penicillin was made in tiny amounts. In 1943, in Peoria in the USA, a mould on a cantaloupe melon from a local market turned out to produce far more. Its descendants, improved further in the lab, made penicillin cheap enough for millions, and fungi are still grown in huge steel tanks to make it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mould on a dish, 1928", "Purified at Oxford", "Better strain on a melon", "Mass production"]}
### Beyond penicillin
Cephalosporins, another major family of antibiotics, came from a mould found near a sewage outlet in Sardinia in 1948. Ciclosporin, from a fungus in a Norwegian soil sample, calms the immune system and made organ transplants far more successful from the 1980s. The first statins were discovered in fungi by the Japanese scientist Akira Endo in the 1970s.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1928", "l": "Penicillin noticed"}, {"y": "1948", "l": "Cephalosporin mould"}, {"y": "1970s", "l": "First statins"}, {"y": "1983", "l": "Ciclosporin approved"}]}
### A double edge
Some fungal chemicals are poisons. The ergot fungus on rye caused outbreaks of poisoning in medieval Europe, yet its compounds later gave medicines for migraine and for bleeding after childbirth.
> Some of medicine's most important drugs are weapons that fungi evolved against their rivals.

# The humongous fungus
Foresters noticed patches of dying conifers in the Malheur National Forest. Around 2000, scientists sampled the fungus at many points and compared their DNA. Samples across the whole area were genetically identical, meaning they belonged to one individual that had grown outwards for millennia.
### How it spreads
Honey fungus sends out black, root-like cords called rhizomorphs, nicknamed "bootlaces", which grow through the soil from tree to tree. It invades roots, kills many trees and then feeds on the dead wood. Under the bark of infected trees lie white sheets of mycelium that smell of mushrooms.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bootlace cords spread", "Infect tree roots", "Tree weakens and dies", "Fungus feeds on wood"]}
### Measuring a giant
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "area, hectares (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Pando aspen, Utah", "v": 43.0}, {"l": "Michigan fungus", "v": 75.0}, {"l": "Oregon fungus", "v": 965.0, "hi": true}]}
Its age is estimated from its growth rate, at somewhere between about 2,000 and 8,000 years. Its weight is unknown, and which organism counts as "largest" depends on whether you measure area or mass.
### Closer to home
Honey fungi are common across Europe, including Portugal, where they attack fruit trees, vines, chestnuts and garden shrubs. Gardeners recognise them by the bootlaces and the clusters of mushrooms at the base of dying trees.
!? Honey fungus mycelium glows faintly in the dark, the "foxfire" sometimes seen on rotting wood at night.
> One fungus can quietly occupy an entire forest, visible only as a few mushrooms each autumn.

# Edible or deadly?
The death cap looks harmless: a greenish cap, white gills and a ring on the stem, with a cup-like sac at its base that is often hidden in the soil. Its toxins, called amatoxins, are not destroyed by cooking, freezing or drying, and a single mushroom can be enough to kill an adult.
### Why it is so dangerous
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "0 h", "l": "Meal tastes good"}, {"y": "6–24 h", "l": "Vomiting, diarrhoea"}, {"y": "Day 2–3", "l": "Seems to improve"}, {"y": "Day 3–5", "l": "Liver failure"}]}
Symptoms start late, often after 6 to 12 hours or more, when the toxins are already in the body. A false recovery can follow before the liver fails. Fast hospital treatment saves many lives, so act at once if poisoning is possible.
### Myths that kill
• A silver spoon does not turn black near poisonous mushrooms
• Peeling the cap does not remove the toxins
• A pleasant smell or taste proves nothing
• Mushrooms growing on wood or nibbled by slugs can still be toxic

### In Portugal
Autumn brings mushroom fairs and guided walks, especially in Trás-os-Montes and the Beiras, where ceps, chanterelles and Caesar's mushrooms are prized. If you suspect poisoning, call the Antipoison Information Centre (CIAV) on 800 250 250 or 112, and keep any leftover mushrooms for identification.

== na-microbes

# Everywhere
A **microbe** is any living thing too small to see without a microscope. Most are single cells. They were the first life on Earth, and they still make up most of its diversity.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Microbes", "items": ["Bacteria", "Archaea", "Fungi and protists", "Viruses (borderline)"]}
### How small?
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.01, "hi": 100.0, "log": true, "unit": "size, µm (log)", "items": [{"l": "Virus", "v": 0.1}, {"l": "Bacterium", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Red blood cell", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Hair width", "v": 80.0}]}
A micrometre is a thousandth of a millimetre. About a thousand bacteria lined up would cross the width of a pinhead.
### Everywhere means everywhere
• Soil: a gram can hold up to around a billion bacteria from thousands of species
• Air: microbes ride clouds and may help ice crystals form, nudging rain
• Deep rock: microbes live kilometres underground
• You: your body carries roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, about 38 trillion, mostly in your gut

!! Microbes are mostly germs => Only around 1,400 known microbe species cause human disease, out of millions
> Microbes are the invisible majority, living in every corner of the planet, including inside you.

# First life
Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Fossil mats of microbes called stromatolites suggest life was present by about 3.5 billion years ago, and possibly earlier. For roughly the next 3 billion years, all life was microscopic.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "3.5 bya", "l": "Microbial mats"}, {"y": "2.4 bya", "l": "Oxygen builds up"}, {"y": "~2 bya", "l": "Complex cells"}, {"y": "0.54 bya", "l": "Animal explosion"}]}
### The oxygen revolution
**Cyanobacteria** were the first organisms to perform the kind of photosynthesis that splits water and releases oxygen. At first, the oxygen reacted with iron dissolved in the sea, rusting out as red layers we can still see in banded iron rocks. Around 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen began to build up in the air: the Great Oxidation Event.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cyanobacteria photosynthesise", "Oxygen rusts the oceans", "Oxygen builds in air", "Ozone layer forms", "Big, active life possible"]}
For many early microbes, oxygen was a poison, and many were pushed into oxygen-free corners. But oxygen allowed much more energy to be extracted from food, which eventually made large, active animals possible.
### Captured partners
Your cells contain mitochondria, the power stations, which descend from bacteria swallowed by an ancestral cell around 2 billion years ago. Plant chloroplasts are captured cyanobacteria.
!? Living stromatolites still grow today in a few places, such as Shark Bay in Western Australia, giving a glimpse of early Earth.
> Microbes ran the planet alone for about 3 billion years, and their oxygen made animals possible.

# Bacteria
A bacterium is a single cell with no nucleus: its DNA floats freely in a loop. Most are 1–5 micrometres long. They have a tough cell wall, and many swim with whip-like tails called flagella.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bacterial cell", "items": ["no nucleus", "1–5 µm long", "divides in two"]}, "r": {"t": "Your cell", "items": ["DNA in nucleus", "10–30 µm", "many internal parts"]}}
### Shapes
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Bacteria shapes", "items": ["Cocci: spheres", "Bacilli: rods", "Spirilla: spirals"]}
### Explosive growth
Bacteria reproduce by splitting in two. In ideal conditions, *E. coli* can divide every 20 minutes.
ex: Doubling every 20 min | 10 hours = 30 doublings | 1 cell becomes over 1 billion
In reality, food runs out and waste builds up long before that, which is why fridges, salt and drying preserve food: they slow or stop division.
### Mostly friends
• Gut bacteria break down fibre, make some vitamins (like vitamin K) and train your immune system
• Bacteria make yogurt, cheese and vinegar
• They recycle nutrients in soil and water
• A small minority cause diseases such as tuberculosis, cholera and pneumonia
!? The Dutch cloth merchant Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was the first to see bacteria, in 1676, using a tiny home-made lens. He called them "animalcules".
> Bacteria are simple cells that multiply astonishingly fast, and the vast majority keep us and the planet running.

# Archaea
Under a microscope, archaea look just like bacteria: small, single cells with no nucleus. But in 1977 the American microbiologist Carl Woese compared their genetic machinery and found they were as different from bacteria as bacteria are from us. Life was split into three great branches.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Three domains of life", "items": ["Bacteria", "Archaea", "Eukaryotes (us, plants)"]}
### Lovers of extremes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thermophiles", "b": "hot springs, deep vents"}, {"a": "Halophiles", "b": "salt lakes, salt pans"}, {"a": "Acidophiles", "b": "acid as strong as vinegar"}, {"a": "Methanogens", "b": "make methane, no oxygen"}]}
Their cell membranes are built from unusual fats that stay stable in heat and acid, which helps explain why so many thrive where other cells fall apart.
### Not just extremes
Archaea also live in ordinary soils, oceans and your gut. **Methanogens** in cows' stomachs, rice paddies and wetlands produce much of the world's natural methane, a powerful greenhouse gas.

!? The pinkish-red colour of some salt pans, like those of Aveiro or Castro Marim, comes partly from salt-loving archaea and algae full of red pigments.
> Archaea look like bacteria but are a separate branch of life, and they may be our own distant ancestors.

# Viruses
A virus is a set of genes, DNA or RNA, wrapped in a protein coat. It has no metabolism of its own: no way to make energy or proteins. To reproduce, it must hijack a living cell. Most are 20–300 nanometres across, far smaller than bacteria.

### Alive or not?
Biologists still argue. Viruses evolve and carry genes, but they can't do anything outside a host. Many prefer to call them "at the edge of life".
### Numbers beyond imagination

> Viruses are packets of genes that can only copy themselves inside cells, and they are the most numerous biological entities on Earth.

# Fermentation
**Fermentation** is how some microbes get energy from sugar without oxygen. It produces leftovers, such as alcohol, carbon dioxide or acids, and humans have learnt to love those leftovers.

### How bread rises

### Ancient technology
Chemical traces in pottery show fermented drinks in China around 7000 BCE and wine in Georgia around 6000 BCE. People used fermentation for thousands of years without knowing why it worked. In the 1850s and 1860s, Louis Pasteur showed that living microbes were responsible.

# Nitrogen fixers
Nitrogen is in every protein and every strand of DNA. The air is about 78% nitrogen, but it's locked in N2 molecules held together by a very strong triple bond. Plants and animals can't break it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Root releases signals", "Bacteria enter root", "Root forms a nodule", "Bacteria fix nitrogen", "Plant feeds bacteria sugar"]}
### A partnership underground
Rhizobium bacteria live inside small lumps, called nodules, on the roots of legumes such as beans, peas, chickpeas, lupins and clover. Using an enzyme called nitrogenase, they turn nitrogen from the air into ammonia the plant can use. In return, the plant gives them sugar. Healthy nodules are pink inside, coloured by a protein that controls oxygen, because oxygen destroys nitrogenase.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["N2 in air", "Fixed by microbes", "Used by plants, animals", "Returned by decay", "Released back as N2"]}
### Farming with microbes
Farmers have long rotated legumes with cereals to enrich soil, even without knowing why. In the early 1900s, the **Haber–Bosch** process learnt to fix nitrogen industrially, using high heat and pressure to make fertiliser.

!? Synthetic nitrogen fertiliser grows the food for roughly half the world's population, but excess runs off fields and pollutes rivers and seas.
> Bacteria in legume roots do at room temperature what industry needs huge heat and pressure for: pulling nitrogen from the air.

# Antibiotic resistance
Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them growing. Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928 and mass-produced from the 1940s, transformed medicine. Fleming himself warned that misuse could make bacteria resistant.

### How bacteria resist

Bacteria can also swap resistance genes directly, even between species, on small loops of DNA called plasmids. That means resistance can spread much faster than by inheritance alone.
### The scale of the problem
A global study published in 2024 estimated that drug-resistant infections directly caused over a million deaths in 2021, and contributed to several million more. Without action, this is expected to rise by 2050.

> Every unnecessary antibiotic gives resistant bacteria an advantage, so careful use protects everyone's medicines.

# Decomposers
When a leaf falls or an animal dies, a crew of bacteria and fungi gets to work. They release enzymes that break tough materials, like cellulose in plant cell walls, into simple molecules they can absorb. What's left goes back to the soil as nutrients.

Wood is especially hard to digest because of **lignin**, a tough glue that stiffens cell walls. Only some fungi, called white-rot fungi, can break it down efficiently.
### Inside a compost heap
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Kitchen and garden waste", "Microbes multiply", "Heap warms to 60 °C", "Heat-loving microbes", "Rich, dark compost"]}
In a well-built compost heap, so much microbial activity happens that the middle can reach 55–65 °C, hot enough to kill many weed seeds and pathogens.
!? Some scientists think so much coal formed about 300 million years ago partly because fungi that digest lignin had not yet evolved, so dead trees piled up; others dispute this.
> Decomposer microbes close the loop of life, turning death and waste back into the nutrients that feed new growth.

# Microbes and climate
Microbes are major players in the carbon cycle. Some take carbon dioxide out of the air; others put it back. Small changes in their balance can matter for the whole planet.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Carbon sinks", "items": ["ocean plankton", "photosynthesis", "sinking dead cells"]}, "r": {"t": "Carbon sources", "items": ["soil respiration", "methane from wetlands", "thawing permafrost"]}}
### The ocean's tiny plants
Microscopic phytoplankton, including cyanobacteria and algae, carry out about half of all photosynthesis on Earth. When they die, some sink to the deep sea, carrying carbon with them. This is called the **biological pump**.
### The soil's carbon bank
Soils store more carbon than the atmosphere and all plants combined. Microbes decide how long it stays there.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "carbon stored, billion tonnes (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Soils", "v": 1700.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Atmosphere", "v": 870.0}, {"l": "Plants", "v": 550.0}]}
### A risky feedback
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["World warms", "Soil microbes speed up", "More CO2 and methane", "Even more warming"]}
Frozen Arctic soils, or permafrost, hold huge amounts of old plant material. As they thaw, microbes start eating it, releasing CO2 and methane.
!? A single ocean cyanobacterium, *Prochlorococcus*, is so abundant that it is thought to be the most numerous photosynthetic organism on Earth.
> Microbes can lock carbon away or release it, and warming risks tipping them towards releasing more.

# Extremophiles
The word **extremophile** means "lover of extremes". The term was coined in the 1970s, as scientists kept finding microbes in places once thought sterile. Most extremophiles are single-celled bacteria and archaea, an ancient branch of life that looks like bacteria but works differently inside.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thermophiles", "b": "heat: hot springs, vents"}, {"a": "Acidophiles", "b": "acid: pH near 0"}, {"a": "Halophiles", "b": "salt: brines, salt pans"}, {"a": "Piezophiles", "b": "pressure: ocean trenches"}]}
### Extreme for whom?
"Extreme" is our point of view. A microbe from a boiling spring can die at 40 °C, the temperature where we feel comfortable. Each extremophile has tuned its proteins, membranes and DNA to its own world.
### Iberian extremes
Spain's Río Tinto, in Huelva, runs red and as acidic as lemon juice, around pH 2, because of iron and sulphur from ancient mines. It is still full of microbes, and NASA has used it to practise searching for life on Mars. Closer to home, the pink colour of some salt pans in Aveiro and the Algarve comes from salt-loving microbes.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 14.0, "unit": "pH", "items": [{"l": "Río Tinto", "v": 2.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Pure water", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Soda lakes", "v": 11.0}]}
### Champions
• Deinococcus radiodurans survives about 1,000 times the radiation dose that would kill a person
• Microbes live in the Mariana Trench, about 11 km down, at over 1,000 times sea-level pressure
• Bacteria have been found living kilometres down in solid rock
!! Extremophiles are rare oddities => In the deep crust and oceans they may make up a large share of all microbial life
> What is deadly for us is simply home for an extremophile.

# Hottest life
In 1977 the submarine Alvin found hot springs on the seafloor near the Galápagos, surrounded by life. Since then, hundreds of these **hydrothermal vents** have been found, including several on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge near the Azores.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Seawater sinks into crust", "Magma heats it to 350 °C+", "Hot fluid vents, full of minerals", "Microbes feed on chemicals"]}
### Why the water doesn't boil
At 2,500 m depth, the water above presses down with about 250 times the pressure at the surface. Under that weight water stays liquid far above 100 °C. Vent fluid can reach about 400 °C, but microbes live in the cooler edges where hot meets cold.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "highest growth temperature, °C", "items": [{"l": "Human cells", "v": 42.0, "t": "~42"}, {"l": "Pyrolobus", "v": 113.0}, {"l": "Methanopyrus", "v": 122.0, "hi": true}]}
### Built for heat
Heat makes molecules shake apart. Heat-loving archaea fight back:
• Proteins packed tightly, with extra chemical "staples" holding them together
• Cell membranes made of tougher fats that don't melt
• Special enzymes that coil DNA more tightly to stop its strands separating
The current record is about 122 °C, set in 2008 by Methanopyrus kandleri grown under high pressure in a lab. Many scientists think the upper limit for life is not much higher, perhaps around 150 °C, because key molecules such as ATP fall apart.
!? Vent microbes don't need sunlight: they get energy from chemicals such as hydrogen sulphide, and feed whole ecosystems of tubeworms, shrimps and crabs.
> Pressure keeps vent water liquid, and tough proteins keep life working at temperatures that would cook us.

# Desert survival
Most mammals are in danger after losing about 15% of their body water; for humans, losses above 10% are serious. A camel can lose around 25% and keep going, then refill fast.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "body weight lost as water, %", "items": [{"l": "Human (serious)", "v": 10.0, "t": "~10"}, {"l": "Camel", "v": 25.0, "t": "~25", "hi": true}]}
ex: 500 kg camel | Loses 25% as water ≈ 125 litres | Drinks about 100 litres in roughly 10 minutes
### How camels save water
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Let body heat rise by day", "Very dry dung, thick urine", "Nose recaptures moisture", "Fur shades the skin"]}
A camel's body temperature can swing by about 6 °C, from around 34 °C at dawn to around 40 °C in the afternoon. It stores heat by day instead of sweating it away, and sheds it in the cool night.
### Safe drinking
Gulping so much water would burst the blood cells of most animals. Camels' red blood cells are oval and flexible, and can swell to more than twice their size without bursting.
### Why the hump?
The hump stores fat, up to tens of kilograms of it. Keeping fat in one place, rather than all over the body, lets heat escape more easily from the rest of the skin.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Hump is a water tank", "r": "Hump is a fat store"}]}
!? The kangaroo rat of North American deserts may never drink at all: it gets water from the chemistry of digesting dry seeds.
!! Camels store water in their humps => The hump is fat; their real trick is losing very little water and tolerating dehydration
> Camels survive by spending water slowly, tolerating dehydration and refilling fast.

# Antarctic fish
The Southern Ocean around Antarctica is about −1.9 °C. Salt stops seawater from freezing at 0 °C, but the blood of an ordinary fish would freeze at about −0.8 °C. Ice crystals in the water would grow inside it.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": -2.5, "hi": 0.5, "unit": "temperature, °C", "items": [{"l": "Southern Ocean", "v": -1.9, "hi": true}, {"l": "Fish blood freezes", "v": -0.8}, {"l": "Fresh water", "v": 0.0}]}
### Antifreeze in the blood
In the 1960s the American biologist Arthur DeVries found that Antarctic notothenioid fish carry **antifreeze proteins**. They bind to tiny ice crystals and stop them growing. The fish don't stop ice from forming; they stop it from spreading.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tiny ice crystal enters", "Antifreeze sticks to it", "Crystal can't grow", "Fish stays unfrozen"]}
### Clear-blooded icefish
Icefish, a group of about 16 species, went further. They have no red blood cells and no haemoglobin; their blood is almost colourless. They survive because very cold water holds more dissolved oxygen.
• A large heart pumping a large volume of blood
• Wide blood vessels and many fine capillaries
• Skin without scales that can take up some oxygen
• A slow, energy-saving lifestyle
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Most fish", "items": ["red blood cells", "haemoglobin", "red blood"]}, "r": {"t": "Icefish", "items": ["no red cells", "no haemoglobin", "clear blood"]}}
!? In 2021 a research ship found an icefish breeding colony under the Weddell Sea covering about 240 km², with an estimated 60 million nests.
> Antarctic fish stay liquid with antifreeze, and icefish even manage without red blood.

# Wood frogs
Wood frogs live across North America, as far north as Alaska and beyond the Arctic Circle. Instead of digging deep below the frost line, they shelter just under leaf litter and let themselves freeze.
### Freezing, carefully
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ice touches skin", "Liver releases sugar", "Sugar fills the cells", "Ice forms only outside cells"]}
Ice inside a cell tears it apart. So the frog controls where ice forms. Within minutes of the first ice touching its skin, its liver floods the body with glucose. The sugar-rich cells resist freezing and don't shrink too much as water is drawn out. Up to about two-thirds of the frog's body water turns to ice.
### Frozen for months
In Alaska, wood frogs have survived temperatures down to about −18 °C and spent up to seven months frozen. Their hearts stop, breathing stops and there is no brain activity. When spring comes they thaw, and within hours the heart restarts.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~2/3", "l": "body water turned to ice"}, {"v": "−18 °C", "l": "lowest survived in Alaska"}, {"v": "~7 months", "l": "frozen in a winter"}]}
### Lessons for medicine
Donated human organs can be kept outside the body only for hours, because cooling damages them. Researchers study frogs like this one to learn how to store organs longer using natural protective substances.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Wood frog", "items": ["ice outside cells", "sugar protects cells", "thaws and lives"]}, "r": {"t": "Most animals", "items": ["ice inside cells", "cells burst", "death"]}}
!! Freezing always kills cells => It's ice inside cells that kills; controlling where ice forms makes survival possible
> A wood frog survives winter by choosing where its ice goes.

# Cave life
Animals that live their whole lives in caves are called **troglobites**. With no light at all, eyes are useless and colour has no purpose. Over many generations, cave species often lose both, and gain longer antennae, legs or sensory organs instead.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lost", "items": ["eyes", "skin colour"]}, "r": {"t": "Gained", "items": ["touch and smell", "slow metabolism"]}}
### Why lose eyes?
Eyes and the brain areas that run them cost a lot of energy. Caves are poor in food, so animals that waste less energy do better. A study on young fish estimated vision can use around 15% of their resting energy. Mutations that switch off eyes are no longer weeded out, and may even help.
### The olm
The olm, or proteus, is a pale, blind salamander about 25 cm long that lives in flooded caves of the Dinaric karst in Slovenia, Croatia and nearby. It keeps feathery gills all its life.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~100 yrs", "l": "possible lifespan"}, {"v": "~15 yrs", "l": "age at first breeding"}, {"v": "Years", "l": "it can go without food"}]}
Its metabolism is extremely slow. One olm in a cave in Bosnia was found in the same spot for seven years.
### Evolution repeating itself
The Mexican tetra lives in rivers with eyes, and in caves without them. Different cave populations lost their eyes independently, showing the same pressures can produce the same result again and again.
!? Portugal's limestone caves, such as those of the Serra de Aire e Candeeiros, hold tiny blind invertebrates that live nowhere else.
> In permanent darkness, evolution trades eyes and colour for energy savings and sharper touch.

# High altitude
At 7,000 m the air holds less than half the oxygen per breath that it does at sea level. Most people need bottled oxygen above about 8,000 m. Bar-headed geese cross the Himalayas each year flying under their own power.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "air pressure, % of sea level", "items": [{"l": "Sea level", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Serra da Estrela", "v": 79.0, "t": "~79"}, {"l": "7,000 m", "v": 41.0, "t": "~41", "hi": true}, {"l": "Everest", "v": 33.0, "t": "~33"}]}
### What tracking showed
Satellite tags fitted from 2009 onwards showed the geese usually follow passes at around 4,000 to 6,000 m, rising higher only when they must. The highest flight recorded was about 7,300 m. They often fly at night and early morning, when the air is colder and denser, giving more lift and oxygen.
### Body upgrades
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Haemoglobin grips oxygen better", "Big lungs, deep breaths", "Dense capillaries in muscles", "More mitochondria"]}
A single change in the geese's haemoglobin helps it grab oxygen from thin air. Their deep, slow breathing moves more air than ours, and their flight muscles are packed with blood vessels.
### People at altitude
Tibetans and Andean highlanders adapted in different ways. Many Tibetans carry a gene variant that stops their blood becoming too thick at altitude; it seems to come from the Denisovans, an extinct human group.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tibetans", "items": ["normal red cell count", "faster breathing"]}, "r": {"t": "Andeans", "items": ["more red cells", "thicker blood"]}}
!? In 1973 a Rüppell's vulture collided with a plane over Ivory Coast at about 11,300 m, the highest bird flight ever recorded.
> Bar-headed geese beat thin air with better haemoglobin, bigger breaths and smart timing.

# Deep-sea giants
Some deep-sea animals are much bigger than their shallow-water relatives. This is called **deep-sea gigantism**. Biologists see a similar pattern in cold polar waters, so temperature seems to matter.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Cold slows growth and ageing", "Bigger cells in the cold", "Big bodies save energy", "Long life, big size"]}
### Why bigger?
In cold water, metabolism runs slowly, so animals grow slowly but keep growing for longer. Large bodies also use less energy per kilogram and can travel far between scarce meals. Cold water holds more oxygen, helping big bodies breathe.
### The giants
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "maximum length, metres (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Giant squid", "v": 12.0, "t": "~12", "hi": true}, {"l": "Japanese spider crab", "v": 3.7, "t": "~3.7 legspan"}, {"l": "Giant isopod", "v": 0.5, "t": "~0.5"}]}
• **Giant squid**: females reach about 12 m including the long tentacles, with eyes the size of dinner plates. It was first filmed alive in the deep in 2012, off Japan.
• **Colossal squid**: shorter but heavier, up to about 500 kg. A young one was first filmed in the wild in 2025.
• **Giant isopod**: a woodlouse relative up to about 50 cm long. One in a Japanese aquarium refused food for over five years.
### Iberian connections
Giant squid are sometimes found stranded or caught off Portugal and northern Spain, where deep canyons come close to shore. Luarca, in Asturias, has a museum devoted to them. In Madeira, fishermen catch black scabbardfish at around 1,000 m deep.
!! Deep-sea giants are monsters that attack ships => They are slow, rarely seen animals that avoid the surface
> Cold, slow lives let some deep-sea animals keep growing into giants.

# Survivors of space
Space is a near-perfect vacuum, bathed in harsh ultraviolet and cosmic radiation, with temperatures swinging hundreds of degrees. Yet a few organisms have survived being left outside spacecraft.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1984-90", "l": "Bacterial spores on NASA satellite"}, {"y": "2005", "l": "Lichens on Foton-M2"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "Tardigrades on Foton-M3"}, {"y": "2015-18", "l": "Bacteria outside the ISS"}]}
### Tardigrades
Tardigrades, or water bears, are animals about half a millimetre long, with eight stubby legs. You can find them in moss on a Portuguese wall. When dry, they curl into a **tun**, losing most of their water and almost stopping their metabolism.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Moss dries out", "Curls into a tun", "Waits years if needed", "Rehydrates and walks"]}
In 2007, on the European Space Agency's TARDIS experiment, dried tardigrades spent 10 days exposed in orbit. Many survived vacuum and cosmic rays; most, but not all, died when also exposed to full solar ultraviolet.
### Protecting the DNA
Tardigrades make a sugar called trehalose that turns into a glass-like solid, holding cell parts in place. In 2016 scientists found a protein, called Dsup, that partly shields their DNA from radiation.
### Lichens and bacteria
Lichens, partnerships of fungus and alga, survived 15 days in open space in 2005 and later longer stays outside the ISS. Clumps of the bacterium Deinococcus survived three years outside the ISS, in a Japanese experiment.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~0.5 mm", "l": "tardigrade length"}, {"v": "10 days", "l": "exposed in orbit, 2007"}, {"v": "3 years", "l": "Deinococcus outside ISS"}]}
!! Tardigrades are indestructible => They survive by shutting down; in an active state they are fragile, and many die in space tests
> Some life can wait out space by drying up and switching off.

# Why it matters
In the 1960s the American microbiologist Thomas Brock found a bacterium, Thermus aquaticus, in the hot springs of Yellowstone. Its enzyme for copying DNA, called **Taq polymerase**, keeps working after being heated near boiling. That made it perfect for PCR, the DNA-copying method invented by Kary Mullis in 1983.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Heat to 95 °C: strands part", "Cool: primers attach", "72 °C: Taq copies DNA", "Repeat about 30 times"]}
ex: Each cycle doubles the DNA | After 30 cycles: 2^30 | About 1 billion copies from one
PCR is used for COVID-19 tests, forensic DNA, diagnosing infections and checking food. Without a heat-proof enzyme, fresh enzyme would have to be added after every heating step.
### Other useful tricks
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Cold-loving enzymes", "b": "detergents for cool washes"}, {"a": "Heat-loving enzymes", "b": "biofuels, food processing"}, {"a": "Salt-loving microbes", "b": "pigments, bioplastics"}]}
### Life beyond Earth
If microbes live in boiling acid, ice and rock on Earth, life elsewhere seems more plausible. Mars may have salty water underground. Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus hide oceans beneath their ice. Europe's JUICE probe, launched in 2023, and NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in 2024, will study these icy moons in the early 2030s.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Mars: salty underground water", "Europa: ocean under ice", "Enceladus: water jets to space"]}
!? Spain's acidic Río Tinto has been used to test equipment designed to look for life on Mars.
> A microbe from a hot spring gave us PCR, and extremophiles tell us where to look for life beyond Earth.

# Forest cover
The world has about 4 billion hectares of forest, roughly 31% of the land. Just five countries, Russia, Brazil, Canada, the United States and China, hold more than half of it.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "World forests", "items": ["Tropical: about 45%", "Boreal: about 27%", "Temperate: about 16%", "Subtropical: about 11%"]}
### Portugal's forest
Portugal's national forest inventory counts about 3.2 million hectares of forest, about 36% of the mainland. Three trees dominate, each covering roughly a quarter of the forest area.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "thousand hectares, Portugal (2015)", "items": [{"l": "Eucalyptus", "v": 845.0}, {"l": "Cork oak", "v": 720.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Maritime pine", "v": 713.0}, {"l": "Holm oak", "v": 350.0}]}
### Three stories
• **Eucalyptus**, from Australia, grows fast and feeds the paper industry. It is controversial: it uses a lot of water and burns fiercely.
• **Maritime pine**, native to the Atlantic coast, has shrunk over recent decades because of fires and the pine wood nematode, a tree-killing worm.
• **Cork oak** forms the montado of the Alentejo. Portugal produces about half of the world's cork.
Most Portuguese forest, over 80%, is privately owned, often in very small plots in the north and centre. That makes coordinated management and fire prevention harder.
ex: Portugal's mainland ≈ 8.9 million ha | Forest ≈ 3.2 million ha | About 36%
!? Finland is the most forested country in the EU, with about three-quarters of its land covered by trees.

# How trees drink
A suction pump can lift water only about 10 metres, because air pressure can't push it any higher. Yet the tallest living tree, a coast redwood in California called Hyperion, is about 116 m tall. Trees don't push water up; they pull it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Water evaporates from leaf", "Pulls next molecule up", "Chain rises up the trunk", "Roots draw in soil water"]}
### A chain of water
This is the **cohesion–tension** theory, proposed in 1894. Water molecules stick strongly to each other (cohesion). Inside the wood, a continuous column of water runs through tiny tubes called **xylem**, from root tip to leaf. When water evaporates from small pores in the leaves, called stomata, it tugs on the column, which is pulled up from below.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Leaves: water evaporates", "Trunk: xylem, under tension", "Roots: take up soil water"]}
### Living on the edge
The water in the xylem is under strong tension, stretched like a rubber band. In drought the tension can grow so high that air bubbles snap into the tubes, blocking them. This is one way drought kills trees.
• A large tree can move hundreds of litres of water on a hot day
• Most of it is lost to the air; only about 1% is used to build the tree
• Physics may cap tree height at roughly 120 to 130 m, because water at the top is so hard to pull
!? On a quiet summer day, a large oak can release a bathtub or more of water into the air.
!! Roots pump water up the tree => The pull comes from the leaves; roots mainly let water in
> Trees drink through their leaves: evaporation at the top pulls an unbroken thread of water from the roots.

# Tree rings
In temperate climates, a tree grows fast in spring and slows in late summer. Spring wood is pale with large cells; late wood is darker and denser. Together they make one visible ring per year.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Spring: fast, pale wood", "Summer: slow, dark wood", "Winter: growth stops", "New ring starts"]}
### Reading the past
Ring width reflects that year's conditions: wide in warm, wet years, narrow in droughts. By matching patterns of wide and narrow rings across living trees, old beams and ancient logs, scientists build long calendars. This is **dendrochronology**, founded by the American astronomer A. E. Douglass in the early 1900s.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Wide ring", "b": "good year: warm, wet"}, {"a": "Narrow ring", "b": "drought or cold"}, {"a": "Scar", "b": "fire or injury"}, {"a": "Double ring", "b": "summer drought pause"}]}
### Longer than history
The European oak and pine record stretches back more than 12,000 years. Tree rings are also used to check radiocarbon dates, making archaeology more precise.
ex: Count rings from the bark inwards | Each ring = 1 year | 87 rings means the tree is about 87 years old at that height
### The Mediterranean twist
In Portugal, many trees pause in the summer drought and grow again in autumn. This can create "false" double rings, so dating Mediterranean trees needs extra care. Many tropical trees have no clear rings at all.
!? A burst of radiation from the Sun in the year 774 left a carbon spike in tree rings worldwide; it helped date a Viking site in Canada to exactly 1021.
> Every ring is a diary page, recording one year of weather in wood.

# Deciduous vs evergreen
Leaves lose water all the time through their pores. In winter, frozen or cold soil makes water hard to replace, and frost can damage soft leaves. Deciduous trees solve this by shedding them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Deciduous", "items": ["broad, thin leaves", "fast growth in summer", "drops leaves in autumn"]}, "r": {"t": "Evergreen", "items": ["tough leaves or needles", "works all year", "slow leaf turnover"]}}
### Autumn colours
Before dropping its leaves, a tree pulls back valuable nutrients, especially nitrogen. As green chlorophyll breaks down, yellow and orange pigments that were there all along show through. Reds are new pigments that some trees make in autumn.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Days shorten, cool", "Tree reclaims nutrients", "Chlorophyll fades", "Seal forms, leaf falls"]}
### Two strategies for Portugal
In the wet north, native woods are mostly deciduous: the English oak, the Pyrenean oak and the sweet chestnut. In the dry south, the cork oak and holm oak are evergreen. Their tough, leathery leaves resist summer drought, which is the real stress in a Mediterranean climate.
### Exceptions
• The larch is a conifer that drops its needles in autumn
• Holly and the strawberry tree are broadleaved but evergreen
• In tropical dry forests, trees drop leaves in the dry season, not in winter
!! Evergreen trees never lose their leaves => They do, gradually: pine needles typically last two to five years
> Dropping leaves and keeping tough ones are two solutions to the same problem: losing water when it can't be replaced.

# Carbon stores
About half of a tree's dry weight is carbon, taken from the air as CO2 during photosynthesis. World forests hold around 660 billion tonnes of carbon, in trunks, roots, dead wood and soil.
ex: 1 tonne of dry wood | ≈ 0.5 tonnes of carbon | × 3.67 ≈ 1.8 tonnes of CO2 removed from the air
### The land sink
Each year, forests and other land ecosystems absorb roughly a quarter to a third of the CO2 that humans emit. The oceans take about another quarter. The rest stays in the air.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of human CO2 emissions, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Stays in air", "v": 47.0}, {"l": "Land, mostly forest", "v": 29.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Oceans", "v": 24.0}]}
### Easily released
That carbon can go back fast. Fires, logging, drought and pests turn forests from a sink into a source. Parts of the south-eastern Amazon now release more carbon than they absorb, because of deforestation and fire. In 2017 fires burned over 500,000 hectares in Portugal, releasing millions of tonnes of CO2.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sink", "items": ["growing forest", "healthy soil"]}, "r": {"t": "Source", "items": ["fire, logging", "drought, pests"]}}
### Old vs young
Young forests grow fast and absorb carbon quickly. Old forests absorb more slowly but store far more. Planting trees helps, but protecting existing forests matters more, since lost carbon takes decades or centuries to rebuild.
!! Planting trees can cancel our emissions => There isn't enough land; forests help, but cutting fossil fuel use is what matters most
> Forests are a carbon bank: a big balance, but one that fire and axes can withdraw in a day.

# Rainforest layers
Tropical rainforests grow where it is warm all year and rain falls heavily, usually over 2,000 mm a year. They cover only about 6% of the land, yet are thought to hold around half of all land species.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Emergents: giants, 50-70 m", "Canopy: most life, 30-45 m", "Understory: dim, young trees", "Forest floor: about 2% light"]}
### Life in the canopy
The canopy is a nearly continuous roof of leaves and the richest layer. Here grow flowers, fruits and plants that live on branches, called **epiphytes**, such as orchids and bromeliads. Some bromeliads hold small pools of water where frogs raise tadpoles, high above the ground.
### The dark floor
Only about 2% of sunlight reaches the ground. Young trees wait for decades in the shade until a big tree falls and opens a gap. Dead leaves rot fast in the heat, and nutrients are taken up again almost at once. That's why rainforest soils are often poor, and farms on cleared land lose fertility within a few years.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Canopy", "items": ["bright, windy", "flowers, fruits", "many insects, birds"]}, "r": {"t": "Floor", "items": ["dark, still", "fungi, decay", "ants, termites"]}}
### Counting the species
In 1982 the American entomologist Terry Erwin sprayed the canopy of single trees in Panama and collected over 1,000 beetle species. His bold estimate of 30 million insect species on Earth is now thought too high, but it showed how much life lives overhead.
!? The tallest known tropical tree, a yellow meranti in Borneo, is about 100 m tall.
> In a rainforest, most of the life is up in the roof, not on the ground.

# Deforestation
The world loses about 10 million hectares of forest a year to deforestation. New planting and natural regrowth offset part of that, so the net loss is lower, around 4 to 5 million hectares a year. Regrowth rarely matches the richness of the old forest.
ex: 10 million hectares = 100,000 km² | Mainland Portugal ≈ 89,000 km² | More than a Portugal-sized area lost each year
### What drives it
Close to 90% of deforestation is to make room for farming. The crop depends on the region.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Amazon, Latin America", "b": "cattle pasture, soy"}, {"a": "Indonesia, Malaysia", "b": "oil palm plantations"}, {"a": "West Africa", "b": "cocoa farms"}, {"a": "Everywhere", "b": "roads, mining, logging"}]}
### Europe's role
Europe imports large amounts of soy for animal feed, plus palm oil, cocoa, coffee, beef and wood. The EU Deforestation Regulation, adopted in 2023, will require companies to prove these goods didn't come from land cleared after 2020. Its start has been delayed more than once.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Road cut into forest", "Valuable trees logged", "Rest burned, cleared", "Pasture or crops"]}
### Some good news
Brazil's Amazon deforestation fell sharply after 2004, rose again in the late 2010s and has fallen since 2023. Satellite monitoring now lets anyone see clearing almost in real time.
!! Most forest is lost to logging for timber => Most is cleared for farming; logging often just opens the way
> Forests mostly disappear to feed people and animals, so what we buy shapes what's cleared.

# Trees talk?
In 1983 two studies, on willows and on poplars and maples, reported that undamaged trees boosted their defences when their neighbours were attacked. Newspapers called them "talking trees".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Insects chew leaves", "Leaves release scent", "Neighbours detect it", "Neighbours build defences"]}
### How it works
Damaged leaves release airborne chemicals, such as methyl jasmonate. Nearby plants that sense them start making bitter or toxic compounds before they are attacked. Sagebrush in the USA, tomato plants and maize all show this. Some scents also call in help: maize under caterpillar attack attracts parasitic wasps that lay eggs in the caterpillars.
### The "wood wide web"
Underground, fungi link to tree roots in partnerships called **mycorrhizae**, trading nutrients for sugar. In 1997 the Canadian ecologist Suzanne Simard reported carbon moving between trees through these fungal links. Books popularised the idea of "mother trees" feeding their young.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Well supported", "items": ["chemical warnings", "fungi help roots"]}, "r": {"t": "Still debated", "items": ["mother trees", "trees sharing food"]}}
A 2023 review found the evidence for resource sharing through fungal networks in real forests much weaker than often claimed.
### Is it communication?
Biologists disagree on the word. A tree may simply leak chemicals, and its neighbours "eavesdrop". Communication usually implies the sender benefits from sending.
!! Trees have friendships and feelings => They respond to chemical signals; there's no evidence of emotions or intentions
> Trees certainly react to each other's chemical signals; whether they are "talking" is a matter of definition.

# Ancient trees
Trees can live far longer than animals because they keep growing new tissue every year. A tree is less one body than a growing stack of layers.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. age, years", "items": [{"l": "Sobreiro Assobiador", "v": 243.0, "t": "~240", "hi": true}, {"l": "Oliveira do Mouchão", "v": 3350.0, "t": "~3,350 (est.)"}, {"l": "Methuselah pine", "v": 4850.0, "t": "~4,850"}]}
### The oldest
Methuselah, a Great Basin bristlecone pine in California, is about 4,850 years old, the oldest known non-clonal tree. In Portugal, the Oliveira do Mouchão olive tree in Abrantes was estimated in 2016 at about 3,350 years old, though dating olives is difficult because their old wood rots away.
### The Whistling Cork Oak
The Sobreiro Assobiador in Águas de Moura, near Palmela, was planted in 1783. It gets its name from the many birds that sing in its branches. It has been stripped of its cork over 20 times.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1783", "l": "planted"}, {"v": "20+", "l": "cork harvests"}, {"v": "2018", "l": "European Tree of the Year"}]}
In 1991 a single harvest gave over 1,200 kg of cork, enough for more than 100,000 bottle stoppers. An ordinary cork oak gives a small fraction of that per harvest.
### How cork is harvested
A cork oak is first stripped at about 25 years old, then every 9 years. The tree is not cut down: skilled workers peel the outer bark by hand with an axe, and it grows back. Cork oaks have long been protected by Portuguese law.
!? Some clonal trees are far older: the roots of a spruce in Sweden called Old Tjikko have survived about 9,500 years, regrowing new trunks.
> Old trees are living archives, and Portugal's Whistling Cork Oak is still giving cork after 240 years.

# Forests and health
In 1982 Japan's forestry agency coined **shinrin-yoku**, "forest bathing": spending time slowly and attentively among trees. It isn't exercise; it's being there, using the senses.
### What studies find
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Lower cortisol, a stress hormone", "Lower pulse and blood pressure", "Better mood, less anxiety", "Better attention afterwards"]}
Japanese researchers compared people walking in forests with people walking in cities. The forest groups showed lower stress hormone levels and blood pressure. Many studies are small and short, and it's hard to separate the forest from the relaxation of a day out, so the size of the effect is still debated.
### A view of trees
In a 1984 study, hospital patients recovering from surgery whose window faced trees went home slightly sooner and needed fewer strong painkillers than those facing a brick wall.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "View of trees", "items": ["shorter stay", "fewer strong painkillers"]}, "r": {"t": "View of a wall", "items": ["longer stay", "more strong painkillers"]}}
### How much?
A 2019 English study of about 20,000 people found that those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature were more likely to report good health and wellbeing. It didn't matter whether the time came in one visit or several.
ex: 120 minutes a week | ÷ 7 days | About 17 minutes a day
In Portugal, forests such as Buçaco, Sintra or the oak woods of Peneda-Gerês are easy places to start.
!! You need a remote wilderness to benefit => Parks and city trees also help; regular, unhurried time matters most
> Regular time among trees is a simple, well-supported way to lower stress, even if the exact effect is still measured.

== na-conservation

# The extinction crisis
Extinction is normal. Over the history of life, more than 99% of all species that ever lived have died out. What worries biologists is the speed. The 2019 global assessment by IPBES, the UN's science panel on biodiversity, estimated that today's extinction rate is at least tens to hundreds of times higher than the average over the past 10 million years.
### The background rate
Fossils let scientists estimate how often species normally vanish, known as the **background rate**. A common rough figure is about one species per million each year. Recent losses of mammals, birds and amphibians are far above that, and many extinctions go unrecorded because most species have never been described.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "extinction rate (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Background rate", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Today, low estimate", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Today, high estimate", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}]}
### What drives it
IPBES ranked five direct drivers, in roughly this order of impact:
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Changes in land and sea use", "Direct exploitation (hunting, fishing)", "Climate change", "Pollution", "Invasive species"]}
These drivers add up. A frog population already squeezed by drained wetlands is far more likely to collapse when a new disease or a drought arrives.
### A sixth mass extinction?
Earth has had five mass extinctions, each wiping out roughly three-quarters or more of species in a geologically short time. We are not there yet: recorded extinctions since 1500 are well under 1% of known species. The concern is the trajectory, which is why some scientists say we are entering a sixth.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "444 Mya", "l": "End-Ordovician"}, {"y": "252 Mya", "l": "The Great Dying"}, {"y": "66 Mya", "l": "Dinosaurs end"}, {"y": "Now", "l": "A sixth beginning?"}]}
!! Extinction only happens to exotic animals far away => Europe has lost species too, and freshwater fish are among Portugal's most threatened animals
> Extinction is natural; the problem is that we have made it many times faster than normal.

# The Red List
The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) started its Red List in 1964. It is now the world's most widely used check-up on the health of species. By 2025 it had assessed about 170,000 species, and more than 47,000 of them were classed as threatened.
### The categories
Experts score each species against set criteria: how fast it is declining, how small its range is and how few adults remain. The result places it on a ladder.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Extinct / Extinct in the Wild", "Critically Endangered", "Endangered", "Vulnerable", "Near Threatened / Least Concern"]}
The middle three rungs together count as "threatened". A species can also be listed as **Data Deficient** when there isn't enough information to judge, which is common for fish, insects and fungi.
### Who is most at risk
The proportion threatened varies hugely between groups.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of assessed species threatened", "items": [{"l": "Reef corals", "v": 44.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Amphibians", "v": 41.0}, {"l": "Sharks and rays", "v": 37.0}, {"l": "Mammals", "v": 26.0}, {"l": "Birds", "v": 12.0}]}
### Why it matters
• Governments use it to set priorities and laws
• Moving down the list is a measurable sign that conservation worked
• Moving up is an early warning that something is going wrong
In 2024 the Iberian lynx moved from Endangered to Vulnerable, two steps away from Critically Endangered, where it sat in 2002.
!? Only a small fraction of the world's estimated 8 million or so species has been assessed. Plants, insects and fungi are badly under-represented, so the true number of threatened species is far higher.
> The Red List is a thermometer for nature: it can't cure anything, but it shows where the fever is.

# Habitat is key
Every species needs somewhere to feed, shelter and breed. When a forest becomes a soya field or a wetland is drained, most of the animals don't move elsewhere; they simply disappear. Agriculture now uses about half of the planet's habitable land, which is why it tops the list of threats.
### Smaller pieces, fewer species
Habitat is lost not only by clearing but also by **fragmentation**: roads, fences and farms cut what is left into islands. Small patches hold fewer species, and their populations are more likely to die out through bad luck, inbreeding or a single fire.
= S = c × A^z (species–area rule: S species, A area)
ex: z is often about 0.25 | Keep 10% of a habitat | 0.1^0.25 ≈ 0.56, so roughly half the species can survive long term
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Continuous forest", "Road and farms cut it", "Isolated small patches", "Local extinctions"]}
### Joining the dots
**Wildlife corridors** link patches so animals can move, find mates and shift their range as the climate warms.
• Hedgerows and river banks act as natural corridors on farmland
• Overpasses for wildlife in Banff, Canada, cut road collisions with large animals by more than 80%
• Europe's Natura 2000 network covers about 18% of EU land, including about a fifth of Portugal
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fragmented", "items": ["isolated patches", "inbreeding", "no escape route"]}, "r": {"t": "Connected", "items": ["corridors link patches", "genes mix", "can track climate"]}}
!! Animals just move when their home is destroyed => Neighbouring habitat is usually already full, so displaced animals mostly die
> Protect enough habitat, keep it connected, and most species can look after themselves.

# Success stories
Bad news dominates conservation headlines, but there is a long list of species pulled back from the edge. The recipe usually combines legal protection, an end to the main threat and patient work over decades.
### Four comebacks
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Humpback whale", "b": "whaling ban, now tens of thousands"}, {"a": "Bald eagle", "b": "DDT banned 1972, off US list 2007"}, {"a": "Iberian lynx", "b": "under 100 in 2002, over 2,000 now"}, {"a": "Giant panda", "b": "forests protected, now Vulnerable"}]}
The bald eagle shows how one fix can matter. The pesticide DDT made eggshells so thin they broke under the sitting parent. In 1963 only about 400 nesting pairs remained in the lower 48 US states; by 2019 there were more than 70,000.
### The lynx at home
The Iberian lynx was the world's most endangered cat. It eats mostly rabbits, and rabbit numbers crashed with disease and habitat loss. Spain and Portugal bred lynxes in captivity, including at a centre in Silves in the Algarve, restored rabbit populations and built underpasses on roads.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "wild Iberian lynx (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "2002", "v": 94.0}, {"l": "2015", "v": 550.0}, {"l": "2024", "v": 2400.0, "hi": true}]}
Since 2015 lynxes have been released in the Guadiana valley near Mértola, and a few hundred now live in Portugal.
!? Road traffic is now one of the main causes of death for Iberian lynx, a sign that a recovering population faces new, modern threats.
> Species recover when we remove the threat and give them time; extinction is not inevitable.

# Protected areas
National parks, nature reserves and marine reserves are the backbone of conservation. The idea is old: Yellowstone in the USA became the first national park in 1872. Portugal's only national park, Peneda-Gerês, was created in 1971.
### The 30×30 target
In December 2022, at the COP15 meeting in Montreal, nearly 200 countries adopted the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework. Its headline target is to protect 30% of land and 30% of the sea by 2030.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% protected, about 2024", "items": [{"l": "Land now", "v": 17.6}, {"l": "Ocean now", "v": 8.4}, {"l": "Target 2030", "v": 30.0, "hi": true}]}
### Quality, not just quantity
A line on a map protects nothing on its own. Critics talk of **paper parks**: areas that are protected in law but still logged, fished or poorly managed. Much of the "protected" ocean still allows fishing; only about 3% is fully or highly protected.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Paper park", "items": ["drawn on a map", "no rangers or money", "fishing continues"]}, "r": {"t": "Effective park", "items": ["clear rules enforced", "funded management", "local people involved"]}}
### Portugal's sea
In 2024 the Azores created the largest network of marine protected areas in the North Atlantic, covering about 30% of its sea, with half of that fully protected from fishing. Fully protected reserves often see fish biomass grow several times over within a decade, and fish spill over into nearby fishing grounds.
!! Protected areas mean keeping people out => Many of the best-run reserves include local communities, farming or fishing rules agreed with them
> A protected area is only as good as the protection that actually happens inside it.

# Rewilding
Traditional conservation often manages a site closely: mowing meadows, culling deer, planting trees. **Rewilding** takes a different approach. It aims to restore the natural processes, such as grazing, predation, flooding and natural fires, and then step back.
### The missing pieces
Many landscapes lack the large animals that once shaped them. Rewilding projects often bring back a **keystone species**: one whose effect on its ecosystem is much larger than its numbers suggest.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Large grazers", "b": "open up scrub, create mosaics"}, {"a": "Beavers", "b": "build dams, store water"}, {"a": "Predators", "b": "keep grazers moving"}, {"a": "Vultures", "b": "clean up carcasses"}]}
### The Greater Côa Valley
In north-east Portugal, farming villages have emptied over decades, and abandoned land fills with flammable scrub. Since 2019 Rewilding Portugal, part of Rewilding Europe, has worked here, building on the Faia Brava reserve, Portugal's first private protected area.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Farmland abandoned", "Scrub builds up", "Wild horses and cattle graze", "Mosaic, fewer big fires"]}
Garrano horses and hardy Tauros cattle graze freely, roe deer have been released and vultures are fed and monitored. The aim is a corridor linking the Côa valley with the Douro and the Malcata mountains.
### Honest debates
The famous story of wolves returning to Yellowstone in 1995 is often told as a neat chain reaction, from fewer elk to more trees and changed rivers. Later research found the effects real but patchier and more complicated than the popular version.
!? Abandoned farmland is expanding across Europe; by some estimates, millions of hectares could become available for rewilding by 2030.
> Rewilding asks not "what should this place look like?" but "what processes would shape it if we let them?"

# Zoos and seed banks
Conservation on site, called **in situ**, is always best. When a species is close to vanishing in the wild, an **ex situ** backup, kept elsewhere, can buy time.
### Arks for animals
Modern zoos run coordinated breeding programmes across many institutions. In Europe, EAZA (the European Association of Zoos and Aquaria) manages them to keep populations genetically healthy.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1969", "l": "Przewalski's horse lost"}, {"y": "1982", "l": "Arabian oryx returned"}, {"y": "1987", "l": "Last 27 condors caught"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "Przewalski's horse back"}]}
The California condor dropped to 27 birds, all taken into captivity. Today there are more than 500, over half flying free. Captive breeding is costly and works best when the cause of decline in the wild can also be fixed.
### Banks for seeds
Seeds of many plants can survive for decades or centuries when dried and frozen. Seed banks store them as an insurance policy against war, disaster and disease.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2008", "l": "Svalbard vault opened"}, {"v": "−18 °C", "l": "storage temperature"}, {"v": "1.3 million", "l": "samples deposited"}]}
The Svalbard vault on a Norwegian Arctic island is a backup of backups. Countries deposit duplicates and keep ownership. In 2015 the vault had its first withdrawal: a research centre that had fled Aleppo during the Syrian war recovered seeds to rebuild its collection.
!! Freezing a species in a bank means it's safe => Without wild habitat, it can't return; and recalcitrant seeds like acorns and avocados can't be dried and frozen
> Zoos and seed banks are a lifeboat, not a destination.

# Citizen science
Professional scientists can't be everywhere. Volunteers can. Citizen science turns millions of ordinary observations into data detailed enough to spot trends no single research team could see.
### A long tradition
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1900", "l": "Christmas Bird Count"}, {"y": "1979", "l": "Big Garden Birdwatch"}, {"y": "2002", "l": "eBird launched"}, {"y": "2008", "l": "iNaturalist begins"}]}
eBird now holds more than a billion bird records, and iNaturalist users have shared hundreds of millions of observations. Photos are checked by other users and by image-recognition software.
### How it helps
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["You log a sighting", "Experts verify it", "Data pooled worldwide", "Trends and maps made"]}
• Bird counts revealed the steep decline of farmland birds across Europe
• Butterfly transects show how species shift north as climates warm
• River monitoring spots pollution incidents that official sampling misses
• Amateurs regularly find species new to a region, and occasionally new to science
In Portugal, the BioDiversity4All platform feeds into iNaturalist, and the Portuguese Society for the Study of Birds (SPEA) runs common bird surveys with volunteers.
!? In the UK's Big Garden Birdwatch, around half a million people count birds over one weekend each year, building one of the world's longest-running garden wildlife datasets.
> Every single sighting is small; millions together can show what is happening to a whole continent's wildlife.

# The value of nature
Nature does work that we would otherwise have to pay for or could not do at all. Economists call these benefits **ecosystem services**.
### Four kinds of service
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ecosystem services", "items": ["Provisioning: food, wood", "Regulating: floods, climate", "Supporting: soil, nutrients", "Cultural: recreation, beauty"]}
### Some numbers
• About three-quarters of the main types of food crop benefit from animal pollination
• Pollinated crops are worth hundreds of billions of dollars a year worldwide
• Many medicines began in nature: aspirin from willow bark, penicillin from a mould, the cancer drug paclitaxel from the Pacific yew
• Wetlands and mangroves soak up floodwater and blunt storm surges
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "trillion US$ per year (estimates)", "items": [{"l": "Global GDP 1997", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "Nature 1997", "v": 33.0}, {"l": "Nature 2011 estimate", "v": 125.0, "hi": true}]}
These figures come from the ecologist Robert Costanza and colleagues. They are rough, and critics say putting a price on nature can backfire: something valued at €1 million can seem worth destroying for €2 million.
### A Portuguese example
The montado, Portugal's cork oak woodland, supplies about half the world's cork. It also stores carbon, shelters birds such as the black vulture and supports grazing, honey and tourism, all from the same land.
!! Protecting nature always costs jobs and money => Many economies, from fishing to tourism, depend on healthy ecosystems
> Nature's services are free to use, which is exactly why they are easy to overuse.

# What you can do
Individual actions won't solve the biodiversity crisis alone, but they add up, and they shape what businesses and politicians think people care about.
### Food
Food production is the biggest pressure on nature, so food choices matter most.
• Waste less: in the EU, households throw away roughly 70 kg of food per person per year
• Choose sustainable seafood, looking for labels such as MSC and avoiding species in decline
• Eat a more plant-rich diet, which needs far less land
ex: Portuguese eat about 55–60 kg of seafood per person a year | One of the highest rates in the world | Small shifts here add up to many tonnes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Waste less food", "Choose sustainable seafood", "Plant native species", "Support protected areas", "Volunteer locally"]}
### Garden and balcony
A small space can help. Native plants feed native insects; a corner left uncut becomes a refuge; a dish of water helps bees and birds in a Portuguese summer. Avoid pesticides and peat-based compost.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Neat lawn, exotic plants", "r": "Wild corner, native flowers"}, {"w": "I can't make a difference", "r": "Join a group and multiply it"}]}
### Beyond your own home
Join a local conservation group, count birds for a survey, vote with nature in mind and ask councils about green spaces. Groups such as Quercus, SPEA and Rewilding Portugal welcome volunteers.

== na-behaviour

# Instinct and learning
An **instinct** is a behaviour that appears complete the first time, without practice or a teacher. A garden spider raised alone still spins the web typical of its species. Learning is any lasting change in behaviour that comes from experience.
### The egg-rolling goose
In the 1930s Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen watched greylag geese. If an egg rolls out of the nest, the goose stretches out and rolls it back with the underside of its bill. If the egg is removed halfway, the goose still finishes the movement with nothing there.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sees egg outside nest", "Movement is triggered", "Rolls egg back", "Finishes even if egg gone"]}
Tinbergen called this a **fixed action pattern**: a built-in sequence that runs to the end once released. In 1973 Lorenz, Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch shared a Nobel Prize for founding the study of animal behaviour.
### Nature and nurture together
Most behaviour mixes both. Many songbirds are born with a rough template of their song but must hear adults during a sensitive period to sing it properly, and they pick up local dialects.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Instinct", "items": ["right first time", "rigid", "good when no time to learn"]}, "r": {"t": "Learning", "items": ["needs practice", "flexible", "good in changing worlds"]}}
### Cracking nuts takes years
West African chimpanzees crack hard nuts with a stone hammer on a root or stone anvil. Young chimps watch their mothers for years, play with stones and nuts, and usually only master the skill at around 6 or 7 years old.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Skilled adult technique", "Practice and failure", "Watching mother", "Curiosity about stones"]}
!! Behaviour is either instinct or learnt => Nearly all behaviour combines inborn tendencies with experience
> Instinct gets an animal started; learning lets it adjust to the world it actually finds.

# Imprinting
Imprinting is a special, fast kind of learning that happens only during a short window early in life. A newly hatched gosling locks on to the first suitable moving object it sees and follows it from then on.
### Lorenz's geese
Konrad Lorenz made imprinting famous in the 1930s. He split a clutch of greylag goose eggs: half hatched with their mother, half with him. The second group followed Lorenz everywhere, and kept doing so even when put with their real mother.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gosling hatches", "Sees a moving object", "Bond forms in hours", "Follows it for weeks"]}
The English biologist Douglas Spalding had described the effect with chicks back in 1873, but Lorenz gave it its name.
### The sensitive period
Imprinting is strongest within roughly the first day or two after hatching. Later, the chicks become wary of new things and the window closes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "strength of following (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "First hours", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "About 15 hours", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "After 2 days", "v": 2.0}]}
### Longer effects
• **Filial imprinting** decides who a chick follows
• **Sexual imprinting** shapes what the animal later finds attractive as a mate
• Birds imprinted on humans may later court people rather than their own kind
### Using it for conservation
Breeders of rare birds avoid imprinting chicks on humans. California condor chicks are fed with a puppet shaped like an adult's head. In the USA, whooping cranes were imprinted on ultralight aircraft and then followed them on migration routes they had never flown.
!? Lorenz's goslings were not unique: imprinting has been found in ducks, chickens and even some mammals such as sheep and goats, which bond with their mothers in the first hours.
> Imprinting is learning with a deadline: what happens in the first hours can shape a whole life.

# Tool use
Using tools was once thought to define humans. In 1960 Jane Goodall watched a chimpanzee in Gombe, Tanzania, strip leaves from a twig and poke it into a termite mound. Her mentor Louis Leakey replied that we must now redefine "tool", redefine "man", or accept chimpanzees as humans.
### Tool users across the animal kingdom
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Chimpanzees", "b": "sticks, stone hammers"}, {"a": "Sea otters", "b": "stones to crack shellfish"}, {"a": "New Caledonian crows", "b": "make hooks from twigs"}, {"a": "Veined octopus", "b": "carries coconut shells"}]}
### Dolphins with sponges
In Shark Bay, Western Australia, some bottlenose dolphins tear off marine sponges and wear them over their beaks while poking in the sandy seabed for fish. The habit, spotted in the 1980s, passes mostly from mother to daughter, which makes it a cultural tradition as well as a tool.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tear off a sponge", "Wear it on the beak", "Probe the sandy floor", "Catch hidden fish"]}
### Making, not just using
Using an object is one level; shaping it is another. New Caledonian crows trim leaves into barbed probes. In a 2002 Oxford experiment, a crow named Betty bent a straight wire into a hook to lift a basket of food from a tube.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Make tools to a design", "Modify objects", "Use objects as found"]}
!? Archaeologists have dug up chimpanzee nut-cracking stones in Côte d'Ivoire about 4,300 years old, so chimps have their own stone age.
!! Only clever mammals use tools => Birds, fish, octopuses and even some insects use tools, so brain size isn't everything
> Tool use isn't a human monopoly; it has evolved many times wherever it pays.

# Self-recognition
In 1970 the psychologist Gordon Gallup put mirrors in front of chimpanzees. At first they treated the reflection as another chimp. After a few days they used it to inspect parts of their own bodies they normally couldn't see.
### The mark test
To test this properly, Gallup anaesthetised the chimps and painted a red mark on a brow they could only see in a mirror. When they woke, they touched the mark while looking at their reflection.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mark placed unseen", "Animal sees mirror", "Touches mark on itself", "Passes the test"]}
### Who passes
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1970", "l": "Chimpanzees"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Bottlenose dolphins"}, {"y": "2006", "l": "Asian elephants"}, {"y": "2008", "l": "Eurasian magpies"}]}
Human children usually pass at around 18 to 24 months. Results for some species are mixed. A 2020 attempt to repeat the magpie study did not find the same result, and a 2019 study claiming a small cleaner fish passed sparked a fierce debate.
### Limits of the test
• It relies on eyesight, but many animals live by smell or hearing
• Some animals may notice the mark but simply not care about it
• Failing does not prove an animal has no sense of self
Dogs fail the mirror test but pay more attention to their own scent when it has been altered, which some researchers see as a smell-based version.

# Animal culture
Biologists use **culture** to mean behaviour that is learnt socially and passed down, so that groups of the same species end up doing things differently. Genes can't explain those differences; tradition can.
### Orcas: one species, many cultures
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fish-eaters", "items": ["large family pods", "chatty calls", "stable routes"]}, "r": {"t": "Mammal-hunters", "items": ["small groups", "hunt in silence", "roam widely"]}}
In the north-east Pacific, fish-eating and mammal-hunting orcas share waters but rarely mix or interbreed. Each pod also has its own dialect of calls. In Patagonia, some orcas deliberately strand themselves on beaches to snatch sea lion pups, a risky skill taught to the young.
### Closer to home
Since 2020 a small group of orcas off Iberia, from the Strait of Gibraltar to Galicia, has been interacting with sailing boats, often biting rudders. The behaviour began with a few individuals and spread through the group. Nobody knows for certain why: play, a fad, or a response to a bad experience are all suggested.
### Classic examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Japanese macaques", "b": "washing sweet potatoes, 1953"}, {"a": "Humpback whales", "b": "songs spread across oceans"}, {"a": "Chimpanzees", "b": "dozens of local customs"}, {"a": "British blue tits", "b": "opening milk bottles"}]}
A 1999 study compared seven long-term chimpanzee study sites and listed 39 behaviours, from tool use to grooming styles, that were common in some communities and absent in others.
!? Humpback whale songs travel like pop hits: new songs have been tracked spreading from eastern Australia across the South Pacific, replacing old songs in population after population within about two years.
> Culture isn't only human: whenever animals copy each other, local traditions can grow.

# Cooperation
Evolution rewards genes that get copied, so helping others seems odd. Biologists have found several ways it can pay.
### Why help?
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why cooperate?", "items": ["Kin: helping relatives", "Mutualism: both gain now", "Reciprocity: payback later"]}
• **Kin selection** (William Hamilton, 1964): relatives share genes, so helping them spreads your own
• **Mutualism**: both sides benefit straight away, as when wolves bring down prey none could kill alone
• **Reciprocal altruism** (Robert Trivers, 1971): I help you now, you help me later
### Three examples
Wolf packs are mostly families: a breeding pair and their grown offspring. Meerkat sentries watch for eagles and jackals while others dig for food; research in the Kalahari found sentries had usually eaten already, so guarding is also safer for them than foraging.
Cleaner wrasse eat parasites off bigger fish. Cleaners are tempted to cheat by biting off tasty mucus instead. Client fish chase cheats or swim away, and cleaners behave better when other clients are watching.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Client arrives", "Cleaner eats parasites", "Client stays healthy", "Client comes back"]}
### Paying it back
Vampire bats that fail to feed can starve within about three days. Well-fed bats regurgitate blood to hungry roost-mates, and they are more likely to share with bats that have shared with them before.
!! Wolf packs are ruled by a fighting "alpha" => The idea came from unrelated captive wolves; in the wild, the "alphas" are usually just the parents
> Cooperation evolves when helping others also, in the long run, helps the helper's genes.

# Courtship
Charles Darwin was puzzled by the peacock's tail: it is heavy, conspicuous and makes escape harder. His answer, set out in 1871, was **sexual selection**: traits can spread because they win mates, even if they cost survival.
### Why females are usually choosy
In most species females invest more in each offspring, with large eggs, pregnancy or care. A bad choice costs them more, so they are pickier, and males compete to impress.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Females choose", "Showy males breed more", "Trait spreads", "Displays get bigger"]}
### Honest signals
A popular idea, the **handicap principle**, says only a healthy male can afford a costly display, so it is an honest advert of quality. Results are mixed: early studies found peahens prefer males with more eyespots, but some later studies, including one in Japan, found no clear preference.
### Displays around the world
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Peacocks", "b": "shimmering tail fan"}, {"a": "Bowerbirds", "b": "decorated stick bowers"}, {"a": "Peacock spiders", "b": "colour flap and dance"}, {"a": "Great bustards", "b": "turn into feather balls"}]}
Male satin bowerbirds in Australia gather blue objects, from feathers to bottle caps, and arrange them around a bower they build. In Portugal's Alentejo, around Castro Verde, male great bustards turn their wings and tails inside out in spring until they look like white balls of feathers.
Peacock spiders, only about 5 mm long, raise a brightly coloured flap on their abdomen and dance. Females that aren't impressed may eat them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Natural selection", "items": ["survive", "find food", "avoid predators"]}, "r": {"t": "Sexual selection", "items": ["attract mates", "beat rivals", "often costly"]}}
!? Darwin wrote to a friend in 1860 that the sight of a feather in a peacock's tail made him feel sick, because he could not yet explain it.
> Spectacular displays exist because choosy partners reward them, even when they make life more dangerous.

# Play
Play looks pointless: it burns energy, distracts from feeding and can attract predators. Yet it is widespread in young mammals and birds, and found in some reptiles, fish and even octopuses. That suggests it pays.

### What it's good for
• Practice: play-fighting and chasing rehearse hunting and escape
• Fitness: running and climbing build muscles and coordination
• Social skills: learning how hard to bite, and how to make up
• Flexibility: dealing with surprises, falls and lost balance

Dogs signal play with a **play bow**: front legs down, rump up. It says "what follows is not serious". Young rats raised without playmates grow up socially awkward and react badly to stress.
### Fun for its own sake?
Ravens slide down snowy roofs and slopes on their backs, then walk back up and do it again. Keas in New Zealand throw objects and roll in snow. Rats make high-pitched chirps when tickled by people and come back for more, which the neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp called a form of laughter.

# Navigation
Animals find their way across oceans and continents with no maps. They do it by combining several senses, often switching between them on different parts of the journey.
### Salmon: following the smell of home
Young salmon learn the chemical scent of their home stream before heading out to sea. Years later, they use smell to pick the right river and branch. Experiments in the 1950s showed salmon with blocked noses lost their way. The Minho and Lima rivers in northern Portugal are near the southern limit of Atlantic salmon in Europe.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hatch in a stream", "Learn its smell", "Years at sea", "Smell leads them home"]}
### Turtles: a magnetic map
Earth's magnetic field varies in strength and angle from place to place. Sea turtles seem to remember the magnetic "signature" of the beach where they hatched. Loggerhead turtles that hatch in Florida ride the North Atlantic currents, some passing near the Azores and Madeira, then return to nest decades later.

How birds sense magnetism is still being worked out. One leading idea involves a protein called cryptochrome in the eye, which may let birds "see" the field.

# Animal emotions
Emotions are hard to measure even in people, because they are partly private. Scientists study animal emotions through behaviour, body changes such as stress hormones and heart rate, and brain systems shared with humans.
### Shared brain machinery
Fear, rage, care and seeking reward all depend on old brain regions that mammals share. Drugs that reduce anxiety in people tend to calm anxious animals too.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Behaviour: approach or avoid", "Body: stress hormones, heart rate", "Brain: shared emotion circuits", "Choices: what they work for"]}
### Clever experiments
• **Optimism tests**: rats kept in good conditions treat an ambiguous signal as good news more often than stressed rats; similar effects have been found in sheep, dogs and bees
• **Freeing a friend**: in a 2011 study, rats repeatedly opened a trap to free a trapped cage-mate, sometimes even before eating chocolate
• **Paying a price**: animals will work hard or accept discomfort to reach things they seem to want

### The law catches up
The EU's Treaty of Lisbon, in force since 2009, recognises animals as sentient beings. In 2017 Portugal changed its Civil Code so that animals are no longer simply "things" but living beings with sensitivity. The UK's 2022 sentience law even includes octopuses, crabs and lobsters.
### Caution both ways
Scientists warn against **anthropomorphism**, assuming animals feel exactly what we would. But dismissing all animal feelings is also an error. The 2024 New York Declaration, signed by hundreds of researchers, said there is strong support for conscious experience in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility in many other animals.
!! Only humans have real feelings => Many animals show the behaviour, body changes and brain activity linked to emotions in us
> We can't feel what an animal feels, but the evidence that many animals feel something is now hard to ignore.
