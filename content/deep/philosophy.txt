== ph-greeks

# What philosophy is
The word comes from Greek: **philo** (love) and **sophia** (wisdom). Philosophy deals with questions where gathering more facts isn't enough on its own. You also have to think hard about concepts, assumptions and arguments.
Many sciences began as philosophy. Physics was once "natural philosophy", and Isaac Newton's great book of 1687 is called *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*. Psychology split off only in the late 1800s. When a question becomes answerable by experiment, it tends to leave home and become its own science.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Philosophy", "items": ["What is real? (metaphysics)", "What can we know? (epistemology)", "How to live? (ethics)", "Good reasoning (logic)"]}
### What philosophers actually do
• Clarify concepts: what do we mean by "fair", "cause" or "person"?
• Find hidden assumptions behind everyday beliefs
• Build arguments and test them against counterexamples
• Ask what follows if a view is true, including awkward consequences
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Science asks", "items": ["What happens?", "How can we measure it?"]}, "r": {"t": "Philosophy asks", "items": ["What does it mean?", "Should we do it?"]}}
The two often work together. Debates about brain death, artificial intelligence or climate justice need both scientific facts and careful thinking about values.
!? A doctorate in almost any subject, from chemistry to economics, is called a PhD: Doctor of Philosophy, a fossil of the time when all serious study was philosophy.
> Philosophy starts where facts alone stop settling the question.

# Before Socrates
Around 600 BCE, in Greek cities on the coast of what is now Turkey, a few thinkers tried something new. Instead of explaining storms and earthquakes by the moods of gods, they looked for natural causes and general principles. Historians call them the **Presocratics**.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.585 BCE", "l": "Thales, Miletus"}, {"y": "c.500 BCE", "l": "Heraclitus, Ephesus"}, {"y": "c.475 BCE", "l": "Parmenides, Elea"}, {"y": "c.420 BCE", "l": "Democritus, Abdera"}]}
### Big ideas, little evidence
• **Thales** said everything is made of water. Wrong, but a bold attempt to find one underlying stuff.
• **Anaximander** proposed a boundless, undefined substance and guessed that humans came from fish-like creatures.
• **Heraclitus** stressed constant change: you can't step into the same river twice.
• **Parmenides** argued the opposite: real change is impossible, and our senses mislead us.
• **Democritus** and his teacher Leucippus imagined tiny, uncuttable pieces moving in empty space: *atomos* means "uncuttable".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thales", "b": "everything is water"}, {"a": "Heraclitus", "b": "everything flows"}, {"a": "Parmenides", "b": "change is an illusion"}, {"a": "Democritus", "b": "atoms and void"}]}
What mattered was less the answers than the method. They gave reasons, criticised each other and expected rivals to argue back. That habit of open disagreement is the real birth of philosophy and, later, of science.
!? Thales is said to have predicted a solar eclipse in 585 BCE. Historians debate whether he really could, but the story shows how early Greeks linked thinking with nature.
> The first philosophers swapped "the gods did it" for "what is it made of, and why?"

# Socrates
Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE) was a stonemason's son who served as a soldier and spent his days talking in the agora, Athens' public square. He wrote nothing. We know him mainly through his student Plato, the historian Xenophon and a mocking comedy by Aristophanes, *The Clouds*, and their portraits don't always agree.
His habit was to ask people who claimed expertise, such as generals, politicians and poets, to define things like courage or justice. Each definition met a counterexample, until the expert admitted confusion. Young Athenians loved watching; the experts did not.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ask: what is courage?", "Hear a definition", "Find a counterexample", "Admit confusion, try again"]}
### The trial
In 399 BCE he was charged with impiety and with corrupting the young. A jury of about 500 citizens convicted him, by roughly 280 votes to 220. Athens had just lived through war defeat and a brutal oligarchy, and some of Socrates' former companions had been involved, which probably counted against him.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "jury votes (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Guilty", "v": 280.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Not guilty", "v": 220.0}]}
He refused to stop philosophising to save himself and turned down a chance to escape. He drank hemlock, a poison, in prison, talking philosophy with friends to the end.
!! Socrates was a lone hero against a backward city => Athens was a democracy, and his trial raises a hard question still debated: can a democracy wrongly silence a critic?
> Socrates showed that a question asked honestly can be more dangerous than any weapon.

# "I know that I know nothing"
A friend of Socrates asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The answer was no. Puzzled, Socrates questioned people with a reputation for wisdom and found they believed they knew things they did not. He concluded he was wiser only in one small way: he didn't think he knew what he didn't know.
!! Socrates said "I know that I know nothing" => That exact line isn't in Plato; it's a later summary of his claim not to think he knows what he doesn't
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "The experts", "items": ["don't know", "think they know"]}, "r": {"t": "Socrates", "items": ["doesn't know", "knows he doesn't"]}}
### The examined life
At his trial, as told in Plato's *Apology*, Socrates said he'd rather die than stop questioning, because a life without self-examination isn't worth living for a human being. He meant testing your beliefs about how to live, not just collecting facts.
### Why it still matters
• Admitting ignorance is the first step to learning anything
• Psychologists describe a related bias: people with little skill in an area often overrate their ability
• Good scientists, doctors and judges build doubt into their methods
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice a belief", "Ask: how do I know?", "Test it", "Keep, revise or drop it"]}
> Knowing the edges of your knowledge is itself a kind of knowledge.

# Plato's cave
Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) tells the story in Book VII of the *Republic*. Prisoners have been chained since childhood facing a cave wall. Behind them a fire burns, and people carry objects past it, casting shadows. The prisoners name the shadows and compete to predict them. For them, shadows are all there is.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["The Sun: the Form of the Good", "Real things outside", "Objects carried past the fire", "Shadows on the wall"]}
### The climb
One prisoner is freed. Turning to the fire hurts his eyes; he wants to go back to the comfortable shadows. Dragged up into daylight, he is dazzled, and only slowly can he look at reflections, then real things, then the Sun itself.
When he returns to free the others, his eyes, now used to light, stumble in the dark. The prisoners laugh at him and say the journey ruined his sight. Plato adds that they would kill anyone who tried to free them, a clear nod to Socrates' fate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chained: shadows only", "Turn to the fire", "Climb into daylight", "Return to free others"]}
### Readings today
• Education as turning the whole person, not pouring facts into an empty head
• A warning about mistaking images, propaganda or social media feeds for reality
• Critics say it is elitist: who decides who has "seen the light"?
!? Films such as *The Matrix* (1999) and *The Truman Show* (1998) are often read as modern retellings of the cave.
> Real learning is uncomfortable, because it means turning away from what is familiar.

# Plato's Forms
Draw a circle as carefully as you can. Under a magnifying glass it's wobbly. No physical circle is perfect, yet you know exactly what a perfect circle is and can prove facts about it. Plato asked: where does that knowledge come from?
His answer was the **Forms**: perfect, unchanging patterns that exist beyond the physical world. Things we see "participate in" or copy them. A beautiful face, sunset and melody are beautiful because they share in the Form of Beauty.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Things we see", "items": ["many and changing", "imperfect copies", "known by senses"]}, "r": {"t": "Forms", "items": ["one and eternal", "perfect patterns", "known by reason"]}}
### Why he believed it
• Mathematics seems to be about perfect objects we never meet in the world
• We judge things as more or less equal or just, which seems to need a standard
• Plato thought true knowledge must be of something that doesn't change
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Form of the Good", "Forms (Circle, Justice)", "Physical things", "Shadows and images"]}
### The objections
Plato himself raised a famous problem in his dialogue *Parmenides*. If big things share a Form of Bigness, then the big things and the Form together seem to need another Form to explain what they share, and so on forever. Aristotle called this the "third man" argument. He also asked how a Form outside space and time could cause anything here.
!? Mathematicians still argue about "Platonism": the view that numbers and shapes really exist, discovered rather than invented.
> For Plato, the world we see is a copy; the original can only be grasped by thinking.

# Aristotle
Aristotle was born in Stagira in northern Greece in 384 BCE. At about 17 he joined Plato's Academy in Athens and stayed for roughly 20 years. Later, King Philip of Macedon hired him to tutor his teenage son, the future Alexander the Great.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "384 BCE", "l": "Born in Stagira"}, {"y": "367 BCE", "l": "Joins Plato's Academy"}, {"y": "343 BCE", "l": "Tutors Alexander"}, {"y": "335 BCE", "l": "Founds the Lyceum"}, {"y": "322 BCE", "l": "Dies in Chalcis"}]}
### A different temperament
Where Plato looked up to the Forms, Aristotle looked down at the world. He dissected animals, classified hundreds of species and collected the constitutions of over 150 Greek cities. For him, the form of a thing lives inside it: the form of an oak is in the acorn, guiding its growth.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plato", "items": ["Forms beyond the world", "mathematics first", "reason over senses"]}, "r": {"t": "Aristotle", "items": ["form inside things", "biology first", "observe, then reason"]}}
### His legacy
• Invented formal logic: the syllogism ruled for over 2,000 years
• Founded biology as a careful, observational study
• Wrote on ethics, politics, rhetoric, poetry and physics
• Medieval Islamic and Christian scholars called him simply "the Philosopher"
His followers were called **Peripatetics**, perhaps because he taught while walking in the Lyceum's covered walkways. What survives, about 30 works, are mostly lecture notes; his polished writings are lost.
!! Aristotle's science was simply wrong => Much of his physics failed, but his biology was so careful that Darwin admired it
> Aristotle brought philosophy down from heaven to look closely at the living world.

# The golden mean
In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle describes a virtue as a settled habit of feeling and acting well. Each virtue sits between two vices: one of too much and one of too little.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "facing danger (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Cowardice", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Courage", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Recklessness", "v": 9.0}]}
### Not the exact middle
The mean is "relative to us", not a mathematical average. Aristotle's example: if 10 kilos of food is too much and 2 too little, a trainer won't prescribe 6 to everyone; a champion wrestler needs more than a beginner. Likewise, the courageous response to a fire differs for a firefighter and a child.
Sometimes the right action is close to one extreme. Fury may be right in the face of cruelty. The mean is whatever the situation calls for, at the right time, towards the right people, for the right reason.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Generosity", "b": "between meanness and waste"}, {"a": "Honesty", "b": "between false modesty and boasting"}, {"a": "Good temper", "b": "between apathy and rage"}, {"a": "Friendliness", "b": "between flattery and rudeness"}]}
### How you get there
You become brave by doing brave things, as you become a guitarist by playing. Aristotle's practical wisdom, **phronesis**, is the skill of seeing what each situation requires.
!! The golden mean means always being moderate => Some acts, like murder, have no right amount; and sometimes strong emotion is the mean
> Virtue is not the midpoint, but the right amount for this moment.

# Eudaimonia
The Greek word joins **eu** (good) and **daimon** (spirit). Translating it as "happiness" misleads, because Aristotle didn't mean a pleasant mood. He meant a whole life going well: flourishing, as a plant thrives when it grows into what it can be.
### The function argument
A knife's function is to cut, so a good knife cuts well. Aristotle asked what is distinctive about humans. Plants grow; animals feel; only humans reason. So a good human life is one of reasoning and acting well, which means practising virtue, over a whole lifetime.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Every thing has a function", "Ours: to live by reason", "Do it well: virtue", "Over a life: eudaimonia"]}
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Feeling good", "items": ["a passing state", "can come from a pill"]}, "r": {"t": "Eudaimonia", "items": ["a whole life", "built by activity"]}}
### What it needs
• Virtue of character and practical wisdom
• Friends, which Aristotle discusses at great length
• Some luck and resources: health, enough money, a decent city
• Time: one good day no more makes a flourishing life than one swallow makes a spring
### Objections
Critics ask whether humans really have one function, and whether a cruel tyrant who feels great is really failing. Aristotle's list of needs also excluded slaves and women from full flourishing, a view now universally rejected.
!? Modern psychologists distinguish "hedonic" well-being (pleasure) from "eudaimonic" well-being (purpose and growth), borrowing Aristotle's word.
> A flourishing life is something you do, not something you feel.

# The Socratic method today
Socrates' style of questioning is called **elenchus**, Greek for "cross-examination". In Plato's *Euthyphro*, Socrates meets a man prosecuting his own father and asks him what piety is. Every definition collapses under follow-up questions. The dialogue ends without an answer, but with clearer thinking.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Claim", "Clarifying question", "Counterexample", "Revised claim"]}
### Where you meet it
• **Law schools**: in the US, professors grill students on cases rather than lecture
• **Therapy**: cognitive behavioural therapy uses Socratic questions to test anxious thoughts
• **Teaching**: guiding a pupil to discover a rule rather than telling it
• **Coaching and management**: "What would success look like?"
ex: Thought: "I'll fail this exam" | Q: What's the evidence? "I struggled with one topic." | Q: How did you do last time? "I passed." | Revised: "I need to revise one topic."
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Clarify", "b": "What do you mean by…?"}, {"a": "Evidence", "b": "How do you know?"}, {"a": "Alternatives", "b": "Could it be otherwise?"}, {"a": "Consequences", "b": "What follows if true?"}]}
Good Socratic questioning is curious, not a trap. Done badly, it can feel like humiliation, as many of Socrates' victims found.
> The best teachers don't hand you answers; they hand you better questions.

== ph-ethics

# Three big theories
Ethics asks what makes actions right or wrong and what kind of person to be. Most modern theories fall into three families, each spotlighting a different part of any action.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ethical theories", "items": ["Consequences: outcomes", "Deontology: duties", "Virtue: character"]}
### One case, three lenses
Suppose you could lie to a friend to spare their feelings about a bad haircut.
• A **consequentialist** asks: which choice leads to the most well-being overall?
• A **deontologist** asks: is there a duty not to lie, whatever the result?
• A **virtue ethicist** asks: what would an honest, kind person do here?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Consequentialism", "b": "Bentham, Mill, Singer"}, {"a": "Deontology", "b": "Kant, Ross, Rawls"}, {"a": "Virtue ethics", "b": "Aristotle, Foot, MacIntyre"}]}
The three often agree: torturing for fun is wrong on every view. They split on hard cases, which is where they get tested.
### Each has a classic weakness
• Consequentialism can seem to justify terrible acts if the sums work out
• Duty theories can seem rigid when rules clash or disaster looms
• Virtue ethics can seem vague: "do what a good person would" isn't always a clear instruction
Many philosophers mix elements. Doctors, for example, weigh outcomes, respect patients' rights and value professional character all at once.
!? The word "consequentialism" was coined only in 1958, by the British philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe, who was criticising it.
> Every ethical theory is a spotlight: it shows one part of the moral picture brightly and leaves another in shadow.

# Utilitarianism
Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) was a London reformer who wanted laws judged by one test: do they increase happiness and reduce suffering? He counted everyone equally, including animals: the question, he said, is not whether they can reason but whether they can suffer.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Right act = max total", "items": [{"s": "Count", "m": "everyone affected"}, {"s": "Add", "m": "pleasures minus pains"}, {"s": "Choose", "m": "the biggest total"}]}
### Mill's refinement
John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), in *Utilitarianism* (1861), added that pleasures differ in quality. Poetry and friendship count for more than idle amusement: better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.
ex: Option A: 4 people gain 5 units | 4 × 5 = 20 | Option B: 10 people gain 3 units | 10 × 3 = 30 | Utilitarian pick: B
### Strengths
• Impartial: your happiness counts no more than a stranger's
• Practical: it underlies cost–benefit analysis and public-health planning
• Drove reforms: Bentham and Mill backed votes for women and an end to slavery
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Appeal", "items": ["everyone counts", "clear goal", "fits policy"]}, "r": {"t": "Objections", "items": ["ignores rights", "hard to measure", "too demanding"]}}
### The objections
Can you really measure and add happiness? Could the sums justify punishing an innocent person to calm a mob? And if every euro you spend on luxuries could save lives abroad, as Peter Singer argues, utilitarianism may demand almost everything you have. Defenders reply with refinements such as "rule utilitarianism": follow rules that, generally obeyed, do the most good.
!? Bentham's preserved skeleton, dressed in his clothes and topped with a wax head, sits in a glass case at University College London, as he requested.
> Utilitarianism asks one simple question with a hard answer: what does the most good?

# Kant's duty
Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) spent his whole life in Königsberg, a Prussian city now Kaliningrad in Russia. In the *Groundwork* (1785) he argued that morality can't rest on consequences, which are uncertain, but on reason itself.
### Two kinds of "ought"
A **hypothetical imperative** says: if you want X, do Y. "If you want to pass, study." A **categorical imperative** says: do this, full stop. Kant thought moral rules are categorical: they bind you whatever you happen to want.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hypothetical", "items": ["if you want X…", "depends on goals"]}, "r": {"t": "Categorical", "items": ["do this, full stop", "binds every rational being"]}}
### The universal law test
Before acting, state your rule ("maxim") and imagine everyone following it.
ex: Maxim: borrow money with a false promise to repay | Universalise: everyone makes false promises | Result: nobody believes promises, so the trick can't work | Verdict: wrong
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["State your rule", "Imagine everyone doing it", "Does it still make sense?", "If not, it's forbidden"]}
### The famous objection
A murderer knocks and asks where your friend is hiding. Kant, in a 1797 essay, said you still must not lie. Most readers find that wrong. Kantians reply that he misapplied his own theory, or that the rule can be framed more precisely: "never lie except to someone planning murder" might pass the test.
!! Kant only cared about rules => He cared about motive: an act has moral worth when done from duty, not merely in line with it
> Before you act, ask whether you could live in a world where everyone did the same.

# People as ends
Kant gave a second version of his supreme principle, now called the **formula of humanity**: treat humanity, in yourself and others, never merely as a means, always also as an end.
### "Merely" is the key word
We use people as means all the time. You use the baker to get bread, and the baker uses you to earn a living. That's fine, because both of you agree and your goals are respected. What's wrong is treating someone like a tool: deceiving, manipulating or coercing them so their own choices don't count.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Means and end", "items": ["paying the baker", "asking for help", "fair contract"]}, "r": {"t": "Merely a means", "items": ["fraud", "manipulation", "slavery"]}}
### Dignity, not price
Kant said things in the world have either a price, which can be swapped for an equivalent, or **dignity**, which cannot. People have dignity because they can set their own goals. You can't trade one person's rights for another's convenience.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Informed consent", "b": "patients decide on treatment"}, {"a": "Human dignity clause", "b": "Article 1, EU rights charter"}, {"a": "Ban on organ sales", "b": "bodies aren't merchandise"}]}
### The objections
What about beings who can't make rational choices, like infants, people with severe dementia, or animals? Kant's theory struggles to explain their moral status, and many Kantians have tried to extend it. Others ask where exactly "persuasion" ends and "manipulation" begins, a question now central to debates about advertising and app design.
!? The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU, binding since 2009, opens with "Human dignity is inviolable", a strongly Kantian idea.
> People are not tools; they have their own goals, and those goals count.

# Virtue ethics
Virtue ethics goes back to Aristotle, Confucius and the Stoics. It faded in modern Europe but returned after Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 essay argued that moral philosophy had lost its way by obsessing over rules. Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre and Rosalind Hursthouse developed it further.
### Shifting the question
Rule-based theories ask "what should I do?" Virtue ethics asks "who should I be?" Right action follows from good character, the way good playing follows from skill.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Practise good acts", "Habits form", "Character grows", "Good acts come easily"]}
### Main virtues and their role
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Honesty", "b": "truthful even when costly"}, {"a": "Courage", "b": "right fear, right boldness"}, {"a": "Kindness", "b": "real concern for others"}, {"a": "Practical wisdom", "b": "seeing what fits the case"}]}
### Strengths
• Fits how moral life feels: we admire people, not just acts
• Explains moral growth and moral education
• Handles nuance: honesty with tact differs from bluntness
### Objections
Critics say it gives poor guidance in a dilemma: if you don't know what a virtuous person would do, being told to do it doesn't help. Lists of virtues also vary across cultures. Defenders answer that rules also need judgement to apply, and that virtues like honesty and courage appear in nearly every tradition.
!? Psychology research on "situationism" found that small things, like being in a hurry, can affect whether people help a stranger. Some philosophers think this challenges the idea of stable character; others disagree.
> Good rules help, but in the end it's good people who apply them well.

# The trolley problem
Philosopher Philippa Foot introduced the basic case in 1967 to discuss abortion law and the doctrine of double effect. Judith Jarvis Thomson named it the "trolley problem" and added the bridge version in the 1970s and 1980s.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Switch case", "items": ["pull a lever", "one dies as side-effect", "most say: pull"]}, "r": {"t": "Bridge case", "items": ["push a man", "his body is the brake", "most say: don't"]}}
### Why the difference?
In both cases one dies and five live, so pure outcome-counting treats them alike. Yet intuitions split sharply. Proposed explanations:
• **Double effect**: harm foreseen as a side-effect can be allowed; harm intended as a means cannot
• **Using a person**: pushing treats him as a tool, echoing Kant
• **Personal force**: pushing with your own hands triggers stronger emotional alarms
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% saying yes (typical surveys, approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Pull the lever", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Push the man", "v": 25.0}]}
Psychologist Joshua Greene scanned brains during these dilemmas and found that personal cases engage emotional areas more. He argued intuitions against pushing are fast gut reactions; critics reply that gut reactions can track real moral differences.
### Beyond the classroom
Self-driving cars made it practical. MIT's online "Moral Machine" collected about 40 million choices from people in over 200 countries and territories, published in 2018, and found broad agreement on sparing more lives but cultural differences in detail.
!! The trolley problem is just a silly puzzle => It isolates one variable at a time, like a lab experiment for moral principles
> The same outcome can feel right or wrong depending on how you bring it about.

# Moral luck
Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel named this puzzle in 1976. We believe people should be judged only for what is under their control. Yet our judgements constantly depend on luck.
ex: Driver A texts, glances up, reaches home | Driver B texts, glances up, a child runs out | Same choice, same risk | A gets a fine at most; B may face prison and lifelong blame
### Kinds of moral luck
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Outcome luck", "b": "how things happen to turn out"}, {"a": "Circumstantial luck", "b": "the tests life puts you to"}, {"a": "Constitutive luck", "b": "temperament you were born with"}]}
Circumstantial luck is chilling. Many ordinary people in 1930s Germany did terrible things they would never have faced in another country. Would you have been different? You can't know.
### The law agrees with luck
Most legal systems, Portugal's included, punish an attempted crime less than a completed one, even when the intent was identical. Drunk driving is punished, but drunk driving that kills is punished far more.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Control principle", "items": ["judge choices only", "same choice, same blame"]}, "r": {"t": "Actual practice", "items": ["outcomes matter", "results change blame"]}}
### Responses
Some say we should judge only intentions and risks, and treat the different outcomes as legally useful but morally irrelevant. Others accept moral luck as a real feature of life: we are agents in a world we don't control.
!? Williams's famous example was a painter like Gauguin who abandons his family for his art. Whether he was justified, Williams suggested, may depend on whether he becomes a great painter, which is partly luck.
> We think blame should follow choices, but in practice it follows results.

# The veil of ignorance
John Rawls (1921–2002), a Harvard philosopher, set out this thought experiment in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), one of the most discussed books in modern political philosophy.
### The original position
Imagine choosing the basic rules of your society from behind a veil that hides your sex, class, talents, health, religion and ethnicity. Because you can't rig the rules for yourself, Rawls argued, whatever you'd choose there counts as fair.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hide who you'll be", "Choose society's rules", "Can't favour yourself", "Choices = fair rules"]}
### Rawls's two principles
• Equal basic liberties for all: speech, conscience, vote, fair trial
• Fair equality of opportunity for jobs and positions
• The **difference principle**: inequalities are allowed only if they benefit the worst off
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "income of worst off (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Society A", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Society B", "v": 18.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Society C", "v": 14.0}]}
In this illustration, Rawls would pick B even if A or C had a higher average income, because B's worst-off group does best.
### Strong objections
• John Harsanyi argued that rational choosers would maximise average welfare, not insure against the worst case
• Robert Nozick (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974) argued that justice depends on how wealth was acquired, not on its pattern
• Communitarians such as Michael Sandel said nobody really chooses as a "person" stripped of all ties
!? The idea of fair division by ignorance is old: "I cut, you choose" for sharing a cake works on the same logic.
> If you didn't know where you'd land, which society would you want to live in?

# Is and ought
Scottish philosopher David Hume (1711–1776), in his *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), noticed that authors often move from statements about what **is** the case to what **ought** to be, without explaining how. He doubted the jump can be made by logic alone.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Conclusion: you ought not smoke", "Value: you ought to protect health", "Fact: smoking harms health"]}
### The missing premise
Facts alone can't produce an "ought". To reach one, you need at least one premise that already contains a value. Often it's hidden because everyone shares it.
ex: Fact: this bridge is unsafe | Hidden value: we should not put people at serious risk | Conclusion: we ought to close the bridge
### Where it bites
• "It's natural, so it's good" (or "unnatural, so bad") skips the value premise
• Evolution explains why we have certain urges; it doesn't show we should act on them
• Data can tell us a policy's effects; deciding whether it's worth it needs values
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Humans evolved to eat sugar, so we should", "r": "Evolution explains urges, not what's right"}]}
### Challenges
Some philosophers think Hume's gap can be bridged. John Searle argued in 1964 that "she promised" seems to imply "she ought to do it". Others say concepts like "cruel" or "rude" mix fact and value from the start.
!? This idea is often nicknamed "Hume's guillotine", because it seems to cut facts off from values.
> Facts tell you how the world is; to say what you should do, you also need a value.

# A hard choice
This "transplant" case, sharpened by Judith Jarvis Thomson in the 1970s, mirrors the trolley switch: kill one, save five. Yet almost nobody accepts the surgeon's choice, even people who would pull the trolley lever.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simple sum", "items": ["5 lives saved", "1 life lost", "net +4"]}, "r": {"t": "Almost everyone", "items": ["it's murder", "violates the visitor", "destroys trust"]}}
### How each theory responds
• **Kantian**: the visitor is used merely as a means; forbidden
• **Rule utilitarian**: if hospitals did this, nobody would visit one; worse overall
• **Virtue ethicist**: no good doctor could do this; it betrays the role
• **Rights theorist**: the visitor's right to life is a barrier, not a number
### Reflective equilibrium
Rawls described a method for ethics that resembles science. Principles are like theories; strong case judgements are like data. When they clash, you adjust one or the other until they fit.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Propose a principle", "Test on a hard case", "Clash? Revise one", "Test again"]}
This is why philosophers invent strange cases. They isolate one factor at a time. If a theory says "sacrifice the visitor", that's evidence against the theory, just as a failed prediction counts against a scientific hypothesis.
!! Thought experiments are unrealistic, so useless => Real cases mix many factors; simple cases show which one is doing the work
> Ethics advances when theories meet hard cases and have to change.

== ph-mind

# Descartes' doubt
René Descartes (1596–1650) was a French mathematician who invented the coordinate graph (x and y axes). He wanted knowledge as solid as geometry, so in his *Meditations* (1641) he decided to tear everything down and rebuild only on what could not possibly be doubted.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["An evil demon could fool me", "I might be dreaming", "My senses sometimes deceive", "Ordinary beliefs"]}
### Three waves of doubt
• Senses deceive: a straight stick looks bent in water
• Dreams: while dreaming you are convinced it's real, so how can you be sure now?
• The evil demon: imagine a powerful deceiver feeding you false experiences, even in maths
### The one certainty
Even if a demon deceives me, I must exist to be deceived. Doubting is a kind of thinking, and thinking needs a thinker. Hence *cogito, ergo sum*: I think, therefore I am. The French version, "je pense, donc je suis", appeared first, in his *Discourse on Method* (1637).
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Doubt everything", "Doubting is thinking", "Thinking needs a thinker", "So I exist"]}
### Objections
• Critics such as Georg Lichtenberg said Descartes was only entitled to "there is thinking", not to an "I"
• To rebuild the world, Descartes relied on a proof that a good God wouldn't deceive us, and his proof of God relied on clear ideas, which looked circular to many readers
!? Descartes died in Stockholm in 1650, a few months after moving there to tutor Queen Christina, who wanted lessons at 5 a.m. in a freezing winter.
> When you doubt everything, the doubter is still left standing.

# Rationalists vs empiricists
In the 1600s and 1700s, European philosophy split over a simple question: where does knowledge come from?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rationalists", "items": ["Descartes, Spinoza", "Leibniz", "reason, innate ideas"]}, "r": {"t": "Empiricists", "items": ["Locke, Berkeley", "Hume", "experience, senses"]}}
### The rationalist case
Some truths seem knowable without looking: 7 + 5 = 12, or every event has a cause. Descartes thought ideas like God and infinity couldn't come from experience and must be innate.
### The empiricist case
John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) compared the newborn mind to white paper, often called a **tabula rasa** or blank slate. Everything is written there by sensation and reflection. Hume pushed this hard: if an idea can't be traced back to an impression, it's suspect.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1641", "l": "Descartes' Meditations"}, {"y": "1689", "l": "Locke's Essay"}, {"y": "1739", "l": "Hume's Treatise"}, {"y": "1781", "l": "Kant's synthesis"}]}
### Kant's compromise
Immanuel Kant argued in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) that both were partly right. Knowledge begins with experience, but the mind supplies the structure, such as space, time and cause, that makes experience possible at all.
!? Locke discussed "Molyneux's problem": could a blind person who regained sight recognise a cube by sight alone? A 2011 study of children treated for congenital blindness in India found they at first couldn't, but learned within days.
!! Empiricists thought the mind does nothing => Locke and Hume gave the mind powers to compare, combine and reflect; they denied only built-in ideas
> Kant's lesson: experience gives the content, the mind gives the shape.

# The problem of induction
David Hume set out the problem in the *Treatise* (1739) and his *Enquiry* (1748). Every prediction from experience rests on one assumption: that nature will continue as before. But how do we know that?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Observed: Sun rose daily", "Assume: nature is uniform", "Predict: it rises tomorrow", "But why trust the assumption?"]}
### The circle
You can't prove it by logic, because a world where the Sun stops is perfectly imaginable. You can't prove it by experience either: "induction has worked in the past, so it will work in the future" uses induction to defend induction.
### Russell's chicken
Bertrand Russell (1912) described a chicken fed by the farmer every morning. Each day its confidence grows. Then one morning the farmer wrings its neck. More data made the chicken surer, not safer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "chicken's confidence (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Day 1", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Day 100", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Day 999", "v": 99.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Day 1000", "v": 0.0}]}
### Responses
• Hume himself: we can't justify it, but we can't help doing it; it's custom, and life needs it
• Popper: science doesn't need induction, only bold guesses and tests
• Probability theorists: we can't be certain, but evidence can rationally raise our confidence
!? Financial crises are a modern chicken story: models trained on years of calm data often fail exactly when conditions change.
> No amount of past evidence logically guarantees the future, yet we have no better guide.

# Falsifiability
Karl Popper (1902–1994) grew up in Vienna, where in 1919 he noticed a contrast. Freud's and Adler's theories seemed to explain every possible behaviour, while Einstein's relativity made a risky prediction about starlight bending near the Sun, which could have failed.
### The asymmetry
No number of white swans proves that all swans are white. But one black swan proves that not all are. Europeans thought this was settled until Dutch explorers saw black swans in Western Australia in 1697.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Confirming", "items": ["1,000 white swans", "still not proof"]}, "r": {"t": "Refuting", "items": ["1 black swan", "enough to disprove"]}}
### The criterion
Popper's *Logic of Scientific Discovery* (German 1934, English 1959) proposed that a theory is scientific only if some possible observation could contradict it. This isn't about whether it's true; it's about whether it takes a risk.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Testable", "b": "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level"}, {"a": "Testable", "b": "This drug lowers blood pressure"}, {"a": "Not testable", "b": "Invisible fairies cause luck"}, {"a": "Not testable", "b": "Things happen for a reason"}]}
### Criticisms
Real tests are messy. When a prediction fails, scientists can blame an auxiliary assumption instead of the main theory, and sometimes they're right to. Philosophers Pierre Duhem and W. V. O. Quine stressed that theories always face tests as a bundle.
!! A theory that explains everything is very strong => If no observation could ever count against it, it tells you nothing about the world
> A good theory sticks its neck out: it says what you should never see.

# The mind–body problem
You decide to raise your arm, and it rises. A thought seems to move matter. But thoughts don't have mass or position the way neurons do. How do the two connect?
### Descartes' dualism
Descartes argued the mind is a non-physical thinking thing, separate from the extended body. He suggested they interact via the pineal gland in the brain.
In 1643, Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia wrote him a sharp question: how can something without size or contact push something physical? His answers never fully satisfied her, or later philosophers.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Mind and body", "items": ["Substance dualism", "Property dualism", "Physicalism", "Idealism"]}
### The main positions
• **Substance dualism**: mind and body are two kinds of thing
• **Property dualism**: one kind of thing, the brain, but with irreducible mental properties
• **Physicalism**: the mind is entirely physical; mental states are brain states or functions
• **Idealism**: reality is fundamentally mental; matter depends on mind
### Evidence from the brain
Brain damage changes personality, memory and judgement. In 1848, railway worker Phineas Gage survived an iron rod through his frontal lobe, and friends said he was "no longer Gage". Physicalists see such cases as strong support. Dualists reply that a damaged instrument can't play well, even if the player is separate.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of philosophers, 2020 PhilPapers survey", "items": [{"l": "Physicalism", "v": 52.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Non-physicalism", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "Other", "v": 16.0}]}
> Every view must explain how the felt life of the mind fits with the physical brain, and none does so effortlessly.

# The hard problem
David Chalmers named it in a 1995 paper after a talk at a 1994 consciousness conference in Tucson, Arizona. The talk became one of the most cited ideas in modern philosophy of mind.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Easy problems", "items": ["how we spot colours", "attention, memory", "reporting states"]}, "r": {"t": "Hard problem", "items": ["why red looks red", "why it feels like anything"]}}
### Why "easy" isn't easy
The easy problems may take centuries, but we know what an answer looks like: find the mechanism that performs the function. For the hard problem, even a complete map of the mechanisms seems to leave a question: why isn't all that processing happening "in the dark"?
### Zombies
Chalmers asks you to imagine a philosophical zombie: an exact physical copy of you that behaves identically but has no inner experience. If that is even coherent, he argues, experience is something extra to physical facts.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Illusionism: the problem is a trick", "Physicalism: it'll be solved", "Dualism: experience is extra", "Panpsychism: matter feels, a bit"]}
### Pushback
Daniel Dennett argued the hard problem is an illusion produced by how the brain models itself, and that zombies are not truly conceivable. Others think it will dissolve as neuroscience matures, as "what is life?" did in biology.
!? In 1998 neuroscientist Christof Koch bet Chalmers a case of wine that the brain basis of consciousness would be found within 25 years. In 2023 Koch conceded and paid up.
> You can describe what a brain does without ever explaining why it feels like something.

# What is it like to be a bat?
Thomas Nagel published this paper in 1974. He chose bats because they're mammals, so surely conscious, yet they perceive the world mainly through echolocation, a sense we lack.
### The argument
A creature is conscious if there is "something it is like" to be it. Science describes from the outside, in an objective, third-person way. But the bat's experience is essentially first-person. Adding more outside facts never seems to give you the inside view.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Outside facts", "items": ["sound frequencies", "brain circuits", "flight paths"]}, "r": {"t": "Inside view", "items": ["what echoes feel like", "the bat's point of view"]}}
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1,400+", "l": "bat species worldwide"}, {"v": "1974", "l": "Nagel's paper"}]}
### Mary's room
Frank Jackson's 1982 version: Mary is a brilliant scientist who knows every physical fact about colour vision but has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room. When she first sees a red tomato, does she learn something new? If yes, physical facts weren't everything.
### Replies
• Some say Mary gains a new ability (to recognise and imagine red), not a new fact
• Others say she meets an old fact in a new way, as you can know "water" and "H₂O" separately
• Jackson himself later changed his mind and became a physicalist
!! Nagel argued minds are non-physical => He argued our current concepts can't yet explain experience, and left physicalism possibly true but not understood
> Knowing everything about a mind from outside may still leave out what it's like inside.

# The Chinese room
John Searle published the thought experiment in 1980 to challenge "strong AI", the claim that a correctly programmed computer literally understands.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chinese questions slide in", "Man follows the rulebook", "Chinese answers slide out", "He understands nothing"]}
### The argument
The man in the room doesn't know Chinese. He matches symbols by their shape. Outsiders think a fluent speaker is inside. A computer, Searle said, does the same: it manipulates symbols by rules (**syntax**) with no grasp of what they mean (**semantics**). Syntax alone isn't enough for meaning.
### The famous replies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Systems reply", "b": "the whole room understands"}, {"a": "Robot reply", "b": "add senses and a body"}, {"a": "Brain simulator", "b": "copy neurons, not rules"}, {"a": "Searle's answer", "b": "still just symbols"}]}
Searle replied to the systems reply: let the man memorise the whole rulebook. He is now the whole system, and he still doesn't understand. Critics answer that our intuitions about something so huge and slow are unreliable.
### Its link to Turing
Alan Turing's 1950 "imitation game" proposed judging machine intelligence by behaviour in conversation. Searle's room is designed to pass that test while lacking understanding, to show behaviour isn't enough.
!? A real rulebook for conversation would be astronomically large, and the man would take years per answer. Defenders of AI say this hides how different the real system is.
> Searle's question: can following rules ever add up to understanding?

# Personal identity
The ancient Greek writer Plutarch told of the ship of Theseus, kept in Athens with rotting planks replaced one by one. Thomas Hobbes added a twist: suppose someone collects the old planks and rebuilds them. Which is the real ship?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Repaired ship", "items": ["same shape, place", "every plank new"]}, "r": {"t": "Rebuilt ship", "items": ["all original planks", "assembled later"]}}
### Theories of personal identity
• **Body**: you are your living organism
• **Memory**: John Locke (1694) said you are the same person as far back as your consciousness reaches
• **Psychological continuity**: overlapping chains of memories, beliefs and intentions
• **No-self**: Buddhists and Hume doubted there's a fixed self at all
### Reid's officer
Thomas Reid attacked Locke's memory theory. A boy is flogged; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers being an officer but not the flogging. By Locke's rule, the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, yet the general is not the boy. Most theorists now use overlapping chains instead.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Boy: flogged", "Officer: remembers it", "General: remembers officer", "Same person as boy?"]}
### Parfit's teleporter
Derek Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) imagined a teleporter that scans you, destroys you and builds an exact copy on Mars. Is it travel or death? He concluded identity may matter less than we think; what matters is continuity of psychology.
!! All your cells are replaced every seven years => Many are, but most neurons in your cerebral cortex and the cells in your eye's lens stay with you for life
> You change constantly, yet something keeps you "you", and philosophers still disagree about what.

# Thinking about AI
Large language models, released widely from 2022, write essays and hold conversations that easily pass casual versions of Turing's test. That turned old thought experiments into live questions for engineers, regulators and the public.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Questions about AI", "items": ["Does it understand?", "Could it be conscious?", "Is it a moral patient?", "Who is responsible?"]}
### The understanding debate
• Searle-style sceptics: models predict the next word from statistics; impressive syntax, no meaning
• Functionalists: if a system uses information in the right ways, that just is understanding
• Middle views: understanding comes in degrees, and models may have some kinds but not others
### The consciousness debate
In 2022 a Google engineer said a chatbot was sentient; most experts disagreed. In 2023, a group of scientists and philosophers published a report listing "indicator properties" drawn from theories of consciousness, and judged that no current system was a strong candidate, while seeing no obvious barrier to building one.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Risk A", "items": ["treat a tool as a person", "be manipulated by it"]}, "r": {"t": "Risk B", "items": ["ignore a real mind", "cause unseen suffering"]}}
### Why it matters in practice
The EU's AI Act (2024) regulates AI by risk and requires that people be told when they're talking to a chatbot. Questions of responsibility, deception and trust don't wait for the deep metaphysics to be settled.
!? Some philosophers argue we may never be sure whether a system is conscious, because the hard problem leaves no test that everyone accepts.
> Old puzzles about minds now have to be answered about machines we use every day.

# Stoicism
The school began around 300 BCE when Zeno of Citium taught at the Stoa Poikile, the "painted porch" in Athens, which gave it its name. It flourished for 500 years and reached its most read writers under the Roman Empire.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Epictetus", "b": "freed slave, teacher"}, {"a": "Seneca", "b": "statesman, Nero's adviser"}, {"a": "Marcus Aurelius", "b": "emperor 161–180 CE"}]}
### Core ideas
• Virtue (wisdom, justice, courage, self-control) is the only true good
• Wealth, health and fame are "preferred indifferents": nice, but not needed for a good life
• Emotions like rage and panic come from mistaken judgements, which we can examine
• Humans are part of one rational cosmos and owe something to all people, not just their city
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Event happens", "Judgement: 'this is terrible'", "Emotion follows", "Re-examine the judgement"]}
### A living influence
In the 1950s and 60s, psychologists Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, founders of what became cognitive behavioural therapy, credited Epictetus's insight that we're upset not by things but by our opinions about them.
### Objections
Critics ancient and modern ask whether Stoicism asks too much. Should a grieving parent really treat their child's death as "indifferent"? And focusing on your inner attitude might make people too accepting of injustice that could be changed.
!! Stoic means emotionless => Stoics aimed to replace destructive passions with good emotions like joy, care and calm resolve
> Stoicism trains the one thing always in your power: how you respond.

# The dichotomy of control
The *Enchiridion* ("handbook"), a summary of Epictetus's teaching made by his student Arrian, opens with this idea. Some things are up to us: our opinions, choices, desires and aversions. Others are not: our body, property, reputation and status.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Up to me", "items": ["my effort", "my judgements", "how I treat people"]}, "r": {"t": "Not up to me", "items": ["the weather", "others' opinions", "the final result"]}}
### The middle category
Modern writers like William Irvine (2009) add a third box: things we can **influence** but not control. You can't control whether you win a match, but you can control how well you train and play.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Control: act fully", "Influence: do your part", "Neither: accept and adapt"]}
### The Stoic archer
Cicero described the Stoic view with an archer. Her goal is to shoot well: choose the bow, aim carefully, release cleanly. Once the arrow leaves, wind may carry it off. Hitting the target is preferred, but shooting well is the real aim.
ex: Job interview tomorrow | Control: prepare, sleep, arrive early | Influence: first impression | Neither: other candidates, the final decision | Spend tonight on the first box
### Objections
Drawing the line is not always easy, and people sometimes underrate their influence on politics, health or work. Critics worry the method could become an excuse for passivity. Stoics answered that duty to others is firmly "up to us".
!? The 20th-century "Serenity Prayer" (grant me the serenity to accept what I cannot change…) expresses a very similar idea in religious form.
> Put your energy where it can change something, and let the rest go.

# Negative visualisation
The Latin name is **premeditatio malorum**, "the premeditation of evils". Seneca recommended imagining exile, illness or loss in advance, so that if they arrived they would hurt less and would not come as a shock.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Imagine losing something", "Feel its value now", "Plan how you'd cope", "Return with gratitude"]}
### Two goals
• **Gratitude**: we stop noticing what we have; imagining its loss makes it vivid again
• **Resilience**: the mind rehearses the blow, so it lands softer
Psychologists call the first problem **hedonic adaptation**: a new phone or job thrills us, then quickly becomes normal. Negative visualisation is a deliberate counter-move.
### Voluntary discomfort
Seneca also suggested spending a few days a month on plain food and rough clothes, asking yourself "is this what I feared?" Modern versions include cold showers or a day without the phone.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Worrying", "items": ["repeats, spirals", "no plan", "drains energy"]}, "r": {"t": "Stoic exercise", "items": ["brief, deliberate", "ends with a plan", "builds appreciation"]}}
### Use with care
For people prone to anxiety, dwelling on disaster can backfire. The Stoic version is short, purposeful and ends in action and gratitude. Business uses a cousin of it: the "pre-mortem", where a team imagines a project has failed and asks why.
!? Epictetus suggested that when kissing your child goodnight you quietly remember they are mortal. It sounds bleak, but his point was to love them more fully today.
> Imagining loss briefly is a way to treasure what you have now.

# Epicureanism
Epicurus (341–270 BCE) bought a house with a garden outside Athens and founded a community there, unusually admitting women and slaves. His school was called simply "the Garden".
### Pleasure, properly understood
For Epicurus, the goal of life is pleasure, but he defined the best pleasure as the absence of pain in the body (**aponia**) and of trouble in the mind (**ataraxia**). Feasts and fame bring anxiety; a quiet life with friends brings lasting calm.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Desires", "items": ["Natural & necessary: food", "Natural, unneeded: luxury", "Empty: fame, power"]}
### The four-part cure
His followers summarised the teaching as a "four-part remedy":
• Don't fear the gods: they don't punish or reward
• Don't fear death: when death is, we are not
• What is good is easy to get
• What is terrible is easy to endure
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ataraxia", "b": "peace of mind"}, {"a": "Aponia", "b": "freedom from bodily pain"}, {"a": "Friendship", "b": "the greatest security"}]}
### Science against fear
Epicurus adopted Democritus's atoms. If everything, including the soul, is atoms that scatter at death, there is no afterlife to fear. The Roman poet Lucretius spread these ideas in *On the Nature of Things*, a poem rediscovered in 1417 that influenced Renaissance science.
### Objections
Critics, from Stoics to Christian writers, said a life aimed at one's own calm is too self-centred and ignores duty. Others doubt that death is truly nothing to us: we can lose future goods even if we don't feel the loss.
!! An epicure is a lover of luxury => Epicurus lived on bread, water and olives, and once asked a friend for a little pot of cheese as a treat
> The pleasant life is simple: good friends, few needs, no unnecessary fears.

# Buddhist philosophy
Siddhartha Gautama was born a prince, probably in the 5th century BCE, at Lumbini in what is now Nepal; scholars debate the exact dates. Shocked by old age, sickness and death, he left home to seek an answer. After years of extreme fasting, he decided harsh self-denial was no better than luxury.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Two extremes", "items": ["indulgence", "self-torture"]}, "r": {"t": "The Middle Way", "items": ["balanced living", "clear seeing"]}}
### Three marks of existence
• **Impermanence** (anicca): everything that arises passes away
• **Unsatisfactoriness** (dukkha): what is impermanent can't give lasting satisfaction
• **Non-self** (anatta): no fixed, unchanging self lies beneath our experiences
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Wanting", "Grasping", "Losing", "Suffering, wanting more"]}
### Philosophy or religion?
Buddhism includes rituals, monasteries and beliefs about rebirth, which make it a religion for about 500 million people. But much of its core is philosophical: careful analysis of mind, causes and the self, offered to be tested in experience rather than taken on authority.
### Questions and objections
Critics ask whether "non-self" is coherent: who is it that suffers and seeks liberation? Buddhist philosophers developed subtle answers about selves as useful labels for changing processes. Some Western readers also worry the goal of ending desire sounds like ending engagement; Buddhists reply the aim is ending clinging, not caring.
!? The Buddha's first sermon, in a deer park at Sarnath near Varanasi, is said to have been given to five former companions who had abandoned him for giving up fasting.
> The Buddha diagnosed suffering as a habit of grasping, and treated it with a way of living.

# Confucius
Kong Qiu, honoured as Kong Fuzi ("Master Kong") and Latinised as Confucius, lived in the small state of Lu in eastern China during an age of war between rival states. He worked as an official and teacher, and spent years travelling in search of a ruler who would govern by virtue. None fully took his advice.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ren", "b": "humaneness, kindness"}, {"a": "Li", "b": "ritual, proper conduct"}, {"a": "Xiao", "b": "respect for parents"}, {"a": "Junzi", "b": "the exemplary person"}]}
### The Analects
Confucius didn't write a book. His disciples later collected his sayings in the *Analects*, short exchanges full of practical wisdom about friendship, learning and government.
### The negative golden rule
Asked for one word to guide a whole life, Confucius answered **shu**, reciprocity, and explained it as not doing to others what you don't want done to you. Similar rules appear in Greek, Jewish, Christian and Hindu sources.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Negative form", "items": ["don't do to others…", "sets a limit"]}, "r": {"t": "Positive form", "items": ["do to others…", "sets a duty"]}}
### Lasting influence
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "479 BCE", "l": "Confucius dies"}, {"y": "136 BCE", "l": "Han state ideology"}, {"y": "605 CE", "l": "Imperial exams begin"}, {"y": "1905", "l": "Exams abolished"}]}
For about 1,300 years, China's civil service exams tested knowledge of the Confucian classics, so the ideas shaped everyone who governed. Critics, especially during China's May Fourth Movement of 1919, blamed Confucian hierarchy for holding back women and the young. Defenders stress its emphasis on education, merit and duty.

# Existentialism
Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) gave a famous lecture in Paris in 1945, published as *Existentialism Is a Humanism*. His key example was a paper knife. A craftsman has the idea of a paper knife first, then makes it: its essence comes before its existence. Humans, Sartre said, are the reverse.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "A paper knife", "items": ["design first", "then it exists", "purpose fixed"]}, "r": {"t": "A human", "items": ["exists first", "then chooses", "defines themselves"]}}
### Condemned to be free
Without a fixed human nature or a designer's plan, we must choose, and even refusing to choose is a choice. That freedom brings **anguish** (we answer for what we make of ourselves) but also dignity.
### Bad faith
Sartre's word for pretending you have no choice. His example was a café waiter acting a little too much like a waiter, playing a role as if it were his fixed nature. "That's just who I am" can be bad faith.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Kierkegaard", "b": "the individual's leap"}, {"a": "Nietzsche", "b": "create your own values"}, {"a": "Sartre", "b": "radical freedom"}, {"a": "Beauvoir", "b": "freedom in a situation"}]}
### Objections
Simone de Beauvoir, Sartre's partner, argued in *The Second Sex* (1949) that freedom is shaped by circumstances such as gender and poverty: one is not born, but becomes, a woman. Others say Sartre ignored biology, culture and childhood. Religious thinkers reply that human nature and purpose may be real, and existentialism simply assumes otherwise.
!? Sartre was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964 and turned it down, saying a writer should not let himself be turned into an institution.
> You aren't handed a script; you write yourself through what you choose.

# The absurd
Albert Camus (1913–1960) grew up poor in French Algeria. In *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) he described the absurd not as a property of the world, nor of humans, but of their meeting: we demand meaning and clarity, and the universe stays silent.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["We crave meaning", "The universe is silent", "The clash: the absurd", "How should we respond?"]}
### Three responses
Camus asked what follows from the absurd and considered three options.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Escape life: rejected", "Leap of faith: rejected", "Revolt: live fully anyway"]}
He rejected the first because it ends the confrontation rather than facing it. He called the second "philosophical suicide": jumping to a comforting answer the evidence doesn't support. His choice was to keep the tension open and live with lucidity and passion.
### Sisyphus
In Greek myth, Sisyphus is condemned to roll a boulder up a hill forever, only to watch it roll down. Camus pictures him walking back down, fully aware of his fate. In that awareness, he says, Sisyphus is greater than his rock.
### Objections
Religious thinkers deny the universe is silent. Others ask why the absurd should lead to revolt rather than despair or indifference. Camus's answer is less a proof than an attitude: lucidity without false hope.
!! Camus was an existentialist => He rejected the label and distanced himself from Sartre; they publicly fell out in 1952
!? Camus won the Nobel Prize in 1957, aged 44, and died in a car crash in France less than three years later.
> The absurd isn't a reason to stop living; it's the background against which we choose to live.

== ph-logic

# What an argument is
In everyday speech an "argument" is a row. In logic it's something calmer: a set of statements where some (the **premises**) are offered as reasons to accept another (the **conclusion**).
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Conclusion: take an umbrella", "Premise: if rain, get wet", "Premise: forecast says rain"]}
### Spotting the parts
Indicator words help. "Because", "since" and "given that" usually introduce premises. "So", "therefore" and "which means" usually introduce the conclusion. Many arguments hide a premise because it seems obvious.
ex: "She's a vet, so she must love animals" | Hidden premise: all vets love animals | Is that premise true? Probably not always | The argument is weaker than it looks
### Two ways an argument can fail
• A premise is false or doubtful
• The premises, even if true, don't support the conclusion
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Good argument", "items": ["true premises", "real support"]}, "r": {"t": "Not an argument", "items": ["insults", "bare assertion"]}}
### Why it's useful
Laying out premises turns a heated disagreement into a map. You can point to the exact step you dispute. Often people agree on more than they think and differ on one premise, about facts or about values.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Find the conclusion", "List the premises", "Add hidden ones", "Test truth and support"]}
!? Aristotle was the first to study the forms of arguments systematically, over 2,300 years ago; his system of syllogisms was taught in European universities until the 1800s.
> To judge an argument, ask two questions: are the reasons true, and do they really support the claim?

# Valid vs sound
Logicians separate two questions. Is the reasoning structure right? That's **validity**. Are the premises also true? Then the argument is **sound**, and its conclusion must be true.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Valid", "b": "if premises true, conclusion must be"}, {"a": "Sound", "b": "valid and premises true"}, {"a": "Invalid", "b": "conclusion could be false anyway"}]}
### Validity is about form
Validity doesn't care about content. These two arguments share a valid form:
ex: All cats fly; Tom is a cat | So Tom flies (valid, unsound) | All humans are mortal; Ana is human | So Ana is mortal (valid, sound)
The common form: all A are B; x is A; so x is B. Any argument with that shape is valid, whatever you plug in.
### Surprising combinations
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Valid, false end", "items": ["all fish fly", "a shark is a fish", "so a shark flies"]}, "r": {"t": "Invalid, true end", "items": ["some birds swim", "penguins are birds", "so penguins swim"]}}
The right-hand argument has a true conclusion, but the premises don't guarantee it: by that logic, sparrows would swim too. A true conclusion doesn't make reasoning good.
### A common trap
"If it rained, the street is wet. The street is wet. So it rained." This is invalid, called **affirming the consequent**: a street cleaner could have wet it.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "If P then Q; Q; so P", "r": "If P then Q; P; so Q"}]}
> Validity is about the shape of reasoning; soundness adds the truth of what you start from.

# Deduction vs induction
These are two basic directions of reasoning, and most real thinking uses both.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Deduction", "items": ["general to particular", "certain if valid", "adds no new info"]}, "r": {"t": "Induction", "items": ["particular to general", "only probable", "extends knowledge"]}}
### Deduction
If all mammals have lungs and whales are mammals, then whales have lungs. If the premises are true, the conclusion can't be false. Mathematics is the great home of deduction. The price: the conclusion was already hidden in the premises.
### Induction
Every swan you've seen is white, so all swans are white. Induction ventures beyond the evidence, which makes it useful and risky. Strength depends on sample size, variety and whether the sample is representative.
ex: Survey 1,000 voters chosen at random | 52% back a proposal | Typical margin of error about ±3 points | Conclusion: probably 49–55% of all voters
### A third type: abduction
Philosopher Charles Peirce named **abduction**, or inference to the best explanation. The lawn is wet and the sky is clear; the best explanation is that the sprinkler ran. Doctors diagnosing and detectives investigating rely on it.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Reasoning", "items": ["Deduction: must be", "Induction: probably is", "Abduction: best explains"]}
!! Sherlock Holmes is a master of deduction => Most of Holmes's reasoning is abduction: guessing the best explanation from clues, which could be wrong
> Deduction keeps you safe but small; induction lets you learn but never guarantees.

# Ad hominem
Latin for "to the person". The fallacy is rejecting a claim because of who makes it, rather than what is said. Data are true or false whatever the scientist drives.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ad hominem", "items": ["Abusive: insults", "Circumstantial: motives", "Tu quoque: you too"]}
### Three common forms
• **Abusive**: "Only an idiot would believe that"
• **Circumstantial**: "Of course she supports the tax; she works in the public sector"
• **Tu quoque** ("you too"): "You tell me not to smoke, but you smoke"
The smoker case is a good test. Hypocrisy is a fair criticism of the person, but it doesn't make the advice wrong. Smoking harms the parent and the teenager alike.
### When the person is relevant
Not every comment about a source is a fallacy. In court, a witness who lied before may be less credible. A study funded by a company selling the product deserves a closer look. The difference: these raise questions about **reliability**; they don't by themselves prove the claim false.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fallacy", "items": ["he's rude, so he's wrong", "dismiss the claim"]}, "r": {"t": "Fair point", "items": ["funded by the seller", "check evidence harder"]}}
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "She's biased, so she's wrong", "r": "She may be biased, so check her evidence"}]}
!? A related error, the **genetic fallacy**, judges an idea by its origin: "that idea came from a dictator, so it's bad".
> Judge the argument, not the arguer; then, separately, ask how reliable the source is.

# Straw man
The name evokes a scarecrow: a fake opponent stuffed with straw, easy to knock down. You "win" a fight with someone who isn't there.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["A states a view", "B distorts it", "B attacks the distortion", "B claims victory"]}
### How it's built
• **Exaggeration**: "lower speed limits near schools" becomes "ban cars"
• **Oversimplification**: dropping the conditions and qualifiers
• **Cherry-picking**: quoting the weakest supporter, not the best
• **Weak man**: attacking a real but feeble version while ignoring stronger ones
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Real claim", "items": ["30 km/h near schools", "at certain hours"]}, "r": {"t": "Straw man", "items": ["everyone must walk", "cars are evil"]}}
### Why it's so common
It works on audiences who haven't heard the original view. It's also easy to do by accident: we remember opponents' views in their simplest form. News headlines and short videos make it easier still.
### How to defend yourself
Restate your view calmly: "That's not what I said. I said X, under conditions Y." Ask the other person to put your view in their own words before arguing.
ex: Claim: "We should tax sugary drinks" | Straw man: "So you want to ban treats" | Fair version: "You think a price signal will cut sugar intake" | Now real debate: does it work, and is it fair?
!? Several European countries, Portugal among them since 2017, have introduced taxes on sugary drinks, so this debate is a real one, not just a textbook case.
> Before you refute a view, make sure it's the view the other person actually holds.

# False dilemma
Also called false dichotomy or black-and-white thinking. It frames a choice as having only two options when there are more, so rejecting one seems to force you into the other.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "False dilemma", "items": ["love it or leave it", "with us or against us"]}, "r": {"t": "Reality", "items": ["change it", "partly agree", "stay neutral"]}}
### Why it persuades
Two options feel simple. If one is plainly terrible, the other looks good by contrast, without being examined. Campaigns, adverts and heated debates use it constantly, across the political spectrum.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "agreement (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Fully against", "v": 0.0}, {"l": "Mixed views", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Fully for", "v": 10.0}]}
### Common forms
• Either/or: "Either we cut taxes or the economy collapses"
• Perfect or nothing: "If it doesn't solve everything, it's useless"
• Two causes only: "It's either genetic or it's upbringing" (usually both interact)
### When two options are real
Some choices really are binary: a light switch is on or off; you either take the flight or you don't. The fallacy is not having two options, but claiming there are only two without showing it.
ex: "Either we build the new motorway or the town dies" | Ask: what else is possible? | Rail link, better buses, remote work, upgrade old road | At least four more options to compare
> When someone offers only two choices, ask what the third one is.

# Slippery slope
The argument says: if we allow A, then B will follow, then C, and soon catastrophe Z. The fallacy lies in asserting each step without evidence that it will actually happen.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Allow A", "Then B (why?)", "Then C (why?)", "Disaster Z"]}
### Do the maths
Even if each step is fairly likely, a long chain becomes unlikely, because the probabilities multiply.
ex: 4 steps, each 80% likely | 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 ≈ 0.41 | Whole chain only about 41% likely | With 6 steps: about 26%
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% chance whole chain happens (80% per step)", "items": [{"l": "1 step", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "2 steps", "v": 64.0}, {"l": "4 steps", "v": 41.0}, {"l": "6 steps", "v": 26.0, "hi": true}]}
### When slopes are real
Some slopes do exist. Precedents in law make later steps easier. Small dishonesties can grow into habits. A good slippery-slope argument shows the **mechanism** pushing each step: legal precedent, financial incentive, psychological habit, and gives evidence for it.
• Weak: "If we allow this, anything goes"
• Stronger: "This ruling sets a precedent courts have extended in three similar cases"
### Not just "slopes"
A cousin is the "line-drawing" worry: if we can't say exactly where to stop, we'll never stop. But we draw lines all the time, like the speed limit and the voting age, even when the exact number is partly arbitrary.
!! Every slippery-slope argument is a fallacy => It's only fallacious when the steps are assumed; with evidence for each link, it can be strong
> For a slope to be slippery, someone has to show why each step leads to the next.

# Appeal to popularity
Latin name: **argumentum ad populum**, the appeal to the people. Also called the bandwagon fallacy. It treats how many believe something as proof that it's true.
### Popular and false
• Most educated Europeans once believed the Sun orbits the Earth
• Many people still believe we use only 10% of our brains (we use virtually all of it)
• Many think the Great Wall of China is visible from the Moon with the naked eye (it isn't)
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Popularity says", "items": ["many believe it", "it feels safe"]}, "r": {"t": "Truth needs", "items": ["evidence", "good reasoning"]}}
### Why we fall for it
In the 1950s, psychologist Solomon Asch asked people to match line lengths after actors gave obviously wrong answers. About three quarters of participants went along with the wrong answer at least once. Belonging feels good; standing out feels risky.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of Asch participants (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Conformed at least once", "v": 75.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Never conformed", "v": 25.0}]}
### When numbers do count
Sometimes popularity is evidence. A busy restaurant probably isn't terrible. Independent guesses can be surprisingly accurate: at a 1906 English fair, the median of about 800 guesses of an ox's weight was within about 1% of the real figure. The key is **independence**: crowds are wise when people judge separately, not when they copy each other.
!! If experts agree, that's just popularity too => A consensus of experts who've checked the evidence is good evidence; it's a crowd of specialists judging independently
> How many people believe something tells you little; why they believe it tells you much more.

# Correlation vs causation
When two things move together, it's tempting to conclude one causes the other. But there are several other explanations to rule out first.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "A and B move together", "items": ["A causes B", "B causes A", "C causes both", "Pure coincidence"]}
### The four suspects
• **Confounder**: hot weather drives both ice cream sales and swimming (and so drownings)
• **Reverse causation**: do people get sick because they're in hospital, or go to hospital because they're sick?
• **Coincidence**: with enough data, unrelated trends will line up by chance
• **Real cause**: sometimes A really does cause B, but you need more evidence to show it
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hot weather", "More ice cream", "More swimming", "More drownings"]}
### Post hoc
A close cousin is **post hoc ergo propter hoc**: "after this, therefore because of this". "I wore my lucky scarf and Benfica won." The order of events alone proves nothing.
ex: Towns with more fire engines have more fire damage | Do fire engines cause damage? | Confounder: bigger towns have more fires and more engines | Compare towns of the same size instead
!? The website "Spurious Correlations" shows absurd matches, such as the number of films Nicolas Cage appeared in tracking swimming-pool drownings in the US for several years.
> When two things rise together, look for the hidden third thing before blaming either.

# Steelmanning
The term is a play on "straw man": instead of building a weak version of your opponent, build the strongest one, made of steel. Philosophers call the underlying idea the **principle of charity**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Straw man", "items": ["weakest version", "easy win", "opponent annoyed"]}, "r": {"t": "Steel man", "items": ["strongest version", "real test", "opponent heard"]}}
### Rapoport's rules
Philosopher Daniel Dennett popularised a set of rules, credited to the game theorist Anatol Rapoport, for criticising a view fairly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Restate their view well", "List what you agree on", "Say what you learned", "Only then, rebut"]}
### Why it works
• You might discover they're right, or partly right
• If you refute the strongest version, the weaker ones fall too
• People listen to someone who has clearly listened to them
• It protects you from defeating a view nobody holds
### An old idea
John Stuart Mill argued in *On Liberty* (1859) that someone who knows only their own side of a question hardly knows even that. Ancient Indian and medieval Islamic debate traditions required stating the opponent's position (**pūrvapakṣa** in Sanskrit) before responding.
ex: View: "We should raise the minimum wage" | Your steel man of opposition: "It may cut hiring for the least experienced workers" | Their steel man of yours: "It lifts pay for millions with modest job effects" | Now discuss the evidence
> Argue with the best version of a view, or you haven't really argued with it at all.

== ph-political

# The state of nature
Social contract philosophers asked you to imagine humans living with no government, laws or police. This wasn't meant as history. It was a thought experiment: by picturing life without the state, we can ask what the state is for and when it is legitimate.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hobbes", "items": ["fear and conflict", "life without safety"]}, "r": {"t": "Locke", "items": ["mostly peaceful", "but no fair judge"]}}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hobbes (1651)", "b": "war of all against all"}, {"a": "Locke (1689)", "b": "free but insecure"}, {"a": "Rousseau (1755)", "b": "simple, peaceful, unequal later"}]}
### The social contract
The common move: free, equal individuals agree to give up some freedom to a government in exchange for something better, such as security, rights or a common good. Legitimate power rests on the consent of the governed, not on divine right or conquest.
### The objections
• **Hume** (1748) noted no one actually signed. Saying you consent by staying is like saying a sailor, carried aboard asleep, consents to the captain because he doesn't jump overboard
• **Historians and anthropologists** note humans always lived in groups with rules, never as isolated individuals
• **Feminist critics** such as Carole Pateman (1988) argued the classic contracts assumed men as the parties and left women's subordination unexamined
!? The idea of consent behind rulers echoes in many modern constitutions, including Portugal's, which says sovereignty "resides in the people".
> Imagining life without government is a way of asking what government owes us.

# Hobbes' Leviathan
Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) lived through the English Civil War (1642–1651), in which perhaps a few per cent of the population died. *Leviathan*, published in 1651, is a book shaped by that terror of collapse.
### The war of all against all
Without a common power to keep everyone in awe, Hobbes argued, people roughly equal in strength, competing for scarce goods and fearful of each other, would strike first. No farming, no trade, no arts, and life would be solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Equality and scarcity", "Fear and distrust", "Constant conflict", "Agree to a sovereign"]}
### The sovereign
To escape, everyone transfers their right of self-rule to one sovereign power, a person or assembly, with near-absolute authority. Rebellion is almost never allowed, because any return to chaos is worse than a bad ruler. But Hobbes kept one exception: you may resist if the sovereign tries to kill you.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "We give up", "items": ["right to rule ourselves", "most resistance"]}, "r": {"t": "We get", "items": ["peace and security", "law and contracts"]}}
### Objections
John Locke mocked the idea that people would escape the danger of foxes by giving all power to a lion. If the sovereign is unchecked, who protects you from the sovereign? Hobbes's defenders reply that he showed why order matters and why failed states are so deadly.
!? The book's famous frontispiece shows a giant crowned figure whose body is made of hundreds of tiny people: the state as an artificial person made of its citizens.
> Hobbes's fear was simple: without someone in charge, everyone loses.

# Locke and rights
John Locke (1632–1704) published *Two Treatises of Government* anonymously in 1689, just after England's Glorious Revolution replaced King James II with William and Mary.
### Natural rights
Even in a state of nature, Locke said, there's a moral law: no one should harm another's life, health, liberty or possessions. People form governments to protect these rights better, with impartial judges and laws.
### Property through labour
When you mix your labour with something unowned, like clearing and farming land, it becomes yours, provided you leave "enough and as good" for others and don't let it spoil.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["People have rights", "Form a government", "It protects rights", "If it violates them, replace it"]}
### The right to revolution
A government that persistently attacks the rights it exists to protect breaks the trust. The people may then resist and replace it.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1689", "l": "Two Treatises"}, {"y": "1776", "l": "US Declaration"}, {"y": "1789", "l": "French Declaration"}, {"y": "1948", "l": "UN Declaration"}]}
### Critics
• Locke invested in the Royal African Company, which traded enslaved people, and helped draft a colonial constitution for Carolina that accepted slavery
• His labour theory of property was used to justify taking land from Indigenous peoples who didn't farm in European ways
• Socialist critics argue property rights can entrench inequality; Locke's defenders reply that his conditions on property ("enough and as good") limit that
!? The US Declaration of Independence (1776) echoes Locke with "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness", swapping his "property" for happiness.
> For Locke, governments exist to protect rights, and lose their claim when they destroy them.

# Rousseau's general will
Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) was born in Geneva, a small republic he admired. His opening line meant that people born free end up bound by unjust social arrangements. His question: what kind of chains, if any, could be legitimate?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Will of all", "items": ["sum of private wants", "what each wants for self"]}, "r": {"t": "General will", "items": ["common good", "what we want as citizens"]}}
### The idea
In the right social contract, each person gives themselves wholly to the community, and so obeys only laws they help make. Freedom becomes self-government: obeying a law you've given yourself.
### Small, direct, equal
Rousseau thought this works best in small republics where citizens vote on laws themselves. He distrusted representatives and large inequalities of wealth, which let some buy others.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1755", "l": "Discourse on Inequality"}, {"y": "1762", "l": "Social Contract, Emile"}, {"y": "1762", "l": "Books burned in Geneva"}, {"y": "1789", "l": "French Revolution"}]}
### The controversy
Rousseau wrote that anyone refusing to obey the general will could be "forced to be free". Twentieth-century critics such as Isaiah Berlin feared this could license tyranny in the name of the people. Defenders reply that Rousseau meant equal laws apply to all, and that his deep commitment was to equality and self-rule.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Admirers", "b": "popular sovereignty, equality"}, {"a": "Critics", "b": "risk of tyranny of majority"}]}
!? Rousseau was also a composer: his opera *Le devin du village* (1752) was performed at the French court and was a popular success.
> Rousseau's dream: a society where obeying the law means obeying yourself.

# Separation of powers
Charles de Montesquieu (1689–1755), a French aristocrat and judge, published *The Spirit of the Laws* in 1748. Inspired partly by England's constitution, he warned that when one person or body makes, enforces and judges the laws, there is no liberty.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "State power", "items": ["Legislative: makes laws", "Executive: applies laws", "Judicial: judges cases"]}
### Checks and balances
Separation isn't just division. Each branch must be able to stop the others from overreaching. Power, Montesquieu said, must check power.
### Portugal's version
The 1976 Constitution names four organs of sovereignty that are separate but interdependent:
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "President", "b": "elected; can veto laws"}, {"a": "Assembly", "b": "230 deputies make laws"}, {"a": "Government", "b": "runs the country"}, {"a": "Courts", "b": "independent judges"}]}
ex: Parliament passes a law | President vetoes it | Assembly can confirm it by absolute majority (more for some laws) | Constitutional Court can strike down unconstitutional laws
### Variations and debates
In the US the president is separate from Congress; in parliamentary systems the government comes from parliament, so executive and legislature overlap. Critics note separation can cause gridlock. Supporters reply that slower, checked power is safer than fast, unchecked power.
!? The EU follows a related logic: the Commission proposes laws, the Parliament and Council adopt them, and the Court of Justice in Luxembourg rules on disputes.
> Liberty survives when no single hand holds all the power.

# Mill's harm principle
John Stuart Mill published *On Liberty* in 1859, developed with his wife, Harriet Taylor Mill. Its central idea: over their own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Can restrict", "items": ["harming others", "violence, fraud"]}, "r": {"t": "Can't restrict", "items": ["harm to self only", "offence, disapproval"]}}
### What it allows
You may argue with, persuade or warn someone about risky choices. But the state may not force them for their own good. Mill also defended free speech strongly: silencing an opinion robs everyone of the chance to test it.
### What it doesn't cover
• Children and people unable to make informed decisions
• Actions harming others, even indirectly
• Mill also excluded "backward" societies, a colonial attitude now widely criticised
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Seatbelt laws", "b": "self-harm or costs to others?"}, {"a": "Smoking bans indoors", "b": "protect bystanders"}, {"a": "Drug policy", "b": "liberty vs harm debate"}]}
### Hard questions
What counts as harm? Does offending someone count? Joel Feinberg added an "offence principle" for some cases. What about costs to public health systems, or to families? **Paternalists** argue some restrictions, like seatbelt laws, protect people at little cost to liberty; Mill's followers worry about where that ends.
!? Portugal decriminalised possession of small amounts of all drugs for personal use in 2001, treating it as a health and administrative matter. It's widely studied abroad, and still debated at home.
> Your freedom ends where harm to others begins, but defining "harm" is the hard part.

# Liberalism and socialism
These are two broad families of political thought from the last 300 years, each with many branches. Both grew out of the Enlightenment and both claim to serve freedom, but they disagree about what threatens it most.

### Key figures
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1689", "l": "Locke: rights"}, {"y": "1776", "l": "Smith: markets"}, {"y": "1848", "l": "Marx & Engels"}, {"y": "1942", "l": "Beveridge welfare plan"}]}
### Each family's critique of the other
• Liberals warn that concentrated state power and central planning threaten freedom and efficiency
• Socialists warn that concentrated wealth makes formal freedoms hollow for the poor
• Both have internal variety: classical vs social liberals, revolutionary vs democratic socialists
### The mixed reality
Most European democracies combine private markets with public health care, education, pensions and labour protections. Debates are often about the right mix, not one or the other. Portugal's national health service (SNS, 1979) and its market economy within the EU are typical.
!? The preamble of Portugal's 1976 Constitution still speaks of opening a path towards a socialist society, while later revisions, especially in 1989, removed the rule that nationalisations were irreversible.
!? "Left" and "right" come from the French National Assembly of 1789, where supporters of the king sat on the right and revolutionaries on the left.
> Most politics today is less "markets or state" than "how much of each, and where".

# Conservatism
Edmund Burke (1729–1797) was an Irish-born member of the British Parliament. In *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), written before the Terror, he warned that tearing down institutions in the name of abstract reason would end in chaos and tyranny.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tradition", "b": "stored wisdom of generations"}, {"a": "Prudence", "b": "reform carefully, not wholesale"}, {"a": "Institutions", "b": "family, church, local life"}, {"a": "Partnership", "b": "the dead, living and unborn"}]}
### Why change slowly?
Societies are complex. No single generation fully understands why its customs exist. Radical reformers may destroy useful structures they don't understand.
### Chesterton's fence
G. K. Chesterton (1929) offered a parable: a reformer finds a fence across a road and wants it removed because he sees no use for it. The wiser person says: first find out why it was put there, then you may remove it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Find an old rule", "Ask why it exists", "Understand its purpose", "Then decide to keep or change"]}
### Not opposed to all change
Burke supported American colonists' grievances and attacked abuses by the East India Company. He believed a state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation.
### Objections
Critics, starting with Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man* (1791), argued that tradition can preserve injustice, such as slavery or excluding women, and that past generations have no right to bind the living. Conservatives reply that their aim is reform that keeps what works.
> Before changing what you inherited, understand why it was there.

# Democracy's critics
Plato watched Athens' democracy sentence his teacher Socrates to death. In the *Republic* he compared the state to a ship whose crew elects whoever flatters them best, not the navigator who studies stars and winds.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plato's worry", "items": ["persuasion beats skill", "voters lack expertise", "can slide to tyranny"]}, "r": {"t": "Democrats reply", "items": ["experts are biased too", "removes bad rulers", "protects interests"]}}
### Modern versions
• Economist Anthony Downs (1957) argued "rational ignorance": one vote rarely decides anything, so it's not worth studying policy
• Philosopher Jason Brennan (2016) proposed "epistocracy", giving extra weight to informed voters
• Others worry about majorities overriding minorities, which is why constitutions protect rights
### Defences
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Peaceful removal of leaders", "Wisdom of many voters", "Equal dignity of citizens", "Sen: no famines with free press"]}
Economist Amartya Sen observed that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, because governments that face elections and criticism must respond.
### Churchill's line
In a 1947 speech to the House of Commons, Winston Churchill said "it has been said" that democracy is the worst form of government except for all those others that have been tried. He presented it as a saying already in circulation.
!? Churchill's famous line was not even his own idea; he was quoting an existing quip.
> Democracy's case isn't that it's perfect, but that it corrects its mistakes without bloodshed.

# Portugal's democracy
The military seized power in 1926. From 1932 António de Oliveira Salazar led the **Estado Novo**, a dictatorship with censorship, a political police (PIDE) and a single party. Marcelo Caetano succeeded him in 1968. From 1961, colonial wars in Angola, Guinea-Bissau and Mozambique drained young lives and money.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1926", "l": "Military coup"}, {"y": "1933", "l": "Estado Novo constitution"}, {"y": "1974", "l": "25 April revolution"}, {"y": "1976", "l": "New constitution"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "Joins EEC"}]}
### The revolution
Junior officers of the Armed Forces Movement (MFA) launched the coup. Radio broadcasts of songs served as signals; "Grândola, Vila Morena" by Zeca Afonso gave the final go-ahead after midnight. Crowds poured into the streets, and soldiers placed red carnations in their rifles. Very few people died.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "48", "l": "years of dictatorship"}, {"v": "≈92%", "l": "turnout, 1975 election"}]}
### Building democracy
On 25 April 1975, Portuguese voted for a Constituent Assembly, with turnout above 90%. The constitution was approved on 2 April 1976. The road was turbulent: the 1975 "hot summer" saw fierce struggles between different visions of the revolution. The former colonies became independent in 1974–75.
### Philosophical threads
Many of this lesson's ideas appear in the 1976 constitution: popular sovereignty (Rousseau), fundamental rights (Locke), separated powers (Montesquieu), and a commitment to social rights that reflects socialist traditions.
!? The revolution's symbol came by chance: a restaurant worker, Celeste Caeiro, handed carnations to soldiers in Lisbon, and they put them in their gun barrels.
> 25 April turned centuries of political ideas into the everyday freedoms Portugal lives with today.

# The question
In 1814 French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace imagined an intellect that knew the position and motion of every particle in the universe. With enough calculating power, he said, it could predict the future perfectly. Philosophers call this figure **Laplace's demon**, and it sharpens the free will problem.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Past events", "Laws of nature", "Brain states", "Your 'choice'"]}
### Two things we might mean by "free"
• **Leeway**: you could have done otherwise, with the past exactly as it was
• **Sourcehood**: your action really comes from you, your reasons and character
Much of the debate is about which of these matters, and whether either survives if causes go all the way back.
### The main positions
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of philosophers, PhilPapers 2020", "items": [{"l": "Compatibilism", "v": 59.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Libertarianism", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "No free will", "v": 11.0}, {"l": "Other", "v": 11.0}]}
These figures come from a 2020 survey of about 1,800 professional philosophers. Popularity doesn't settle a question, but it shows where expert opinion leans.
### Why it matters
The answer touches law, punishment, praise, regret and how we see ourselves. If nobody could ever have acted otherwise, is it fair to punish anyone, or to feel proud of anything?
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Is free will real?", "items": ["Yes, needs indeterminism", "Yes, fits with causes", "No, an illusion"]}
> The free will debate asks whether being caused and being free can both be true of the same choice.
!? Laplace's demon later met trouble from quantum physics and chaos theory, but the philosophical puzzle survives either way.

# Determinism
**Causal determinism** is the thesis that the state of the world at one time, together with the laws of nature, fixes exactly what happens at every later time. Rerun history from the same starting point and you get the same result, down to every thought.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["State of the world", "Laws of nature apply", "Next state fixed", "Repeat forever"]}
### Not the same as fatalism
Fatalism says certain outcomes will happen whatever you do. Determinism says what you do is part of the causal chain. Your effort still matters: it's one of the causes, even if it was itself caused.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fatalism", "items": ["outcome fixed anyway", "effort pointless"]}, "r": {"t": "Determinism", "items": ["effort is a cause", "outcome depends on it"]}}
### Is it true?
Classical, Newtonian physics looked deterministic. Quantum mechanics, in its standard interpretation, includes genuine chance. But some interpretations, such as the many-worlds view, are deterministic, so physics hasn't settled it. For the brain, many scientists think quantum effects average out at the scale of neurons.
### Hard determinism
**Hard determinists**, such as the 18th-century philosopher Baron d'Holbach, accepted determinism and concluded free will is an illusion. Spinoza thought people believe they are free only because they are aware of their desires but ignorant of their causes.
### The strongest worry
If determinism is true, every choice you make was settled before you were born. Critics of hard determinism reply that this proves too much: it would also make reasoning and deliberation pointless, yet we can't stop doing them.
> Determinism says the past plus the laws fix the future; it doesn't say your choices make no difference.
!! Determinism means nothing you do matters => Your choices are links in the causal chain, so they still change what happens

# Libertarian free will
In philosophy, "libertarian" here has nothing to do with politics. It names the view that we are free, determinism is false, and freedom requires that at least some choices are not fixed in advance.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Agent causation", "b": "you, not events, cause the act"}, {"a": "Event-causal", "b": "some brain events are undetermined"}, {"a": "Non-causal", "b": "choices need no cause at all"}]}
### Main versions
• **Agent causation** (Thomas Reid, 18th century; Roderick Chisholm, 20th): the person, as a substance, starts new causal chains
• **Self-forming actions** (Robert Kane): in hard, torn decisions, such as duty versus desire, the brain is genuinely undetermined, and whichever way you go, you make yourself through the effort
• Immanuel Kant argued we must think of ourselves as free whenever we act, even though science sees the world as determined
### The strongest objection: luck
If a choice isn't determined by your prior reasons, desires and character, what decides it? Peter van Inwagen's **rollback argument** imagines rewinding a choice 1,000 times.
ex: Rewind the moment of choice 1,000 times | Libertarian: outcomes vary, e.g. 700 lie, 300 tell the truth | Which happens this time seems a matter of chance | Is that really control?
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Undetermined means free", "r": "Undetermined may just mean random"}]}
### Replies
Kane says the agent's effort is involved either way, so both outcomes are your doing. Agent-causalists say the agent settles it, not chance. Critics answer that agent causation is mysterious: how does a person cause something without any event in them causing it?
> Libertarians protect the idea that you could have done otherwise, but must explain how an undetermined choice is still under your control.
!? Kant called compatibilism a "wretched subterfuge", yet he too struggled to explain how freedom fits a law-governed world.

# Compatibilism
Compatibilists hold that free will and determinism can both be true. Thomas Hobbes and David Hume defended versions of it centuries ago. Hume distinguished freedom as acting on your own will from the impossible freedom of acting without any cause.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Free act", "items": ["your own reasons", "no coercion", "no manipulation"]}, "r": {"t": "Unfree act", "items": ["gun to your head", "compulsion, addiction", "brainwashing"]}}
### Modern versions
• **Hierarchical**: Harry Frankfurt (1971) said you're free when you act on desires you endorse; a willing addict differs from an unwilling one
• **Reasons-responsiveness**: John Fischer and Mark Ravizza (1998) say you're free if your decision process would respond to good reasons to act differently
• **Free will worth wanting**: Daniel Dennett argues this is the only kind of freedom anyone needs
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Act: lift the cup", "Choice: I want tea", "Reasons: thirsty, like tea", "Causes: biology, upbringing"]}
### The consequence argument
The best-known objection, by Peter van Inwagen (1983): if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the laws and the distant past. We have no power over either. So we have no power over our acts.
### Manipulation arguments
Derk Pereboom imagines a man whose brain is programmed by scientists to reason his way to a crime. He meets every compatibilist test, yet seems unfree. Pereboom argues ordinary determinism is no different in principle. Compatibilists reply that being shaped by nature and upbringing differs from being designed by someone else, or bite the bullet.
> For compatibilists, freedom is not the absence of causes but the presence of the right kind: your own reasons, working without coercion.
!! Compatibilism just redefines freedom to dodge the problem => It argues the everyday meaning of "free" was never about lacking causes

# Libet's experiment
In 1983 American neuroscientist Benjamin Libet asked volunteers to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it. They noted the position of a fast clock spot when they first felt the urge. Meanwhile, scalp electrodes recorded a slow build-up called the **readiness potential**.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "−550 ms", "l": "Brain activity rises"}, {"y": "−200 ms", "l": "Felt urge to move"}, {"y": "0 ms", "l": "Wrist moves"}]}
### The striking claim
Brain activity began roughly a third of a second before people reported deciding. Some concluded the brain "decides" before we're aware of it. Libet himself kept room for a conscious veto, sometimes called "free won't".
### The criticisms
• **Trivial choices**: flicking a wrist at random isn't like choosing a career
• **Timing reports**: judging the clock position of an inner feeling is unreliable by tens of milliseconds
• **What the signal means**: in 2012 Aaron Schurger and colleagues proposed the readiness potential reflects random neural noise building up to a threshold, not a decision already made
• **Meaningful choices**: a 2019 study found the readiness potential was largely absent when people made deliberate choices, such as which charity gets money
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bold reading", "items": ["brain decides first", "consciousness late"]}, "r": {"t": "Cautious reading", "items": ["general readiness", "noise near threshold"]}}
### Later studies
In 2008 fMRI studies predicted simple left-or-right button choices up to several seconds early, but only about 60% of the time, little better than a coin's 50%.
> Libet showed brain activity precedes reported decisions; whether that shows free will is an illusion is far from established.
!! Neuroscience has proven free will is an illusion => The experiments involve trivial choices and their interpretation is disputed

# Quantum randomness?
Quantum mechanics, the physics of the very small, says some events, such as when a radioactive atom decays, can't be predicted even in principle under the standard interpretation. That seems to break Laplace's clockwork. Does it make room for free will?
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Your choice was…", "items": ["Determined: not up to you?", "Random: not up to you?", "Something else?"]}
### The randomness dilemma
Either a choice is determined by prior causes, or it isn't. If it isn't, it seems partly random. A random event in your brain doesn't sound like you choosing: it sounds like a twitch. So adding chance doesn't obviously add control.
ex: Decision between apple and cake | A quantum event tips it to cake | You didn't choose that event | More randomness, not more freedom
### Does it reach the brain?
• Neurons are large, warm and wet, so quantum effects are generally thought to wash out quickly
• Physicist Roger Penrose and anaesthetist Stuart Hameroff proposed quantum processes in brain microtubules; most neuroscientists remain unconvinced
• Even if quantum events matter in the brain, the dilemma above still applies
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hope", "items": ["breaks determinism", "leaves options open"]}, "r": {"t": "Problem", "items": ["chance isn't control", "may not reach neurons"]}}
### A libertarian reply
Robert Kane accepts that indeterminism alone isn't freedom. He argues it can play a limited role: it keeps a torn decision open while your own effort and reasons settle it. Critics reply this still leaves the final tip to chance.
> Quantum chance may break determinism, but chance alone gives us dice, not decisions.
!? Many physicists use "random" carefully: the standard interpretation says quantum outcomes are truly chancy, but rival interpretations disagree, and experiments can't yet decide between them.

# Moral responsibility
In a famous 1962 essay, "Freedom and Resentment", P. F. Strawson argued that blame and praise are rooted in **reactive attitudes**: resentment, gratitude, indignation, love. These are part of human relationships, he said, and no theory about determinism could or should make us drop them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Backward-looking", "items": ["deserved blame", "punish because guilty"]}, "r": {"t": "Forward-looking", "items": ["guide behaviour", "protect others"]}}
### The sceptical challenge
Philosopher Galen Strawson (P. F.'s son) offers a "basic argument": to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for how you are. But you didn't create your starting self, so ultimate responsibility is impossible.
### Life without basic desert
**Free will sceptics** like Derk Pereboom argue we should drop the idea that wrongdoers deserve to suffer. But we can still:
• Hold people to account and ask them to explain
• Protect society, as we quarantine someone with a dangerous disease
• Aim at rehabilitation and moral growth
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Protect the public", "Prevent future harm", "Rehabilitate", "Repair relationships"]}
### Objections
Critics say dropping desert could permit harsh "preventive" measures against the innocent, or empty gratitude and love of meaning. Others argue our practices already mix both kinds of reasons.
### What about behaviour?
A 2008 study found people who read text denying free will cheated more on a test. A large 2015 replication project failed to reproduce it, so claims that disbelief in free will makes people behave badly are not well supported.
> Even without ultimate freedom, holding each other responsible can make sense as a way to guide behaviour and protect people.
!! If there's no free will, nobody can be held responsible => Many sceptics keep accountability; they reject only the idea of deserved suffering

# Frankfurt's cases
In 1969 American philosopher Harry Frankfurt challenged a principle most people took for granted: the **principle of alternate possibilities**. It says you're morally responsible for an act only if you could have done otherwise.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Black wants Jones to act", "Implants a device", "Jones decides alone", "Device never used"]}
### The story
Black wants Jones to commit a crime. He secretly installs a device that will force Jones's decision if Jones starts to waver. But Jones decides on his own, for his own reasons. The device does nothing. Jones couldn't have done otherwise, yet he seems fully responsible.
ex: Jones's reasons lead him to act | Black's device stays idle | Alternative: impossible, device would force it | Verdict: most say Jones is responsible
### Why it matters
If Frankfurt is right, what matters for responsibility is the actual history of your choice, not unused alternatives. That helps compatibilists: even if determinism removes alternatives, responsibility can survive.
### The strongest reply
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Frankfurt", "items": ["no alternatives", "still responsible"]}, "r": {"t": "Critics", "items": ["a 'flicker' remains", "or begs the question"]}}
• **Flicker of freedom**: Black's device needs a sign, such as a twitch, that Jones is wavering; Jones could still have shown that sign
• **Dilemma defence** (David Widerker, Robert Kane): if Jones's choice is undetermined, Black can't know in time; if it's determined, the case assumes what it needs to prove
Defenders have built ever more elaborate versions to answer these replies; the debate continues.
> Frankfurt's cases suggest responsibility depends on how you actually chose, not on whether you could have chosen otherwise.
!? Frankfurt's 1969 paper is one of the most cited articles in modern philosophy of action; he later became widely known for a short book called On Bullshit.

# Feeling free
Whatever the metaphysics, the experience of choosing is vivid. You weigh options, feel torn, settle on one and feel the act is yours. Philosophers ask what this experience shows.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice options", "Weigh reasons", "Feel torn", "Settle and act"]}
### The illusion view
Psychologist Daniel Wegner argued in 2002 that the feeling of willing an act is the mind's inference, not a direct view of causes. In his experiments, people sometimes felt they had caused movements that someone else had actually made, when a thought about it came just before.
### The necessity view
Kant argued that when we deliberate we can't help regarding ourselves as free. Even a convinced determinist, deciding what to cook, has to weigh options as if the outcome is open. Jean-Paul Sartre went further: we are "condemned to be free" and can't escape responsibility for our choices.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Feeling as evidence", "items": ["deliberation is real", "choice is ours"]}, "r": {"t": "Feeling as illusion", "items": ["mind explains itself", "causes hidden"]}}
### A middle path
Compatibilists say the feeling isn't misleading about everything. Deliberation really happens, and it really causes what you do. What may be mistaken is the extra sense that nothing at all determined it.
• The feeling of choice can be wrong in detail, as Wegner shows
• Deliberation still shapes action, as everyday life shows
> We may be wrong about where choices come from, but deliberating is still the way creatures like us decide.
!! If the feeling of freedom can be fooled, freedom is fake => An unreliable feeling shows our self-knowledge is limited, not that deliberation does nothing

# Practical freedom
In Homer's Odyssey, Ulysses wants to hear the Sirens' song without wrecking his ship. He has his crew tie him to the mast and plug their own ears. He used a free choice now to limit his future self. Philosophers call this **precommitment**.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Choose environment", "Environment shapes habits", "Habits shape choices", "Choices reshape you"]}
### Shaping future causes
Whatever your view of free will, choices today become causes tomorrow. Aristotle argued we become brave by doing brave acts, so we're partly responsible for the character we end up with.
### Tools that work
• **Implementation intentions**: "if it's 7 a.m., I put on running shoes". Studies find these if-then plans help people follow through
• **Choice architecture**: put fruit, not biscuits, on the counter
• **Social circles**: habits like smoking and exercise tend to be shared within friend groups
• **Friction**: make bad options a little harder, good ones a little easier
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Phone in another room", "b": "less distraction"}, {"a": "Gym bag by the door", "b": "easier to train"}, {"a": "Savings auto-transfer", "b": "money saved by default"}]}
ex: Goal: study 30 min a day | If-then plan: after dinner, open book | Remove cue: phone in kitchen | Result: fewer daily decisions needed
### A philosophical point
Even a hard determinist can endorse this. Learning about precommitment is itself a cause that changes what you do next. Free will debates are about ultimate origins; practical freedom is about what's within reach now.
> You may not choose your causes from scratch, but you can choose actions that become better causes.
!? Ulysses-style contracts are used today, for example in self-exclusion schemes that let gamblers ban themselves from casinos, including in Portugal.

# Meaning given or made?
Philosophers distinguish two questions. The **meaning of life**: does human existence as a whole have a point? And **meaning in life**: what makes a particular life meaningful? Different answers to the first shape answers to the second.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Where does meaning come from?", "items": ["Given: God or nature", "Made: we choose it", "Found: real values"]}
### Three broad views
• **Supernaturalism**: meaning comes from God or a soul's relation to a higher order
• **Subjectivism**: a life is meaningful if you care about it, whatever "it" is
• **Objectivism**: some things, like love, knowledge and creativity, make lives meaningful whether or not you feel it
### Existentialism
In a 1945 Paris lecture, Jean-Paul Sartre said "existence precedes essence". Unlike a paper knife, made for a purpose, humans exist first and define themselves through choices. There is no given human nature to follow.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Given", "items": ["purpose from outside", "discovered"]}, "r": {"t": "Made", "items": ["purpose chosen", "created by acts"]}}
### Objections on each side
Against pure subjectivism: Richard Taylor imagined Sisyphus drugged to love rolling his stone; he feels fulfilled, yet many feel his life still lacks meaning. Against "given" meaning: Kurt Baier argued that being made for someone else's purpose, like a tool, might lower rather than raise our dignity.
### A hybrid
Philosopher Susan Wolf argues meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness: when you are actively engaged with something genuinely worthwhile.
> Meaning may be given, made or found; many philosophers think it needs both caring and caring about something worth it.
!? Sartre's "paper knife" example was chosen precisely because it has a fixed purpose before it exists, which is what he denied of humans.

# Religious answers
For most of history and for most people today, the question of meaning has been answered within a religion. Answers vary a lot, but many share a structure: a problem with the human condition, and a path to its solution.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Christianity", "b": "love God and neighbour"}, {"a": "Islam", "b": "worship and serve God"}, {"a": "Hinduism", "b": "dharma, then liberation"}, {"a": "Buddhism", "b": "end suffering: nirvana"}]}
### Some details
• **Judaism**: a covenant with God, lived through commandments, and the idea of repairing the world
• **Hinduism**: four aims of life, duty, prosperity, pleasure and finally liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth
• **Buddhism**: craving causes suffering; the Eightfold Path leads to its end
• **Christianity**: a relationship with God, offered through Christ, and eternal life
In Portugal, about 80% of people who answered the 2021 census religion question identified as Catholic, and places like Fátima draw millions of pilgrims a year.
### Strengths
Religious answers offer meaning that is objective, permanent and shared. Suffering can fit into a larger story. Communities give rituals and support across a lifetime.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strengths", "items": ["lasting purpose", "community", "answers suffering"]}, "r": {"t": "Challenges", "items": ["is it true?", "tool objection", "rival faiths"]}}
### Objections
• They depend on beliefs, about God or rebirth, that many doubt
• Plato's old Euthyphro puzzle can be adapted: does God's purpose make something meaningful, or does God choose it because it's meaningful already?
• Being created for a purpose may not by itself make that purpose ours
> Religions usually locate meaning in a relationship with something greater than ourselves, which gives it permanence but rests on faith.
!! Religious people never struggle with meaning => Many traditions describe doubt and "dark nights" as part of the spiritual path

# Aristotle again
Aristotle (384–322 BC) didn't ask about cosmic meaning. He asked what a good human life is. His answer was **eudaimonia**, often translated as happiness but better as flourishing: living and doing well over a whole life.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Humans have capacities", "Reason is distinctive", "Use it well: virtue", "Over a lifetime: flourish"]}
### The function argument
A knife's good is cutting well; a flute player's good is playing well. Aristotle argued humans have a characteristic function too: living according to reason. A good life is one where we exercise that function excellently, through virtues such as courage, justice, honesty and practical wisdom.
### Needs and community
• Some external goods help: health, enough money, friends
• Humans are "political animals": we flourish in a community, the city
• Friendship, especially friendship based on shared goodness, is central
• Virtue is formed by habit: we become just by doing just acts
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Flourishing life", "Virtuous activity", "Good habits and friends", "Basic goods: health, means"]}
### Objections
Critics doubt that humans have a "function" at all; that may assume a purpose-driven nature science doesn't support. Aristotle's view also excluded many people, accepting slavery and denying women full participation. And he ranked philosophical contemplation as the highest life, which looks like a philosopher's bias.
### Modern echoes
Martha Nussbaum's capabilities approach, used in thinking about human development, draws on Aristotle. Positive psychology distinguishes pleasure-based wellbeing from flourishing-based wellbeing.
> For Aristotle, meaning isn't found by asking about the cosmos but by living well: using your abilities fully, with good character, among others.
!? Aristotle thought you couldn't call a life happy until it was over, because fortune could still ruin it, echoing a saying attributed to Solon.

# Nihilism
The word was popularised by Ivan Turgenev's 1862 novel Fathers and Sons, about a young man who rejects all authority. Philosophically, **existential nihilism** holds that life has no inherent meaning, purpose or value.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Existential", "b": "life has no inherent meaning"}, {"a": "Moral", "b": "nothing is really right or wrong"}, {"a": "Passive", "b": "despair, resignation"}, {"a": "Active", "b": "clear away, rebuild values"}]}
### Nietzsche's diagnosis
Friedrich Nietzsche wasn't recommending nihilism; he predicted it. In The Gay Science (1882) a madman announces "God is dead": belief in God was fading in Europe, and with it the foundation of its values. Nietzsche feared a slide into empty comfort-seeking.
### Nietzsche's response
• **Revaluation of values**: create new values that affirm life
• **Amor fati**: love your fate, everything about your life
• **Eternal recurrence**: could you will to live this exact life again forever?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Old values collapse", "Nihilism: nothing matters", "Despair or courage", "Create new values"]}
### The strongest objections
If nothing has value, why is creating values better than despair? Self-made values seem arbitrary. Thomas Nagel (1971) adds a twist: from a cosmic viewpoint nothing seems to matter, but that viewpoint can't show that things don't matter either. We can step back, see our lives as small, and still care.
> Nihilism says meaning isn't built into the universe; the real question is what follows from that.
!! Nietzsche was a nihilist => He diagnosed nihilism as a danger and spent his work trying to overcome it

# Camus' answer
Albert Camus, born in French Algeria in 1913, published The Myth of Sisyphus in 1942. He began by asking whether life is worth living at all, which he saw as the one truly serious philosophical question.
### The absurd
For Camus, the **absurd** is not the world itself. It's the clash between our longing for meaning and clarity and a universe that stays silent.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Facing the absurd", "items": ["Suicide: rejected", "Leap of faith: evasion", "Revolt: live fully"]}
### Three responses
• **Physical suicide**: gives in to the absurd rather than facing it
• **Philosophical suicide**: a leap into religion or a system that explains everything away; Camus saw this as evasion
• **Revolt**: keep the absurd in view and live with intensity, freedom and passion
### Sisyphus
In Greek myth, Sisyphus is condemned to roll a boulder up a hill forever, only to see it roll down. Camus focuses on the walk back down: Sisyphus knows his fate fully and doesn't bow to it. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Push boulder up", "It rolls down", "Walk down, aware", "Push again, defiant"]}
### Objections
Critics ask why revolt is better than faith or despair: if the universe offers no values, Camus seems to smuggle some in, such as honesty and passion. Nagel thought defiance was overdramatic, and recommended irony instead. Others say calling the universe "silent" assumes it should have spoken.
> Camus' answer to a meaningless universe is not despair but lucid, defiant engagement with life.
!? Camus won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1957, aged 44, one of the youngest ever; he died in a car accident three years later.

# Frankl's search
Viktor Frankl (1905–1997) was a Viennese psychiatrist. In 1942 he was deported with his family. His wife, mother, father and brother died in the camps. He survived Theresienstadt, Auschwitz and Dachau subcamps, and in 1946 wrote the book known in English as Man's Search for Meaning.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1942", "l": "Deported with family"}, {"y": "1945", "l": "Liberated"}, {"y": "1946", "l": "Writes his book"}, {"y": "1997", "l": "Dies in Vienna"}]}
### Three sources of meaning
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Creating: work or deeds", "Experiencing: love, beauty", "Attitude to suffering"]}
He observed that prisoners who held on to a reason to live, a loved one, an unfinished book, seemed better able to endure. He often cited Nietzsche's line that someone with a why to live can bear almost any how.
### Logotherapy
Frankl built a school of therapy, **logotherapy**, on the idea that the will to meaning is a basic human drive. Instead of asking what we expect from life, he said, we should ask what life expects from us.
### Cautions
• Survival in the camps mostly depended on luck, timing and health; Frankl himself said the best did not return
• Historians note he spent only days in Auschwitz itself; most of his time was in other camps
• Studies link a sense of purpose to better health and longer life, but these are correlations, not proof of cause
> Frankl argued that even when we can't change our situation, we can choose the attitude we take to it.
!? The book has sold more than ten million copies and has been translated into dozens of languages, including Portuguese, as O Homem em Busca de um Sentido.

# Ikigai
In Japanese, **ikigai** combines iki (life) and kai or gai (worth, effect). It means something that makes life worth living. It can be large, like a vocation, or small, like a morning walk or grandchildren.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "In Japan", "items": ["small joys count", "not about pay", "felt, not planned"]}, "r": {"t": "Western version", "items": ["4 circles", "love, skill, need, pay", "career tool"]}}
### Where the famous diagram came from
The four-circle Venn diagram, combining what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs and what you can be paid for, isn't Japanese. It comes from a Spanish "purpose" diagram from around 2011. A 2014 blog post relabelled it as ikigai, and it then spread widely online.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1966", "l": "Kamiya's ikigai book"}, {"y": "~2011", "l": "Spanish purpose chart"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "Blog labels it ikigai"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Global bestseller"}]}
### In Japanese thought
Psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya wrote a classic 1966 book on ikigai, after working with leprosy patients in isolation. In Japanese surveys, people often name family, hobbies and daily routines, not jobs, as their ikigai.
### The evidence
• A large 2008 Japanese study found people without a sense of ikigai had higher death rates over seven years, even after adjusting for health
• This is correlational: ill or depressed people may lose their ikigai
• Claims that ikigai explains Okinawan longevity are popular but not well established

# Meaning vs happiness
Psychologists separate **hedonic** wellbeing, feeling good and satisfied, from **eudaimonic** wellbeing, purpose and growth. The two usually go together, but not always.
### A well-known study
In 2013 Roy Baumeister and colleagues surveyed about 400 Americans. Meaning and happiness were strongly correlated, but each had distinct predictors once the overlap was removed.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Happiness", "items": ["present-focused", "getting, needs met", "less stress"]}, "r": {"t": "Meaning", "items": ["past, present, future", "giving, helping", "more stress, worry"]}}
• Raising children was linked to more meaning but not more happiness
• Having more money, being healthy, and feeling good were tied more to happiness
• Caution: it was one correlational survey, and its findings have not been widely replicated directly
### A global puzzle
A 2014 analysis of Gallup data from about 130 countries found people in richer countries reported higher life satisfaction, but people in poorer countries were more likely to say their lives had meaning. Higher religiosity in poorer countries explained much of this.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "pattern by country income (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Richer: satisfaction", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Richer: meaning", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Poorer: satisfaction", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Poorer: meaning", "v": 3.0}]}
### Why it matters
If you only chase feeling good, you may avoid the effortful, sometimes stressful projects, such as raising a family or building something, that give life meaning. Philosophers from Aristotle to Mill made similar points.
> Meaning and happiness often travel together, but meaning can involve stress and sacrifice that happiness alone would avoid.
!? John Stuart Mill wrote that happiness is best found by aiming at something else, such as others' happiness or an art, a point psychologists still echo.

# Small meanings
Most people aren't living without meaning. In surveys, the average person rates their life as fairly meaningful. Psychologists Laura King and Samantha Heintzelman summed this up in a 2014 paper titled "Life is pretty meaningful".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Coherence", "b": "my life makes sense"}, {"a": "Purpose", "b": "I have goals and direction"}, {"a": "Significance", "b": "my life matters to others"}]}
### Where people find it
• Family and close relationships, by far the most common answer in surveys across many countries
• Work done well, or meaningful work done at all
• Friends and community, faith, nature, beauty and creativity
• Helping others, even in small ways
### Routine helps
King's lab found that having daily routines was linked to higher meaning in life. Predictability helps life feel coherent, which may explain why upheaval often brings a crisis of meaning.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "One grand purpose", "items": ["big and singular", "fragile if lost"]}, "r": {"t": "Many small meanings", "items": ["spread out", "more robust"]}}
### The strongest objection
Leo Tolstoy, in his Confession (written around 1879–80), had everything: family, fame, wealth and health. Yet he felt it all meaningless, since death would erase it. He asked: what will come of all of it? Small meanings, he felt, were not enough without a meaning that death couldn't destroy. He found his answer in faith.
### A reply
Others answer that things can matter without lasting forever. A good meal, a friendship or a song isn't worthless because it ends.
> For most people, meaning isn't one grand answer but many threads: people, work, beauty and care.
!! A meaningful life requires doing something extraordinary => Most people find meaning in ordinary relationships and daily acts

# A reflective exercise
This exercise asks you to look back from the end. Stephen Covey popularised a version of it in his 1989 book The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, as "begin with the end in mind". Writer David Brooks later contrasted **résumé virtues**, skills for the job market, with **eulogy virtues**, kindness, honesty, courage.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Résumé virtues", "items": ["skills, titles", "achievements"]}, "r": {"t": "Eulogy virtues", "items": ["kindness, honesty", "how you loved"]}}
### How to do it
ex: Imagine your 80th birthday | Pick 3 people: family, friend, colleague | Write what you'd like each to say | Underline the values | Compare with last week's diary
### Why it helps
Looking from far away shrinks daily worries and makes values clearer. It's a cousin of **values clarification** used in acceptance and commitment therapy, where people identify what matters and then take small steps towards it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Imagine the speeches", "Spot the values", "Compare with today", "Pick one small change"]}
### What people tend to regret
• Research by Thomas Gilovich found that over the long term, people regret things they didn't do more than things they did
• Palliative-care nurse Bronnie Ware reported that dying patients often wished they had lived truer to themselves and worked less; these were her observations, not a scientific study
### A caution
The answers can surprise or unsettle you. That's useful: a gap between what you value and how you spend your days is information, not failure.
> Picturing how you'd like to be remembered reveals what you value now, and where to start.
!? In the short term people regret actions more, but over years the balance shifts to regretting inaction.

# The scientific method
There is no single recipe that every scientist follows step by step. But most good science shares a loop: notice something, guess an explanation, work out what the guess predicts, then check whether the world agrees.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Observe", "Form a hypothesis", "Derive a prediction", "Test it", "Revise"]}
### The key move: a risky prediction
A hypothesis earns respect when it predicts something that could easily turn out false. In 1846 the French astronomer Urbain Le Verrier used odd wobbles in Uranus's orbit to calculate where an unseen planet should be. Within days, astronomers in Berlin pointed a telescope there and found Neptune, less than 1° from the predicted spot.
ex: Observation: Uranus drifts off course | Hypothesis: an unknown planet pulls it | Prediction: look at this patch of sky | Test: Neptune found, 23 September 1846
### What makes a test fair
• Decide in advance what result would count against you
• Measure carefully and record everything, not just the hits
• Let others repeat the work with their own equipment
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Weak test", "items": ["prediction set later", "only hits counted"]}, "r": {"t": "Strong test", "items": ["prediction set first", "misses reported"]}}
### When results disagree
A failed prediction doesn't always kill a theory at once. The same orbital trick later suggested a planet "Vulcan" inside Mercury's orbit. It was never found; the anomaly was finally explained by Einstein's general relativity in 1915. So a surprise can mean a missing piece or a wrong framework, and working out which takes time.
!! Science means following one fixed method => Methods vary a lot: astronomers can't run experiments on stars, and field biologists mostly observe
> Science is less a recipe than a habit: say what you expect, then let the world tell you whether you were wrong.

# Bacon's induction
Francis Bacon (1561–1626) was an English lawyer and Lord Chancellor who wanted to reform how knowledge was made. In his *Novum Organum* (1620), "the new instrument", he attacked the habit of starting from ancient authorities, especially Aristotle, and arguing downwards.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old way", "items": ["start from authority", "deduce details"]}, "r": {"t": "Bacon's way", "items": ["collect observations", "build up to laws"]}}
### Tables of evidence
Bacon proposed sorting observations into tables: cases where a feature appears, cases where it is absent, and cases where it varies in degree. His own example was heat. By comparing sunlight, friction and fire with cold cases, he concluded heat is a kind of motion, a remarkably good guess.
### The four idols
Bacon also listed the mental traps that distort observation.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Idols of the tribe", "b": "biases all humans share"}, {"a": "Idols of the cave", "b": "your personal quirks"}, {"a": "Idols of the market", "b": "confusion caused by words"}, {"a": "Idols of the theatre", "b": "loyalty to grand systems"}]}
The "tribe" idols include noticing hits and forgetting misses, which psychologists now call **confirmation bias**.
### The problem of induction
A century later David Hume asked a hard question: why trust that the future will resemble the past? Any argument for it seems to assume what it is trying to prove. Ten thousand white swans don't guarantee the next one; Europeans learnt this in 1697 when black swans were seen in Australia.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Many cases observed", "General law proposed", "One counterexample", "Law must change"]}
!? Bacon is said to have died after stuffing a chicken with snow to test whether cold preserves meat; the story may be embellished, but it fits his love of experiment.
> Bacon taught science to start from the world rather than from books, and Hume showed that this road never reaches certainty.

# Popper's falsification
Karl Popper (1902–1994), born in Vienna, set out his view in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934 in German). His starting point was a puzzle about Hume: no number of confirmations proves a universal law, but a single solid counterexample can refute one.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Verify", "items": ["1,000 white swans", "still not proof"]}, "r": {"t": "Falsify", "items": ["one black swan", "law refuted"]}}
### Science vs non-science
Popper used this to draw a line, the **demarcation** between science and pseudoscience. A scientific theory must forbid something: it must name observations that would prove it wrong.
He was struck by a contrast in 1919. Einstein's theory predicted that starlight would bend by a specific amount near the Sun, and Eddington's eclipse expedition could have shown it didn't. By contrast, Popper felt that Freudian psychoanalysis and some Marxist theories could explain any outcome at all, so nothing could ever count against them.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1915", "l": "Einstein's prediction"}, {"y": "1919", "l": "Eclipse test"}, {"y": "1934", "l": "Popper's book"}, {"y": "1959", "l": "English edition"}]}
### Strongest objections
• **Duhem–Quine problem**: a failed test never hits one hypothesis alone; you can blame the instrument or a background assumption instead
• Scientists don't actually drop theories at the first anomaly, and often they're right not to
• Some useful claims are hard to falsify, such as "there exist black holes"
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Prediction fails", "items": ["Theory is wrong?", "Instrument faulty?", "Background belief wrong?"]}
!! A theory that has passed many tests is proven => For Popper it is only "corroborated" and stays open to refutation
> A theory is scientific when it sticks its neck out: it tells you what would show it to be wrong.

# Kuhn's revolutions
Thomas Kuhn (1922–1996) trained as a physicist, then studied the history of science. His book *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) became one of the most cited academic books of the 20th century and made "paradigm shift" an everyday phrase.
### Normal science and crisis
A **paradigm** is the shared package a field works within: core theories, standard methods, model examples and a sense of which questions matter. Most of the time scientists do "normal science", solving puzzles inside it, not trying to overturn it.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Normal science", "Anomalies pile up", "Crisis", "Revolution", "New paradigm"]}
### Copernicus over Ptolemy
Ptolemy's Earth-centred system worked well for about 1,400 years, patched with circles on circles. Copernicus published his Sun-centred model in 1543, yet it was not more accurate at first. It took Kepler's ellipses and Galileo's telescope in the early 1600s, and then Newton, before the new view clearly won.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 150", "l": "Ptolemy's Almagest"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "Copernicus publishes"}, {"y": "1609", "l": "Kepler's ellipses"}, {"y": "1687", "l": "Newton's Principia"}]}
### Strongest objections
Kuhn said rival paradigms can be **incommensurable**: they use words differently and value different things, so there is no neutral way to compare them. Critics feared this made science a matter of fashion or group psychology. Kuhn later softened his view, arguing that accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness still guide choices, even if they don't decide them mechanically.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Popper", "items": ["refute boldly", "logic of testing"]}, "r": {"t": "Kuhn", "items": ["puzzle-solving", "history of practice"]}}
!? Kuhn cited Max Planck's remark that a new scientific truth wins less by convincing opponents than because the opponents eventually die.
> Science changes not only by adding facts but sometimes by changing the whole frame through which facts are seen.

# Occam's razor
The principle is named after William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347), an English Franciscan friar and logician. The popular slogan, "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity", was written by later authors, but it captures his habit of cutting away needless assumptions. Hence the "razor".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Simpler", "items": ["fewer assumptions", "easier to test"]}, "r": {"t": "More complex", "items": ["more assumptions", "each needs support"]}}
### Why prefer simplicity?
• Every extra assumption is another chance to be wrong
• Simpler theories make sharper predictions, so they are easier to test
• Complex theories can be bent to fit any data, which makes them fit noise as well as signal
Statisticians call that last danger **overfitting**: a model with too many adjustable parts can match last year's figures perfectly and still predict next year badly.
ex: Your car won't start | A: the battery is flat | B: a thief drained it, then returned the keys | Both fit; A needs fewer assumptions, so check it first
### Limits of the razor
Simplicity is a guide, not a guarantee. Nature is sometimes complicated. Chemists once explained burning with a single substance, phlogiston; the more complex oxygen theory replaced it because it fitted the measurements better. And "simple" can be hard to define: fewer entities, fewer equations or fewer kinds of thing?
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "The simplest theory is true", "r": "Test the simplest fit first"}]}
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["List explanations", "Keep those that fit", "Prefer fewest assumptions", "Test, and stay open"]}
!! Occam's razor says the simplest answer is always right => It says don't add assumptions the evidence doesn't require
> When two explanations fit the facts equally well, bet on the one that asks you to believe less.

# Correlation and causation
Two things can rise and fall together without one causing the other. Ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer; the hidden cause is hot weather. Statisticians call such a hidden factor a **confounder**.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "A and B move together", "items": ["A causes B", "B causes A", "C causes both", "Pure chance"]}
### The controlled experiment
The cleanest solution is to change one thing yourself and keep the rest equal. In medicine this becomes the **randomised controlled trial**: patients are assigned by chance to treatment or comparison, so confounders tend to be spread evenly between groups. Ideally neither patients nor doctors know who got what (a "double-blind" trial).
!? In 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind gave six pairs of sailors with scurvy different remedies. Only the pair given oranges and lemons recovered quickly, an early controlled comparison.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Recruit volunteers", "Randomly split groups", "Treat only one group", "Compare outcomes"]}
### When you can't experiment
You can't randomly assign people to smoke for 30 years. In such cases scientists combine clues. In 1965 Austin Bradford Hill listed signs that a link is likely causal.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Strength", "b": "heavy smokers far more at risk"}, {"a": "Dose-response", "b": "more exposure, more effect"}, {"a": "Timing", "b": "cause comes before effect"}, {"a": "Consistency", "b": "seen in many populations"}]}
!! A strong correlation proves cause => Correlation is a clue to investigate, not a verdict
> Before believing that A causes B, ask what else might be driving both.

# Peer review
When scientists submit a paper to a journal, an editor sends it to usually two or three independent experts. They judge whether the methods are sound and the conclusions follow, then recommend acceptance, revision or rejection. Reviewers are usually unpaid and often anonymous.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Author submits", "Editor screens", "Experts review", "Revise or reject", "Publication"]}
### A younger habit than you'd think
The Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions*, founded in 1665, is the oldest science journal still published. But systematic outside review spread mainly after the Second World War. *Nature* made it routine only in the 1970s. Einstein was annoyed in 1936 when a journal sent his paper to an anonymous referee.
### What it catches, and what it misses
Reviewers can spot weak statistics, missing controls and overblown claims. They usually can't see raw data or repeat the experiment, so careful fraud often slips through.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Often catches", "items": ["flawed methods", "overstated claims"]}, "r": {"t": "Often misses", "items": ["fabricated data", "results that won't replicate"]}}
In 1998 *The Lancet* published Andrew Wakefield's small study linking the MMR vaccine to autism. It passed review, was later found to be fraudulent, and was retracted in 2010. In 2015 a large project tried to repeat 100 psychology studies and got clearly matching results in only about 36–39% of them, depending on the measure.
### Reforms
• Pre-registration: stating methods before collecting data
• Open data so others can check analyses
• Preprints, discussed publicly before formal review
!! Peer-reviewed means proven true => It means a few experts found no obvious flaws; replication is the stronger test
> Peer review is a filter, not a guarantee; science's real check is other people trying again.

# Pseudoscience
Pseudoscience looks like science, using technical words, charts or lab coats, but doesn't behave like it. Philosophers call the task of drawing that line the **demarcation problem**, and no single test settles every case.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Vague, flexible predictions", "Failures explained away", "No mechanism that fits physics", "Little change in centuries", "Appeals to testimonials"]}
### Homeopathy
Homeopathy, invented by Samuel Hahnemann in the 1790s, dilutes substances again and again. A common "30C" remedy is diluted 100-fold thirty times.
ex: Each step dilutes 1 : 100 | 30 steps = 1 part in 10 to the power 60 | A glass of water holds about 10 to the power 25 molecules | Almost certainly no original molecule remains
Large reviews, such as Australia's national health research council in 2015, found no reliable evidence that it works better than placebo for any condition.
### Astrology
Astrology is testable in principle, which is to its credit. When tested, it fails. In a 1985 study published in *Nature*, astrologers tried to match birth charts to personality profiles and did no better than chance.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "accuracy (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Chance", "v": 33.0, "t": "1 in 3"}, {"l": "Astrologers", "v": 33.0, "t": "about 1 in 3", "hi": true}]}
### A fair caveat
Some ideas once called fringe, such as continental drift, became mainstream. The difference is that they produced new evidence and made predictions that came true. Being unpopular isn't the problem; refusing to be tested is.
!? Popper's falsification idea was born partly from asking why astrology and some psychology felt different from Einstein's physics.
> Pseudoscience is defined less by what it claims than by how it treats evidence that it's wrong.

# Models are maps
A map of Lisbon's metro leaves out streets, hills and true distances, and that is exactly why it's useful. Scientific models work the same way: they keep what matters for a purpose and drop the rest.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Reality", "items": ["endlessly detailed", "hard to compute"]}, "r": {"t": "Model", "items": ["simplified", "makes predictions"]}}
### "All models are wrong"
The British statistician George Box wrote in 1976 that all models are wrong, and later added that some are useful. The point isn't despair. It is to ask how wrong a model is, and whether that matters for the job.
ex: Newton's gravity is "wrong" (Einstein improved it) | Yet it's accurate enough to plan most spacecraft paths | GPS satellites need Einstein's corrections | Otherwise errors grow by about 10 km a day
### Kinds of model
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Physical", "b": "scale model in a wind tunnel"}, {"a": "Mathematical", "b": "equations for a pandemic"}, {"a": "Computer", "b": "climate and weather forecasts"}, {"a": "Animal", "b": "mice standing in for humans"}]}
### Trouble with maps
Models can mislead when people forget their assumptions. Before 2008, many financial risk models assumed house prices across regions wouldn't fall together. Weather forecasts, by contrast, are honest about limits: they give probabilities and get less reliable after about a week.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Prediction", "Model", "Simplifying assumptions", "Messy reality"]}
!? Jorge Luis Borges wrote a one-paragraph story about an empire whose map was as large as the empire itself, and therefore useless.
!! A model that's wrong should be thrown away => Keep it if it's useful and you know where it breaks
> A good model, like a good map, is judged by whether it gets you where you need to go.

# Science corrects itself
Science's claims are always provisional. That sounds like a weakness, but it is the source of its reliability: errors are found and fixed because people keep checking.
### Continental drift
In 1912 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. He pointed to the fit of South America and Africa, matching fossils and matching rock layers across oceans. Most geologists rejected him, partly with good reason: he couldn't explain what force moved continents.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1912", "l": "Wegener's proposal"}, {"y": "1930", "l": "Wegener dies in Greenland"}, {"y": "1963", "l": "Seafloor magnetic stripes"}, {"y": "late 60s", "l": "Plate tectonics accepted"}]}
After the Second World War, mapping the ocean floor revealed long ridges and symmetrical magnetic stripes on either side, showing new crust forming and spreading. The mechanism had arrived, and the theory of **plate tectonics** won within about a decade.
!? The Azores sit near a meeting point of three plates, the North American, Eurasian and African, on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the Atlantic still widens by about 2 cm a year.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Claim made", "Others test it", "Errors found", "Claim revised"]}
### The awkward part
Self-correction is slow and not automatic. It depends on people funding replications, publishing null results and admitting mistakes. Some errors, like the MMR-autism fraud, took over a decade to retract fully.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Science changed its mind, so it's unreliable", "r": "Changing with evidence is how it stays reliable"}]}
> Science doesn't promise to be right; it promises a way to find out when it's wrong.

# Laozi and the Tao
Laozi, "Old Master", is traditionally dated to the 6th century BCE, a contemporary of Confucius. Many historians doubt he was a single real person; the *Tao Te Ching* was probably compiled over time, roughly in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE. It is short, about 5,000 Chinese characters in 81 brief chapters, and one of the most translated books in the world.
### The Way that can't be named
**Tao** (or Dao) means "way" or "path". The text opens by warning that the Tao that can be spoken of is not the eternal Tao. It is the source and pattern of everything, more like the way nature flows than a god who commands.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tao", "b": "the natural way of things"}, {"a": "Te", "b": "virtue, inner power"}, {"a": "Wu wei", "b": "effortless, unforced action"}, {"a": "Pu", "b": "the uncarved block, simplicity"}]}
### Wu wei
Wu wei literally means "non-action", but it doesn't mean doing nothing. It means acting without strain, the way an expert cook carves along the joints rather than hacking. Water is the favourite image: soft and yielding, yet it wears down stone.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Forcing", "items": ["strain and control", "many rules"]}, "r": {"t": "Wu wei", "items": ["go with the grain", "fewer interventions"]}}
### Politics too
The text advises rulers to govern lightly, like cooking a small fish: poke it too much and it falls apart.
### Objections
Confucian critics saw Taoism as a recipe for withdrawal from social duty. Others ask whether "going with the flow" offers any guidance when nature itself includes disease and cruelty.

# Yin and yang
The words originally meant the shady side and the sunny side of a hill. The same hill has both, and as the Sun moves, each turns into the other. That image holds the core idea: opposites that define and need each other.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Yin", "b": "dark, cool, receptive, rest"}, {"a": "Yang", "b": "bright, warm, active, motion"}]}
### Four features
• **Interdependence**: there's no "light" without "dark" to contrast it
• **Relativity**: something is yin or yang only compared with something else
• **Change**: each grows as the other shrinks, like day into night
• **Seeds**: each contains a trace of the other, shown by the small dot in each half
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Yang at its peak", "Yin begins to grow", "Yin at its peak", "Yang begins to grow"]}
### Where it shows up
Yin–yang thinking runs through the *I Ching*, an ancient book of divination, and through Chinese cosmology, cooking, martial arts and traditional medicine. Diagrams of the **taiji** appear from the 11th century CE; the familiar black-and-white swirl with dots became popular later still, long after the ideas.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c.900 BCE", "l": "Core of the I Ching"}, {"y": "c. 300 BCE", "l": "Yin-yang school"}, {"y": "11th c.", "l": "Zhou Dunyi's diagram"}, {"y": "1949", "l": "On South Korea's flag"}]}
### Strength and limits
The idea is a good antidote to black-and-white thinking: a strength overdone becomes a weakness. Critics note that it is so flexible it can describe almost anything, which makes it a way of seeing rather than a testable theory. Traditional pairings also linked yin to the female and yang to the male, a mapping many now question.

> Yin and yang aren't enemies but partners, each turning into the other over time.

# Confucian relationships
Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551–479 BCE) was a teacher and minor official in a time of war between Chinese states. His sayings were collected by students in the *Analects*. He believed society could be restored not by harsher laws but by people of good character fulfilling their roles.
### The five relationships
The list was spelt out most clearly by his follower Mencius. Each tie runs both ways: the senior partner owes care and example, the junior owes respect and loyalty.

### Key virtues

# Hindu philosophy
"Hinduism" is a later umbrella term for many traditions of the Indian subcontinent. Its oldest texts, the **Vedas**, date back to about 1500–1200 BCE. The later **Upanishads** (roughly 800–200 BCE) turned from ritual to philosophy: what is the self, and what is ultimate reality?
### Four key ideas
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dharma", "b": "duty, right order, ethics"}, {"a": "Karma", "b": "actions bring consequences"}, {"a": "Samsara", "b": "the cycle of rebirth"}, {"a": "Moksha", "b": "liberation from the cycle"}]}
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Action (karma)", "Consequences", "Rebirth (samsara)", "New actions"]}
### Self and ultimate reality
Many Upanishads link **atman** (the deepest self) with **Brahman** (ultimate reality). A famous phrase, "tat tvam asi", "you are that", suggests they are one. The Advaita Vedanta school, systematised by Shankara around the 8th century CE, takes this literally; other schools keep God and self distinct.
### Many schools
Classical Indian philosophy counts six orthodox schools that accept the Vedas, including Nyaya (logic), Samkhya, Yoga and Vedanta. Buddhists, Jains and the materialist Charvaka school rejected Vedic authority, and they debated each other sharply.

Critics, including Indian reformers, have argued that karma was used to justify caste inequality: your low status is your own past fault. Others ask whether karma really explains the suffering of babies, or merely redescribes it. Defenders see karma as a moral law of responsibility, not a reason to accept injustice.

> Hindu philosophy asks not only how to live well, but how to be free from the cycle altogether.

# The Bhagavad Gita
The Gita, "Song of the Lord", is a poem of 700 verses in 18 chapters. It sits inside the vast Sanskrit epic, the *Mahabharata*, and was probably composed sometime between the 5th and 2nd centuries BCE; dates are disputed.
### The setting
On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, the archer Arjuna sees relatives and teachers in the enemy ranks. He drops his bow, refusing to kill his own family. His charioteer, Krishna, turns out to be divine, and the dialogue that follows is the Gita.

### Act without attachment
Krishna's central teaching is to do your duty fully but give up attachment to the "fruits" of action. Worry about results, praise or blame distorts what you do. A doctor should operate as well as possible, then accept the outcome calmly.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Karma yoga", "b": "path of selfless action"}, {"a": "Jnana yoga", "b": "path of knowledge"}, {"a": "Bhakti yoga", "b": "path of loving devotion"}]}
### Readings and objections
Gandhi called the Gita his "spiritual dictionary" and read the battle as an allegory for the struggle within each person. Others read it literally, and critics ask whether it justifies violence in the name of duty. A further question: if duty is set by your social role, does the text entrench caste? Commentators have debated both for centuries.

# Four Noble Truths
Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, lived in northern India and Nepal, probably in the 5th century BCE, though exact dates are disputed. After years of extreme fasting failed, he took a "Middle Way" and reached awakening. His first teaching, in a deer park at Sarnath, set out the Four Noble Truths.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Dukkha: suffering", "Cause: craving", "Cessation: it can end", "Path: the way out"]}
### The doctor's structure
The truths follow the pattern of medicine: symptom, cause, prognosis, treatment. **Dukkha** is broader than pain. It means unsatisfactoriness: even good things end, and wanting them to stay causes friction.
### The Eightfold Path
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Eightfold Path", "items": ["Wisdom: view, intention", "Ethics: speech, action, living", "Mind: effort, mindfulness, focus"]}
The eight "right" factors are practised together, like strands of a rope, not as steps one to eight.
### Strongest objections
Critics like Nietzsche saw Buddhism as life-denying: if desire is the problem, is the cure to stop caring? Buddhists reply that the target is **craving** (tanha), clinging and grasping, not all motivation; wanting to help others is encouraged. Others ask whether full cessation (nirvana) is a realistic goal or an ideal to steer by.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Craving", "items": ["must have it", "clinging"]}, "r": {"t": "Healthy intention", "items": ["care and effort", "without grasping"]}}
!! Buddhism says life is miserable => It says life contains unsatisfactoriness, and offers a way to end it
> The Buddha's teaching works like a diagnosis: name the problem, find its cause, and treat it.

# Impermanence
Buddhism lists three marks of existence. **Anicca**, impermanence: everything changes. **Dukkha**, unsatisfactoriness: clinging to changing things hurts. **Anatta**, no-self: there is no fixed, unchanging "you" behind experience.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Suffering (dukkha)", "Clinging to the unchanging", "Impermanence (anicca)"]}
### No fixed self
Buddhist texts analyse a person into five "heaps": body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations and consciousness. Each is changing; look for a permanent owner and you don't find one. A famous dialogue compares a person to a chariot: wheels, axle and frame, but no extra "chariot" thing besides them.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Body", "Feelings", "Perceptions", "Mental formations", "Consciousness"]}
### Western echoes
David Hume, looking inward in 1739, found only a "bundle" of perceptions, never a self. The philosopher Derek Parfit argued that personal identity matters less than we think and noted the parallel with the Buddha.
ex: Your body replaces many cells constantly | Red blood cells last about 4 months | Some neurons last a lifetime | Change is real, but uneven
### Objections
If there's no self, who is reborn, and who is responsible for past actions? Buddhist schools answer with a stream of causally linked moments, like a flame lighting a new candle. Critics doubt this explains moral responsibility. Others argue that some sense of a stable self is psychologically necessary.
!! Your whole body is replaced every seven years => That's a myth: cells renew at very different rates, and some last your entire life
> If nothing lasts, peace comes not from holding on but from letting things change.

# Zen
Zen grew out of Chinese **Chan** Buddhism, shaped by Taoist ideas. Legend credits the Indian monk Bodhidharma, around the 5th–6th century CE, with founding it. It reached Japan in the late 12th and 13th centuries, where Eisai founded the Rinzai school and Dogen the Soto school.

### Practice over theory
Zen's name means "meditation". Its core practice is **zazen**, seated meditation, often just sitting with attention. A traditional saying describes Zen as a transmission outside the scriptures, pointing directly at the mind.

A koan is a puzzle given by a teacher that can't be solved by logic. The "one hand clapping" koan comes from the 18th-century Japanese master Hakuin. Another: a monk asks whether a dog has Buddha-nature, and the master answers "Mu", roughly "no" or "not".

### Culture

Critics say anti-intellectualism can become a cover for vagueness. Historians have shown some Zen leaders supported Japanese militarism before 1945, raising hard questions about whether meditation alone makes people ethical.

# Augustine
Augustine was born in 354 in Thagaste, a Roman town in what is now Algeria, to a pagan father and a devout Christian mother, Monica. He trained as a teacher of rhetoric and spent years searching: first in Manichaeism, a religion that saw the world as a battle between good and evil powers, then in the Neoplatonism of Plotinus.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "354", "l": "Born in Thagaste"}, {"y": "386", "l": "Converts in Milan"}, {"y": "c. 397", "l": "Writes Confessions"}, {"y": "410", "l": "Goths sack Rome"}, {"y": "430", "l": "Dies in Hippo"}]}
### Why the Confessions is new
The *Confessions* (about 397–400) is written as a long prayer addressed to God. Augustine looks back at his own restless desires, from stealing pears as a boy "for the thrill of it" to his famous conversion in a Milan garden in 386. No earlier author had examined his inner life in such detail. The book is less a list of events than an investigation of what a self is.
### Memory and time
In Book 10 he marvels at memory as a vast inner palace, holding things he is not thinking of right now. In Book 11 he asks what time is, and admits he knows until someone asks him to explain it. His answer: time lives in the mind as a stretching of the soul.

After Rome was sacked in 410, he wrote *The City of God*, contrasting the earthly city, built on love of self, with the heavenly city, built on love of God.
> Augustine turned philosophy inwards: to find truth, he said, look within yourself.

# The problem of evil
The problem is old. A version usually credited to Epicurus runs: if God wants to stop evil but can't, he isn't all-powerful; if he can but won't, he isn't all-good. So why is there evil? This is called the **logical problem of evil**: it claims God and evil cannot both exist.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "God is said to be…", "items": ["all-powerful", "all-good", "all-knowing", "…yet evil exists"]}
### Augustine's two answers
First, evil is not a thing that God created. It is a **privation**, an absence of good, the way blindness is an absence of sight or rot is a lack of soundness in wood. Second, moral evil comes from free creatures who turn away from the higher good towards lesser goods. A world with free beings who can choose love is, he argued, better than a world of puppets.
### Later replies and objections
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Free will defence", "b": "real choice makes evil possible"}, {"a": "Soul-making", "b": "hardship builds virtue"}, {"a": "Sceptical theism", "b": "we can't see God's reasons"}]}
Critics raise strong objections. Calling evil an "absence" does not make a child's pain hurt any less. Free will may explain cruelty, but what about **natural evil**: earthquakes, plagues, cancer? The 1755 Lisbon earthquake, which killed tens of thousands on All Saints' Day, made many Europeans ask exactly this. Modern philosophers often shift to the **evidential problem**: even if God and evil are logically compatible, does the sheer amount of suffering make God unlikely?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Logical problem", "items": ["God + evil impossible", "needs a contradiction"]}, "r": {"t": "Evidential problem", "items": ["evil makes God unlikely", "weighs the evidence"]}}
> The problem of evil is the most discussed challenge to belief in God, and the answers remain contested on both sides.
!? Alvin Plantinga's 1974 free will defence persuaded many philosophers, including some atheists, that the purely logical version fails, so debate has largely moved to the evidential one.

# Boethius
Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 477–524) was a Roman aristocrat serving Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king who ruled Italy. He reached the top: consul in 510, and later head of the civil service. Then he was accused of treason, imprisoned near Pavia, and executed.

Lady Philosophy goes through what people chase and shows that each falls short.

# Islamic golden age
From the late 700s, the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad paid scholars to translate Greek science and philosophy into Arabic. Many translators were Christians who worked from Greek and Syriac. The result was a library of Aristotle, Plato's ideas, Galen and Euclid in Arabic.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Greek texts", "Syriac and Arabic", "Arabic commentaries", "Latin in Toledo", "European universities"]}
### Not just preserving
Philosophers in the Islamic world (*falsafa*) built new systems on this base. They asked how Greek reason fitted with the Qur'an, how the soul relates to the body, and what makes something necessary.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Al-Kindi (d. c. 873)", "b": "first great Arab philosopher"}, {"a": "Al-Farabi (d. 950)", "b": "called the Second Teacher"}, {"a": "Avicenna (980–1037)", "b": "medicine, soul, being"}, {"a": "Averroes (1126–98)", "b": "the Commentator on Aristotle"}]}
### A famous debate
Around 1095 the theologian al-Ghazali wrote *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, arguing that some of their claims, such as the eternity of the world, were unproven and against faith. About 80 years later Averroes, a judge in Córdoba, replied with *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, defending philosophy. The exchange is still read as a classic of faith-and-reason debate.

In the 1100s, translators in Toledo, such as Gerard of Cremona, turned Arabic works into Latin. Medieval Latin writers simply called Aristotle "the Philosopher" and Averroes "the Commentator". Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine* was taught in European medical schools for about 500 years.

# Avicenna's floating man
Ibn Sina (980–1037), known in Latin as Avicenna, was a Persian polymath who wrote on medicine, logic and metaphysics. In his work on the soul, part of the huge *Book of the Cure*, he set out a thought experiment that later readers called the "floating man".
### The set-up
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Created fully grown", "Eyes covered", "Floating, limbs apart", "Feels nothing at all", "Still knows: I exist"]}
Imagine you are created in an instant, already adult, suspended in air so still that you feel no breeze. Your limbs are spread so they do not touch each other. You have never had any sensations. Would you know that you exist? Avicenna says yes, at once. Yet you would not know that you have a body, hands or a heart.
### What he concluded
If you can know your self while knowing nothing of your body, then the self is not the same thing as the body. For Avicenna this pointed to an immaterial soul. He also used it to show that self-awareness is the most basic kind of knowledge, prior to learning anything through the senses.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Avicenna (c. 1020s)", "items": ["floating man", "self known without body"]}, "r": {"t": "Descartes (1641)", "items": ["evil demon doubt", "I think, so I exist"]}}
### Objections
• Imagining something is not proof it is possible: can a never-stimulated brain even think?
• Knowing X without knowing Y doesn't show X and Y differ. Someone can know Lisbon without knowing it is Portugal's largest city.
• Modern neuroscience suggests our sense of self is built from body signals, including the inner sense of where our limbs are.
!! Descartes invented the "I exist" argument from nothing => Avicenna made a similar move about 600 years earlier, and Latin scholars read him
> The floating man asks what you would still know about yourself if every sense were switched off.

# Maimonides
Moses ben Maimon (c. 1138–1204), known as Maimonides or by the acronym Rambam, was born in Córdoba in Muslim Spain. When the strict Almohad rulers took power, Jews were pressed to convert, and his family fled. After years in Fez, he settled in Egypt, where he became a court physician and leader of the Jewish community.

### Who was perplexed?
He wrote the *Guide for the Perplexed* in Arabic, in Hebrew letters, for an educated Jew who had studied Aristotle and was troubled that Scripture seemed to clash with philosophy. His approach: when the Bible talks of God's hand or anger, read it as metaphor. Reason and revelation, properly read, agree.
### Speaking about God by negation
Maimonides held that God is so far beyond us that positive statements mislead. We can only say what God is not. Each denial removes an error and brings us closer.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "God is not a body", "b": "no shape, place or parts"}, {"a": "God is not powerless", "b": "rather than God is strong"}, {"a": "God is not many", "b": "absolute unity"}]}
### Where reason stops
Aristotle taught that the world is eternal. Maimonides argued neither eternity nor creation can be strictly proved. Since reason cannot decide, he accepted creation, as Scripture teaches. This careful line, giving reason its due but naming its limits, influenced Thomas Aquinas, who cites "Rabbi Moses".
!! Medieval religious thinkers simply rejected reason => Maimonides treated rational study as a religious duty
> Maimonides argued that faith understood through reason is deeper than faith without it.

# Thomas Aquinas
Thomas Aquinas was born around 1225 near Aquino, in southern Italy, into a noble family. When he chose to join the new Dominican order of wandering preachers, his brothers kidnapped him and held him at home for about a year. He escaped, studied under Albert the Great, and became a master at the University of Paris.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "By reason alone", "items": ["God exists", "God is one", "natural moral law"]}, "r": {"t": "Only by revelation", "items": ["the Trinity", "the Incarnation", "the Resurrection"]}}
### Two roads to one truth
Aquinas held that some truths can be reached by reason, and others only through revelation. Because both come from God, a real conflict is impossible. If philosophy seems to contradict faith, there is a mistake in the reasoning, and better reasoning will find it. This let him use Aristotle, a pagan, as a serious guide.
### Big ideas
• **Natural law**: basic moral rules, such as preserving life, can be known by reason by anyone.
• **Just war**: war needs proper authority, a just cause and right intention.
• **The Summa Theologiae**: a vast, unfinished guide to theology, written as thousands of questions.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["State the question", "List the objections", "Give the answer", "Reply to each objection"]}
### Not always welcome
In 1277 the Bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions linked to Aristotle's influence, and some matched Aquinas's views. Only later did he become the Catholic Church's model theologian: he was made a saint in 1323. Critics, from Protestant reformers to modern sceptics, question whether reason can really go as far towards God as he thought.
> For Aquinas, faith and reason are like two windows looking onto the same landscape.
!? Classmates nicknamed the large, quiet Thomas "the dumb ox"; Albert reportedly replied that this ox's bellowing would be heard around the world.

# Five ways
In his *Summa Theologiae* Aquinas gives five short arguments that God exists. Each starts from something we observe and reasons back to its source. They take up barely a page.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["1 Motion → first mover", "2 Causes → first cause", "3 Contingent things → necessary being", "4 Degrees of good → perfect being", "5 Order in nature → intelligence"]}
### The first way, step by step
Things change: a log becomes hot because fire heats it. Nothing changes itself from potential to actual; something already actual must do it. If each mover is itself moved by another, without a first, nothing would move at all. So there must be a first, unmoved mover, "and this everyone understands to be God".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hand moves stick", "Stick moves stone", "Each needs a mover", "So a first mover"]}
### A common misreading
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "First in time: a Big Bang starter", "r": "First in order: what holds change up now"}]}
Aquinas actually thought reason could not prove the universe had a beginning. His chain is like a hanging lamp: the chain only works if something holds the top right now.
### Objections
• Why can't the chain go on for ever? Defenders say an infinite chain of borrowed motion explains nothing; critics reply there's no contradiction in it.
• Even if there is a first cause, why think it is personal, good or the God of any religion?
• Hume and Kant doubted that cause-and-effect reasoning applies beyond experience.
• The fifth way, from order, was weakened in many eyes by Darwin's explanation of design-like features.
> The five ways ask why there is change and order at all, not just how they began.
!! "Who moved the first mover?" defeats the argument => The argument claims the first mover is not the kind of thing that needs moving, so the real debate is whether that idea makes sense

# Ockham's razor
William of Ockham was an English Franciscan friar, born around 1287 in the village of Ockham in Surrey. He studied at Oxford, was summoned to the papal court at Avignon to answer charges about his teaching, and in 1328 fled to the protection of Emperor Louis of Bavaria. He died in Munich around 1347, possibly from the Black Death.
### What he actually said
The famous wording, "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity", doesn't appear in his writings. He wrote similar things, such as "plurality should not be posited without necessity". The name "razor" came much later: it shaves off unneeded assumptions.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Explanation A", "items": ["wind moved the door", "1 assumption"]}, "r": {"t": "Explanation B", "items": ["a ghost moved it", "then hid", "3 assumptions"]}}
### Why he used it
Ockham was a **nominalist**: he argued that "redness" or "humanity" are not real things floating apart from individual red objects or individual people. They are names and concepts in our minds. Why posit extra entities when individuals explain everything?
### The razor today
Scientists and doctors use a version of it: prefer the explanation that needs fewer assumptions. A doctor hearing hooves thinks horses, not zebras. Software engineers favour the simplest design that works.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "The simplest theory is true", "r": "Prefer it until evidence demands more"}]}
!! Ockham's razor proves the simplest answer is right => It's a rule of thumb for choosing what to test first, not proof; nature is sometimes complicated
> Don't add assumptions you don't need, but don't remove ones the evidence requires.
!? Ockham was excommunicated after his flight from Avignon; his razor was partly a weapon for cutting back what he saw as needless theological speculation.

# Universities
The word *universitas* simply meant a guild or corporation: a self-governing body of teachers or students, like a guild of bakers. Bologna (from about 1088) and Paris (late 1100s) were the models. Students paid masters, elected officers and could go on strike or move away.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "c. 1088", "l": "Bologna"}, {"y": "c. 1150", "l": "Paris"}, {"y": "c. 1167", "l": "Oxford"}, {"y": "1218", "l": "Salamanca"}, {"y": "1290", "l": "Lisbon / Coimbra"}]}
### Portugal's university
King Dinis founded a "General Study" in Lisbon in 1290, confirmed by the Pope the same year. It moved back and forth between Lisbon and Coimbra several times before settling in Coimbra for good in 1537 under João III. Today the University of Coimbra is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
### What students learned
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Theology, Law, Medicine", "Philosophy (Aristotle)", "Quadrivium: maths, music, stars", "Trivium: grammar, logic, rhetoric"]}
Arts students started young, around 14, with the seven liberal arts. Only after years of logic and philosophy could they enter the higher faculties. Theology took the longest, often more than a decade.
### Learning by arguing
Teaching was built on the **disputation**: a question was posed, arguments on each side were set out, a master gave an answer, and every objection was answered. This "scholastic method" trained people to state opposing views fairly before rejecting them.
!! Medieval universities just repeated Church dogma => Disputation required arguing both sides, and masters disagreed fiercely, sometimes with official condemnations
> The university, with its degrees, faculties and debates, is one of the Middle Ages' most lasting inventions.

# Dare to know
In 1783 a Berlin magazine asked, in a footnote, "What is enlightenment?" Kant answered in December 1784 with a short essay. Enlightenment, he said, is leaving behind an **immaturity** that is self-inflicted: not a lack of intelligence, but a lack of courage to use it without someone else's guidance.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rely on guardians", "Notice you can think", "Find the courage", "Use your own reason"]}
### Why we stay immature
Kant blamed laziness and fear. It's comfortable to have a book think for you, a pastor be your conscience and a doctor decide your diet. Guardians, meanwhile, warn us that independent thinking is dangerous. The motto *Sapere aude*, "dare to be wise", comes from the Roman poet Horace.
### Public and private reason
Kant's surprising distinction: an officer must obey orders on duty (the "private" use of reason, in a post). But as a scholar writing for the public, the same officer may freely criticise military policy.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Private use", "items": ["in your job or office", "must obey"]}, "r": {"t": "Public use", "items": ["as a writer to all", "must be free"]}}
He wrote under the Prussian king Frederick the Great, whose attitude he summed up as: argue as much as you like, but obey. Kant added that his was not yet an enlightened age, only an age of enlightenment: the process was still going on.
### Later critics
• Hamann, Kant's friend, said "immaturity" was easy to blame on others while the guardians stayed in charge.
• In 1944, Adorno and Horkheimer argued that Enlightenment reason can turn into a tool of domination.
• Michel Foucault, in 1984, recast it as a permanent attitude of questioning, not a finished project.
> Enlightenment, for Kant, is not knowing more but daring to think for yourself.
!? The same year, Moses Mendelssohn published his own answer to the question in the same Berlin magazine.

# Spinoza
Baruch (or Bento) de Spinoza was born in Amsterdam in 1632 to a family of Portuguese Jews who had fled the Inquisition. His father, Miguel, was a merchant born in Portugal. The community spoke Portuguese and Spanish; Spinoza grew up between Dutch, Portuguese and Hebrew.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1632", "l": "Born in Amsterdam"}, {"y": "1656", "l": "Expelled (herem)"}, {"y": "1670", "l": "Political treatise"}, {"y": "1677", "l": "Dies; Ethics printed"}]}
### Expelled at 23
In July 1656, the Amsterdam synagogue issued an unusually harsh *herem*, a ban, cursing him and forbidding members to speak with him. The text does not specify his "abominable heresies", but they likely included denying an immortal soul and a personal God. He earned his living grinding lenses.
### God or Nature
In the *Ethics*, written like a geometry book with definitions and proofs, Spinoza argues there is only one substance, which he calls "God, or Nature". Mind and body are not two things but two ways of seeing the same reality.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Traditional God", "items": ["creates the world", "has plans, judges", "outside nature"]}, "r": {"t": "Spinoza's God", "items": ["is the world", "no plans or anger", "nature itself"]}}
Freedom, for Spinoza, is not escaping causes but understanding them. The more we grasp why we feel what we feel, the less our passions push us around.
### Freedom of thought
His *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670), published anonymously, argued that the Bible should be studied like any historical text, and that a state is safest when people may think and say what they like.
!! Spinoza was simply an atheist => Some readers saw him as an atheist, others as "God-intoxicated"; the label depends on what you mean by God
> Spinoza made God and Nature one, and found freedom in understanding why things happen.
!? In 1929 Einstein said he believed in "Spinoza's God", one who shows himself in the order of the world, not one who concerns himself with human fates.

# Voltaire
Voltaire was the pen name of François-Marie Arouet (1694–1778). His wit got him locked in the Bastille twice. Exiled to England in 1726, he admired its relative freedom of religion and the press, and Newton's science, and praised them in his *Letters on the English*.
### Lisbon, 1 November 1755
On All Saints' Day morning a huge earthquake, followed by a tsunami and fires, destroyed much of Lisbon. Tens of thousands died. People across Europe asked how a good God could allow it.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1710", "l": "Leibniz: best world"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Lisbon earthquake"}, {"y": "1756", "l": "Poem on Lisbon"}, {"y": "1759", "l": "Candide"}]}
### Candide
Leibniz had argued that God, being perfect, must have created the best of all possible worlds. In *Candide* (1759), the tutor Pangloss repeats that "all is for the best" while being hanged, flogged and enslaved, and while Lisbon shakes around him. The book ends with the advice that "we must cultivate our garden": work at what's in front of you.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pangloss", "items": ["all is for the best", "explain away suffering"]}, "r": {"t": "Candide's end", "items": ["tend our garden", "do useful work"]}}
### Against intolerance
In 1762 Jean Calas, a Protestant in Toulouse, was tortured and executed on a false charge of killing his son to stop him converting. Voltaire campaigned for three years until Calas was cleared, and wrote his *Treatise on Tolerance*.
Critics note the caricature: Leibniz didn't deny suffering; he claimed any other world would be worse overall. Rousseau also replied that people, not nature, had packed Lisbon into tall buildings.
!! Voltaire said "I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it" => An English writer, Evelyn Beatrice Hall, wrote that line in 1906 to sum up his attitude
> Voltaire used laughter as a weapon against cruelty and comfortable excuses.

# Hume's scepticism
David Hume (1711–1776), from Edinburgh, wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature* in his twenties. He later said it "fell dead-born from the press". Suspected of atheism, he was twice turned down for university posts, yet became famous as a historian.
### Do we ever see a cause?
Watch one billiard ball hit another. You see ball A move, contact, ball B move. You never see a "force" or "necessary connection" passing between them. After many repetitions, your mind simply comes to expect B to move.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["A hits B, B moves", "Again, many times", "Habit forms", "We expect it: 'cause'"]}
This leads to the **problem of induction**: we assume the future will be like the past, but we can't prove that without assuming it again. The sun has risen every day; logic alone can't guarantee tomorrow.
### Three targets
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Causes", "b": "only constant conjunction"}, {"a": "The self", "b": "a bundle of perceptions"}, {"a": "Miracles", "b": "testimony rarely enough"}]}
Look inside for your "self", Hume said, and you only ever find a perception: heat, a thought, a feeling. On miracles, he argued we should believe a report only if its being false would be even more miraculous than the event.
### Is he right?
Critics reply that if Hume's doubts were true, science would be impossible; defenders say science works on well-grounded habits, not certainty. Thomas Reid argued we are entitled to trust common sense. Many think the problem of induction is still unsolved.
!! A sceptic thinks nothing is true => Hume lived by habit and common sense; he only doubted that reason could prove what we instinctively believe
> Hume showed that much of what we call knowledge rests on habit, not proof.
!? Hume also argued you can't derive an "ought" from an "is": facts alone don't tell you what to do.

# Kant's synthesis
In 1781 Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) published the *Critique of Pure Reason*. He was trying to settle a long fight between two camps about where knowledge comes from.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rationalists", "items": ["Descartes, Leibniz", "reason gives knowledge"]}, "r": {"t": "Empiricists", "items": ["Locke, Hume", "experience gives it"]}}
### The Copernican turn
Copernicus explained the sky's motion by moving the observer. Kant did the same for knowledge. Instead of our minds conforming to objects, objects as we experience them conform to our mind's structure. The mind is not a blank screen but an active organiser.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Raw sensations", "Placed in space and time", "Ordered by concepts", "An experienced world"]}
Space and time are the "forms" of our senses. Concepts such as cause and effect are rules the understanding applies. So Hume was right that we never see causation in the raw, but wrong to think it is merely habit: it is a condition of any experience at all. In his short phrase, thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind.
### Two sides of things
• **Phenomena**: things as they appear to us, which science can know in great detail.
• **Things in themselves**: what reality is like apart from our minds, which we can never know.
### Objections
Non-Euclidean geometry and Einstein's relativity challenged Kant's claim that the geometry of space is fixed by our minds. Others asked how he can say anything about things in themselves, even that they exist and cause our sensations.
!! Kant said the outside world isn't real => He said it is real, but we know it only as it appears through our own forms of experience
> We don't see the world as it is; we see it as minds like ours must see it.
!? Kant never travelled more than about 150 km from Königsberg, and neighbours were said to set their watches by his afternoon walk.

# The Encyclopédie
The project began modestly in 1745 as a French translation of Ephraim Chambers' English *Cyclopaedia*. Under Denis Diderot and the mathematician Jean d'Alembert it became something far bigger: a full survey of human knowledge.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "28", "l": "volumes (17 text, 11 plates)"}, {"v": "72,000", "l": "articles, roughly"}, {"v": "21 years", "l": "from first to last volume"}]}
### A map of knowledge
D'Alembert's preface sorted knowledge by mental faculty, following Francis Bacon. Notably, theology was placed as one branch of philosophy, not at the top.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Human knowledge", "items": ["Memory → history", "Reason → philosophy", "Imagination → arts"]}
### Crafts deserve respect
Diderot visited workshops to describe how stockings, glass and paper were made. Thousands of engravings showed tools and trades in detail. The message: practical skill is knowledge too, and spreading it improves lives.
### Battles with censors
Articles slipped criticism into unlikely places, and cross-references led readers from dull entries to sharp ones. In 1759 the government revoked its licence and the Pope condemned it. D'Alembert stepped back, and Diderot finished the text volumes largely in secret.

> The Encyclopédie aimed to change how people think by giving everyone access to how things work.

# Wollstonecraft
Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797) grew up in a household with a violent, debt-ridden father. She worked as a companion, a governess and a teacher, then as a writer and translator in London, unusual for a woman then.
### The argument
In *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), she replied to writers like Rousseau, who held that girls should be educated only to please men. If women seemed vain or weak, she argued, that was the product of training, not nature.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Girls denied education", "Taught only to please", "Seem shallow and weak", "Taken as proof of nature"]}
Her claim rests on Enlightenment logic: if rights come from reason, and women have reason, then women have the same rights. She called for girls and boys to be taught together, for women to be able to earn their own living, and for marriage to be a partnership of equals.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rousseau's Émile", "items": ["girls trained to please", "virtue as modesty"]}, "r": {"t": "Wollstonecraft", "items": ["girls trained to think", "virtue as reason"]}}
### Limits and legacy
Later feminists noted that she still assumed most women would be mothers at home, and wrote mainly about the middle class. Her reputation suffered when her husband William Godwin published a frank memoir of her love affairs. In the 20th century she was rediscovered as a founder of modern feminism.
!! She argued women were already equal in practice => She argued they were equal in reason but kept unequal by education and law
> Wollstonecraft's point: you can't judge what someone is capable of after denying them the means to develop it.

# Adam Smith
Adam Smith (1723–1790) was professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow. His first book, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), was about ethics. He saw it as just as important as *The Wealth of Nations* (1776).
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Moral Sentiments", "items": ["1759", "sympathy", "impartial spectator"]}, "r": {"t": "Wealth of Nations", "items": ["1776", "division of labour", "free trade"]}}
### Self-interest and markets
Smith's famous point: we don't get our dinner from the butcher, brewer or baker's kindness, but from their own interest. In a competitive market, people pursuing their own gain are led, "as by an invisible hand", to serve others' needs too.
### The pin factory
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "10", "l": "workers, each one step"}, {"v": "48,000", "l": "pins a day together"}, {"v": "< 20", "l": "pins a day for one alone"}]}
Dividing work into small steps multiplies output. But Smith warned that endless repetition can make workers "stupid and ignorant", and so he supported public education.
### Not a simple free-marketeer
• He warned that business owners rarely meet even for fun without conspiring against the public.
• He backed some rules, like on banking, and public works.
• His ethics rests on **sympathy**: imagining ourselves in another's place, judged by an inner "impartial spectator".
He criticised the 1703 Methuen Treaty between Portugal and Britain (port wine for English cloth) as an example of special trade favours. Economists still argue about how far markets need rules, and both sides cite Smith.
!! The invisible hand is the core of Smith's book => The phrase appears only once in The Wealth of Nations
> Smith thought markets can turn self-interest to public good, but only within a society of laws, morals and fellow-feeling.

# Revolutions
Enlightenment thinkers didn't plan revolutions, but they supplied the vocabulary. John Locke (1689) argued people have natural rights to life, liberty and property, and governments exist by consent to protect them. A government that violates them may be resisted.

### America, 1776
The Declaration of Independence asserts that people have unalienable rights and that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed". The US Constitution of 1787 divided power between legislature, executive and courts, as Montesquieu recommended.

# Pombal's reforms
Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo (1699–1782) became King José I's chief minister in 1750 and dominated government for 27 years. He was made Marquis of Pombal in 1769. He had served as a diplomat in London and Vienna, where he saw how other states were modernising.

# Hegel's dialectic
Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) finished his first major book, *The Phenomenology of Spirit*, in Jena in 1806, as Napoleon's army fought outside the town. He saw in Napoleon a "world-soul on horseback": history itself on the move.
### How the dialectic works
For Hegel, any one-sided idea, pushed far enough, shows its own limits and turns into its opposite. The clash is not a dead end. It produces a richer idea that keeps what was true in both.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["A position", "Its inner tension", "Its opposite", "A richer whole"]}
His key word is *aufheben*, a German verb with three meanings at once.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "To cancel", "b": "the old form ends"}, {"a": "To preserve", "b": "its truth is kept"}, {"a": "To lift up", "b": "onto a higher level"}]}
### History as freedom
Hegel read world history as the growth of freedom: in ancient despotisms one person was free, in Greece and Rome some were, and in the modern world all are, in principle. He called the driving force **Spirit** (*Geist*): humanity's shared mind becoming aware of itself.
### Master and slave
In a famous passage, two consciousnesses fight for recognition. One becomes master, the other slave. Yet the slave, through work, shapes the world and gains self-understanding, while the master grows dependent. Marx later borrowed this pattern.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Hegel's formula: thesis–antithesis–synthesis", "r": "Others coined it; Hegel rarely used it"}]}
Critics were fierce. Schopenhauer called him a charlatan; Karl Popper in 1945 blamed his view of the state for feeding authoritarianism. Admirers reply that he was describing how freedom becomes real in institutions, not praising any tyranny.
> Hegel taught that conflict between ideas is how understanding grows, not a sign it has failed.
!? Hegel wrote that philosophy, like the owl of Minerva, takes flight only at dusk: we understand an era only as it ends.

# Marx
Karl Marx (1818–1883) was born in Trier, studied Hegel in Berlin, and became a radical journalist. Expelled from several countries, he settled in London in 1849, where he spent decades writing in the British Museum reading room, often in poverty, supported by his friend Friedrich Engels.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1818", "l": "Born in Trier"}, {"y": "1848", "l": "Communist Manifesto"}, {"y": "1849", "l": "Exile in London"}, {"y": "1867", "l": "Capital, vol. 1"}, {"y": "1883", "l": "Dies in London"}]}
### History from the bottom up
Marx turned Hegel around: it isn't ideas that drive history, but how people produce their living. Each economy creates classes, such as lords and serfs or owners and wage workers, whose interests clash.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Law, politics, religion, ideas", "rest on…", "Relations: owners and workers", "Means of production"]}
### Exploitation and alienation
Workers sell their time for a wage. Marx argued they produce more value than they are paid, and the extra, **surplus value**, becomes profit. He also described **alienation**: workers feel estranged from what they make, from their work and from each other.
### Very different outcomes
• Social democratic parties built welfare states and labour rights within democracies, partly inspired by his critique.
• Revolutionary regimes in the USSR, China and elsewhere claimed his name; they produced rapid industrialisation, but also one-party rule, famines and mass repression.
• Scholars still debate how far these regimes followed or betrayed his ideas.
### Strongest objections
Most economists reject his labour theory of value. The revolutions he expected in advanced industrial countries did not come; workers' living standards rose instead. Defenders reply that his analysis of crises, inequality and globalisation remains insightful.
!! Marx designed the Soviet system => He wrote little about how a post-capitalist society should run; Lenin and others filled the gap
> Marx argued that economics shapes history, an idea that influenced historians and social scientists far beyond politics.
!? Only about a dozen people attended Marx's funeral at Highgate Cemetery in 1883.

# Mill's utilitarianism
John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) had an extraordinary childhood. His father, a close ally of Jeremy Bentham, taught him Greek from age three. At 20 he suffered a breakdown; reading Wordsworth's poetry helped him recover. It convinced him that feelings and culture matter, not just calculation.
### Quality, not just quantity
Bentham's utilitarianism says the right act produces the greatest happiness, measured by amount of pleasure. Critics mocked this as a "philosophy fit for pigs". Mill's reply, in *Utilitarianism* (1861): some pleasures are higher in kind.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bentham", "items": ["pleasure is pleasure", "count the amount"]}, "r": {"t": "Mill", "items": ["some kinds are higher", "count quality too"]}}
### Who decides which is higher?
Mill's test is the **competent judge**: ask people who have experienced both pleasures. If nearly all who know both prefer one, even with more discontent, it is higher.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Try both pleasures", "Compare as an expert", "Most prefer one", "That one is higher"]}
### Liberty and equality
In *On Liberty* (1859), Mill set out the **harm principle**: the only good reason to restrict someone's freedom is to prevent harm to others. In *The Subjection of Women* (1869), shaped with Harriet Taylor, he argued for full legal equality of the sexes. As an MP, in 1867 he proposed giving women the vote.
### Objections
• Snobbery: are opera fans really better judges than football fans?
• If quality matters apart from pleasure, critics say Mill has quietly left pure utilitarianism.
• Utilitarianism in general can seem to permit sacrificing individuals for the greater total.
!! Utilitarians only care about crude pleasure => Mill ranked intellectual, moral and creative satisfactions above physical ones
> Mill thought a richer, harder life of the mind is worth more than easy contentment.

# Kierkegaard
Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) lived almost his whole life in Copenhagen. In 1841 he broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen, whom he loved, a decision that haunted his writing. He published many books under playful pseudonyms, so readers would have to judge each view for themselves.
### Three ways to live
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Religious: faith, inwardness", "Ethical: duty, commitment", "Aesthetic: pleasure, novelty"]}
The **aesthete** seeks interest and pleasure and dodges commitment, but ends in boredom and despair. The **ethical** person takes on duties, like marriage and work. The **religious** life requires a personal relationship with God that reason can't justify. Moving between them isn't a calculation; it takes a choice, often called a "leap".
### Truth that must be lived
Kierkegaard said "subjectivity is truth". He didn't mean anything you feel is true. He meant the most important truths concern how you exist. You can know all about swimming and still need to jump in.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Objective truth", "items": ["what is the case", "held at a distance"]}, "r": {"t": "Subjective truth", "items": ["how you relate to it", "staked with your life"]}}
### Anxiety
He called anxiety "the dizziness of freedom": the vertigo of facing open possibilities, knowing you could choose anything. It is unpleasant, but also a sign we are free.
### Critics and heirs
He attacked the Danish state church for making Christianity comfortable. Critics argue his "leap" makes faith irrational; others find his stress on personal choice too individualistic. Sartre, Camus and Heidegger took up his themes, often without his faith.
!? In his early twenties he wrote in his journal that he needed to find a truth for which he could live and die.
> For Kierkegaard, the question is not just what's true, but how you will live by it.

# Schopenhauer
Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) was born in Danzig to a wealthy merchant family. His main work, *The World as Will and Representation*, appeared in late 1818 and sold poorly. In Berlin he scheduled his lectures at the same hour as Hegel's; almost no one came.
### Will behind everything
Following Kant, he said we know the world only as it appears to us: as representation. But inside ourselves we meet something deeper: **will**, a blind urge to strive and want. He claimed this same will drives everything, from gravity to animals to human desire.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Desire: lack, pain", "Satisfaction, briefly", "Boredom", "New desire"]}
Life swings, he said, like a pendulum between pain and boredom. Getting what you want only frees you to want something else.
### Ways out
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Art: will-free contemplation", "Music: the will itself in sound", "Compassion: feel others' pain", "Asceticism: quiet the will"]}
In art we stop wanting and simply look. Compassion, he said, is the basis of ethics: when you see another's suffering as your own, you act kindly. Saints who renounce desire go furthest.
### East and West
He was one of the first Western philosophers to engage closely with Hindu and Buddhist thought, keeping a statue of the Buddha in his study. He saw parallels with the Buddhist idea that craving causes suffering.
### Objections
Critics call his view gloomy temperament dressed as metaphysics. If the will is blind and all-powerful, how can anyone deny it? And he lived comfortably on inherited money while praising renunciation.
!! Pessimism means giving up => Schopenhauer offered art, music and compassion as real, if temporary, relief
> Schopenhauer saw desire as endless, and found peace in moments when we stop wanting.
!? Wagner, Tolstoy, Freud, Einstein and the young Wittgenstein all read him closely.

# Nietzsche's "God is dead"
Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) became a professor of classical philology at Basel aged just 24. Ill health forced him to retire in 1879, and he wrote his major works while wandering between Swiss and Italian lodgings. In 1889 he collapsed in Turin and never recovered.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1844", "l": "Born, son of a pastor"}, {"y": "1869", "l": "Professor at 24"}, {"y": "1882", "l": "The Gay Science"}, {"y": "1889", "l": "Collapse in Turin"}, {"y": "1900", "l": "Dies"}]}
### The madman's message
In *The Gay Science* (1882), a madman runs into the market with a lantern in the morning, crying that he seeks God. The bystanders, who don't believe, laugh. He tells them: God is dead, and we have killed him. Then he says he has come too early: people haven't grasped what they have done.
### What it means
Nietzsche wasn't announcing a fact about heaven. He meant that in modern Europe, belief in God no longer guided culture. Science, history and doubt had eroded it. But Western morality, such as equal worth and pity for the weak, rested on that belief.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Belief in God fades", "Values lose foundation", "Risk: nihilism", "Task: create values"]}
### Not a celebration
The tone is alarm, not triumph. Without a foundation, he feared, people would slide into **nihilism**: the sense that nothing matters. Some would cling to faded values out of habit.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Nietzsche cheering God's death", "r": "Nietzsche warning of its consequences"}]}
### Responses
Religious thinkers reply that faith has not disappeared and that values can rest on God whatever culture believes. Secular critics argue morality can stand on reason, sympathy or social agreement without either God or Nietzsche's drama.
> "God is dead" is a diagnosis of a culture, not a proof about God.
!? Nietzsche's father was a Lutheran pastor, and he himself first studied theology before switching to classics.

# The Übermensch
In *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883–85), the prophet Zarathustra announces the **Übermensch**, usually translated "overman" or "superman". Humanity, he says, is a rope stretched between animal and overman: something to be overcome.
### Three transformations
Zarathustra describes how the spirit changes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Camel: bears duties", "Lion: says 'No' to them", "Child: new 'Yes', new values"]}
The camel carries inherited values; the lion fights them; the child plays and creates. The overman is not a stronger brute but a creator: someone who shapes their own values and says yes to life, including its suffering.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "The 'last man'", "items": ["comfort, no risk", "everyone the same"]}, "r": {"t": "The overman", "items": ["creates values", "affirms all of life"]}}
Zarathustra contrasts the overman with the **last man**, who wants only comfort, small pleasures and safety. Nietzsche feared this was where modern society was heading.
### How it was distorted
After Nietzsche's collapse, his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche controlled his archive. She was an antisemite and German nationalist, and she edited his notes into a book, *The Will to Power*, to fit her views. She later welcomed Hitler to the archive. The Nazis used "Übermensch" in a racial sense he never intended.
### Was he innocent?
Nietzsche mocked antisemites and German nationalism, and broke with Wagner partly over them. But critics note his writing does praise hierarchy and scorn equality, which made it easier to misuse. Scholars still argue over how political his ideal is.
!! The Übermensch is a master race => It's an individual ideal of self-creation; Nietzsche despised racial antisemitism
> Nietzsche's overman is not someone who rules others, but someone who masters and creates themselves.

# Eternal recurrence
In *The Gay Science* (1882), Nietzsche asks you to imagine a demon creeping into your loneliest hour. It tells you that you will live this exact life again, and again, infinitely: every pain, joy and thought in the same order.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["This exact life", "Every moment again", "No change at all", "Forever"]}
### Rejoice or despair?
Would you curse the demon, or call it divine? Nietzsche called this "the greatest weight". The idea is a test: it puts infinite weight on every choice.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Despair", "items": ["regret, resentment", "wish life were other"]}, "r": {"t": "Rejoice", "items": ["love of fate", "would choose it all again"]}}
### Love of fate
The ideal response is **amor fati**, "love of fate": not merely putting up with what happens, but wanting nothing to be different, not forward, not backward. Even your worst moments made you who you are.
### Test or theory?
In his notebooks Nietzsche toyed with recurrence as a physical theory: with finite matter and infinite time, all arrangements might repeat. Most readers today treat it as an ethical thought experiment rather than cosmology.
### Objections
• If you can't remember the earlier lives, in what sense is it "you" again?
• For someone whose life includes terrible suffering, is affirming it all wise or cruel?
• Can you want the past to be as it was without also endorsing the wrongs in it?
> Live so that you could wish this moment to return forever.
!? Milan Kundera's novel *The Unbearable Lightness of Being* (1984) opens by reflecting on eternal recurrence and its opposite: a life lived only once, light and weightless.

# Pragmatism
Pragmatism is the first major philosophy born in the United States. It grew from conversations in a "Metaphysical Club" in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in the 1870s. Its key thinkers were Charles Sanders Peirce, William James and John Dewey.
### The pragmatic test
Peirce's rule (1878): to clarify an idea, ask what practical effects it would have. If two ideas make no difference in practice, they are really the same idea.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Take an idea", "Ask: if true, then what?", "Look at the effects", "That's its meaning"]}
### The squirrel and the tree
James told of a camping argument. A squirrel clings to a tree trunk; a man walks round the tree, but the squirrel keeps moving round too, always facing him. Does the man "go round" the squirrel? James said it depends what "round" means: north, east, south, west of it, yes; front, side, back, no. Once that is clear, the dispute dissolves.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Peirce", "b": "meaning = practical effects"}, {"a": "James", "b": "truth = what works out"}, {"a": "Dewey", "b": "learning by doing"}]}
### Truth that works
James went further: a true belief is one that proves useful over the long run, its "cash value" in experience. Dewey applied pragmatism to education, democracy and science, treating ideas as tools for solving problems.
### Objections
Bertrand Russell objected that useful and true come apart: a comforting lie may work well. Others ask: useful for whom? Pragmatists reply that over time, beliefs that misdescribe reality tend to stop working.
!! Pragmatism means doing whatever is convenient => It is a theory of meaning and truth tied to experiment and evidence
> Pragmatism asks of any idea: what difference would it make if it were true?

# Darwin's impact
Charles Darwin published *On the Origin of Species* in November 1859. Alfred Russel Wallace had reached a similar theory independently, and their papers were presented together in 1858. In 1871, *The Descent of Man* applied the theory directly to humans.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Variation in offspring", "Some traits help survival", "Those are inherited", "Populations change"]}
### End of the watchmaker?
In 1802 William Paley argued that if you find a watch on a heath, you infer a watchmaker; the eye is even more intricate, so it implies a designer. Natural selection offered a different explanation for apparent design: small changes, kept when useful, over millions of years.
### What changed in philosophy
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Human place", "b": "continuous with animals"}, {"a": "Purpose", "b": "explained without a goal"}, {"a": "Ethics", "b": "morality's evolved roots"}, {"a": "Knowledge", "b": "minds as adapted tools"}]}
Pragmatists like Dewey saw ideas as tools that evolve. Later thinkers explored how cooperation and empathy could have evolved, and what that means for morality.
### Misuses and debate
"Survival of the fittest" was coined by Herbert Spencer. So-called Social Darwinists used it to justify inequality, empire and eugenics, which most philosophers reject as confusing how nature works with how we ought to act. Many religious believers accept evolution: the Catholic Church has said it is compatible with faith. Others still reject it.
!! "Fittest" means strongest => It means best suited to reproduce in a given environment, which can mean cooperative, small or camouflaged
> Darwin showed that order and apparent design in nature can arise without a designer, which changed how philosophers think about purpose.
!? Darwin delayed publishing for about 20 years, partly fearing how his ideas would be received.

# What they're for
A thought experiment is an experiment run in the imagination. You set up a scenario, change one thing, and see what follows. The German word *Gedankenexperiment* was popularised by the physicist Ernst Mach in the 1890s, but the method goes back to ancient Greece.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Imagine a scenario", "Vary one detail", "Notice your judgement", "Revise the theory"]}
### Why not just run real experiments?
• Some are impossible: you can't ride a beam of light.
• Some are unethical: you can't push someone off a bridge to test a moral theory.
• Some test concepts, not facts: what counts as "the same ship"?
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Thought experiments", "items": ["Science: Galileo, Einstein", "Ethics: trolley problem", "Mind: Mary's room", "Identity: teleporter"]}
### How they work
A good one isolates a single factor. The trolley problem keeps numbers fixed (five lives versus one) and changes only whether you pull a lever or push a person. If your answer changes, the difference must come from that one detail. Daniel Dennett called such scenarios **intuition pumps**: devices for drawing out and examining our instincts.
### Limits
Intuitions differ between people and cultures, and wording can change answers. Some scenarios describe things that may be impossible, so our reactions may not be reliable. And a vivid story can lead us to overlook what has been left out.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "A thought experiment proves a theory", "r": "It tests and challenges it"}]}
> Thought experiments are philosophy's laboratory: cheap, safe, and able to go where real experiments can't.
!? Schrödinger's cat (1935) was meant to show how absurd one reading of quantum mechanics was, not to suggest cats can be alive and dead.

# The ship of Theseus
The Greek writer Plutarch, around 100 CE, told how the Athenians preserved the ship of their hero Theseus. As planks rotted, they replaced them, until philosophers argued whether it was still the same ship. In 1655 Thomas Hobbes added a twist: what if someone kept the old planks and rebuilt them?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Repaired ship", "items": ["all new planks", "same shape, same place", "in continuous use"]}, "r": {"t": "Rebuilt ship", "items": ["all original planks", "assembled later"]}}
### Competing answers
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Continuity", "b": "the repaired ship, gradually"}, {"a": "Same matter", "b": "the rebuilt one, old planks"}, {"a": "Function", "b": "whichever is used as it"}, {"a": "Convention", "b": "no fact; we just decide"}]}
### Why it matters
The puzzle reaches far beyond boats. Clubs, bands and countries change all their members over time. Is Portugal the "same" country as in 1143? Lisbon's restored trams, cathedral repairs and museum restorations raise the question in daily life. Philosophers also apply it to people: if your body's materials keep changing, what makes you the same person?
### A tidier view?
Some philosophers say objects are like events stretched through time, with temporal "parts". Then two ships can share early parts and split later, like a road that forks. Critics find this strange: is the ship you see now only a slice of the real thing?
!! Every cell in your body is replaced every seven years => Many cells renew, but most brain neurons and the lens of the eye last your whole life
> The ship of Theseus shows that "the same" can mean several different things, and we usually don't notice which one we're using.

# Brain in a vat
Imagine a mad scientist removes your brain, keeps it alive in a vat of nutrients, and wires it to a computer. The computer sends exactly the signals your nerves would receive. You "see" your room, "feel" your coffee cup, and notice nothing odd. How could you tell?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Brain in a vat", "Computer sends signals", "Same experiences as now", "Could you tell?"]}
### An old doubt in new clothes
In 1641 Descartes imagined an evil demon deceiving him about the entire outside world. The brain in a vat swaps the demon for technology. Films like *The Matrix* (1999) and Nick Bostrom's 2003 "simulation argument" gave it fresh life.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Descartes (1641)", "items": ["evil demon", "all-powerful deceiver"]}, "r": {"t": "Vat (20th century)", "items": ["scientist + computer", "simulated world"]}}
### The sceptic's argument
• If I know I have hands, I know I'm not a handless brain in a vat.
• I can't know I'm not a brain in a vat.
• So I don't know I have hands.
### Replies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "G. E. Moore", "b": "I know I have hands, so…"}, {"a": "Hilary Putnam", "b": "the claim refutes itself"}, {"a": "Contextualism", "b": "'know' shifts with context"}]}
Moore simply ran the argument backwards: he is more sure of his hands than of any philosophical premise. Hilary Putnam (1981) argued that a real brain in a vat couldn't even think about real vats, because its word "vat" would refer to simulated ones. So if you can truly think "I am a brain in a vat", it's false. Critics doubt this settles it.
> You can't prove from the inside that your experiences come from a real world, yet almost no one lives as though they don't.
!? Putnam's own reply depends on a theory of meaning: words get meaning from causal contact with things, which the vat brain lacks.

# Experience machine
In 1974, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, the American philosopher Robert Nozick described a machine run by "superduper neuropsychologists". Plug in and you'd experience anything you chose: writing a great novel, falling in love, winning a match. You would believe it was all real. Would you plug in for life?
### Why most say no
If pleasure or happy experiences were all that mattered, plugging in would be the obvious choice. Most people refuse. Nozick suggested we care about three things the machine can't give.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Doing things", "b": "not just feeling we did"}, {"a": "Being someone", "b": "brave, kind, actually"}, {"a": "Real contact", "b": "with reality and real people"}]}
### A blow to hedonism
**Hedonism** in this sense says only pleasure is good in itself. The experience machine is one of the most cited arguments against it. If you'd rather a real friend than a perfect simulated one, you value something beyond your experiences.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Inside the machine", "items": ["perfect experiences", "no real achievements"]}, "r": {"t": "Real life", "items": ["mixed experiences", "real people and deeds"]}}
### Objections
• **Status quo bias**: maybe we just fear change. In a reversed version, people are told they're already in a machine and asked if they want to unplug into an unknown real life. Many say they would stay.
• Worries about the machine breaking, or who runs it, may cloud the answer.
• Some hedonists bite the bullet: plugging in would be better, even if it feels wrong.
!? Philosopher Felipe De Brigard published a reversed-machine study in 2010; responses suggested much of our refusal depends on which life we start from.
> Most people want their lives to be real, not just to feel good.

# Mary's room
In 1982 Australian philosopher Frank Jackson imagined Mary, a brilliant scientist. She has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room, learning about the world through black-and-white screens. She knows every physical fact about colour: wavelengths, the retina, the brain's responses.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mary knows all physics", "Never sees colour", "Leaves the room", "Sees a red tomato", "New knowledge?"]}
### The argument
If Mary learns something new when she first sees red, namely what it is like to see red, then not all facts are physical facts. Something about conscious experience escapes physics. These felt qualities are often called **qualia**.
### Replies from physicalists
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "What does Mary gain?", "items": ["New ability, not a fact", "Acquaintance with red", "Old fact, new concept", "Nothing: she'd predict it"]}
• **Ability hypothesis**: she gains know-how, like recognising and imagining red, not a new fact.
• **New concept**: she learns an old physical fact in a new way, as Frege's two names can pick out one planet.
• **Dennett**: if she truly knew everything physical, she wouldn't be surprised at all; we just can't imagine "everything".
### A change of mind
Jackson later abandoned the argument and became a physicalist himself, around the late 1990s. Others still defend it as a powerful challenge to the view that physics can describe everything.
!? A real cousin of this puzzle, Molyneux's problem (1688), asks whether someone born blind who gains sight could recognise a cube by sight alone; a 2011 study of patients after cataract surgery suggested not straight away.
> Mary asks whether knowing every fact about the brain tells you what an experience actually feels like.

# The teleporter
Imagine a machine on Earth scans every cell of your body, records it, and destroys the original. The data travels to Mars, where an exact copy is built from local atoms. The copy steps out with your memories, personality and plans, convinced it is you. Is it?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Scan every cell", "Destroy the original", "Send data to Mars", "Build exact copy"]}
### Two kinds of view
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Psychological view", "items": ["memories, character", "so the copy is you"]}, "r": {"t": "Biological view", "items": ["this living body", "so you died on Earth"]}}
John Locke (1690s) tied personal identity to consciousness and memory. On that view, teleporting is just travel. Others hold that you are a living organism, and organisms don't survive being dismantled.
### The branch-line case
Derek Parfit, in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), added a twist: the scanner malfunctions and doesn't destroy you. Now there's you on Earth and a copy on Mars. They can't both be you, since they'll soon live different lives. So which one is?
### Parfit's answer
Parfit concluded that **identity is not what matters**. What matters is psychological continuity and connectedness. If the copy will carry on your projects and love your people, that is almost as good as ordinary survival. He found this comforting: death seemed less of a wall.
### Objections
• Many feel strongly that being copied isn't surviving; the original should fear the scanner.
• If identity doesn't matter, should you care more about your future self than about strangers?
!? *Star Trek* fans have long noted that its transporter, read literally, might kill the traveller every time.
> The teleporter asks what makes you "you": your body, your mind, or something else.

# Newcomb's problem
The physicist William Newcomb devised this puzzle around 1960; Robert Nozick published it in 1969. A Predictor, who has been right almost every time, offers you two boxes.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Box A (open)", "b": "€1,000, always"}, {"a": "Box B (closed)", "b": "€1 million or nothing"}, {"a": "Predicted 'B only'", "b": "€1 million was put in B"}, {"a": "Predicted 'both'", "b": "B was left empty"}]}
You may take only Box B, or both boxes. The prediction was made yesterday; the boxes are already filled.
### Two arguments that can't both win
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "One-boxers", "items": ["follow expected value", "one-boxers get rich"]}, "r": {"t": "Two-boxers", "items": ["money's already there", "both always adds €1,000"]}}
ex: Predictor right 99% | Take B only: 0.99 × €1,000,000 = €990,000 expected | Take both: 0.01 × €1,000,000 + €1,000 = €11,000 expected
The two-boxer replies: whatever is in Box B, it's there now. If it holds a million, taking both gives you €1,001,000. If it's empty, both gives €1,000 instead of nothing. Taking both is better in every case. This is called **dominance**.
### Why it matters
The puzzle splits decision theory into two camps. **Evidential** theory says pick the action that is best news about your outcome. **Causal** theory says pick the action that causes the best outcome. In most choices they agree; here they don't.
!? Nozick remarked that almost everyone thinks the answer is perfectly obvious, but they split roughly evenly on which answer it is.
> Newcomb's problem shows that two sensible rules for rational choice can point in opposite directions.

# The original position
In *A Theory of Justice* (1971), the American philosopher John Rawls asked: what rules for society would you choose if you didn't know what place you would have in it?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Forget your place", "Behind the veil", "Choose the rules", "Then live by them"]}
### The veil of ignorance
Behind the veil, you don't know your sex, ethnicity, talents, wealth, religion or even your idea of a good life. You know general facts about economics and psychology. You want to do well, but you can't tailor rules to favour yourself. It's like cutting a cake when you don't know which slice you'll get.
### Rawls's two principles
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1 Equal liberties", "b": "speech, vote, conscience"}, {"a": "2a Fair opportunity", "b": "jobs open on equal terms"}, {"a": "2b Difference", "b": "inequality must help the worst-off"}]}
Rawls argued that, facing such high stakes, you would play safe and protect the worst position you could end up in.
### Strong objections
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rawls", "items": ["protect the worst-off", "play safe"]}, "r": {"t": "Harsanyi", "items": ["maximise the average", "take a fair gamble"]}}
• John Harsanyi argued rational people would maximise average welfare, accepting some risk.
• Robert Nozick (1974) argued justice depends on how holdings were acquired, not on a final pattern.
• Communitarians like Michael Sandel said no one is a "self" stripped of all ties and values.
!! The veil means people should forget who they are in real life => It's a test for fairness, not a recommendation to ignore real identities
> Rawls asks you to judge the rules of society as if you could end up anywhere in it.
!? Rawls served as an infantryman in the Pacific in the Second World War, and later said the war shook his religious faith.

# Galileo's falling bodies
Aristotle taught that heavier objects fall faster, in proportion to their weight. For almost 2,000 years, few tested it carefully. In *Two New Sciences* (1638), Galileo knocked it down with pure reasoning.
### The argument
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tie heavy to light stone", "Light one drags: slower", "Pair is heavier: faster", "Contradiction!", "All fall at same rate"]}
Suppose Aristotle is right. Tie a heavy stone to a light one. The light stone falls more slowly, so it should hold back the heavy one: the pair falls slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair together is heavier than the heavy stone, so it should fall faster. It can't do both. So speed of falling can't depend on weight.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Aristotle", "items": ["heavy falls faster", "speed ∝ weight"]}, "r": {"t": "Galileo", "items": ["all fall alike", "air resistance aside"]}}
### What about feathers?
A feather does fall slower than a hammer on Earth. Galileo knew this: the difference comes from air resistance, not weight. In a vacuum they fall together.
### Real experiments too
Galileo also rolled balls down gently sloping ramps, timing them with water clocks, to measure acceleration. Thought experiment and real measurement worked together.
!! Galileo dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa => The story comes from a later biography by his pupil and there's no firm evidence he did it
> A single, careful contradiction can bring down a theory without any equipment at all.
!? In 1971 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the Moon; with no air, they landed at the same moment.

# Einstein's light beam
Around 1895, aged 16, Albert Einstein was at a school in Aarau, Switzerland. He later recalled asking himself: what would I see if I ran alongside a beam of light at the speed of light?
### The puzzle
If you could catch up with a light wave, it should look frozen: a wave of electric and magnetic fields standing still. But the laws of electromagnetism, set out by James Clerk Maxwell in the 1860s, allow no such thing. Something had to give.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chase a light beam", "See a frozen wave?", "Maxwell: impossible", "Light speed is fixed"]}
### Special relativity, 1905
Einstein made two assumptions: the laws of physics are the same for everyone moving steadily, and light always moves at the same speed, about 300,000 km per second, however fast you go. Then he followed the logic.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Time dilation", "b": "moving clocks run slow"}, {"a": "Length contraction", "b": "moving things shorten"}, {"a": "Simultaneity", "b": "depends on the observer"}, {"a": "E = mc²", "b": "mass is a form of energy"}]}
### In your pocket
GPS satellites carry atomic clocks. Because they move fast, special relativity makes them run slow by about 7 microseconds a day; weaker gravity makes them run fast by about 45. Without correcting the net 38 microseconds, positions would drift by around 10 km a day.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "−7 μs", "l": "per day: speed effect"}, {"v": "+45 μs", "l": "per day: gravity effect"}, {"v": "~10 km", "l": "daily error if ignored"}]}
!! Einstein was bad at maths at school => He excelled at maths; he did fail the general part of an entrance exam at 16, while scoring highly in maths and physics
> Einstein's revolution began with a question a child might ask, taken completely seriously.

# Technology isn't neutral
A common slogan says tools are neutral: what matters is how people use them. Philosophers of technology argue that's only half true. Tools make some actions easy and others hard, and so they quietly steer what people do.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Neutral tool view", "items": ["only use matters", "a hammer is a hammer"]}, "r": {"t": "Value-laden view", "items": ["design steers use", "defaults shape habits"]}}
### Defaults are decisions
Whatever happens when you do nothing is a design choice. In organ donation, some countries ask you to opt in; others, including Portugal since 1993, count you as a donor unless you opt out. A well-known 2003 study compared neighbouring countries with similar cultures.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% consenting to donate (2003 study)", "items": [{"l": "Germany (opt in)", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Austria (opt out)", "v": 99.98, "hi": true}]}
### Examples everywhere
• Cars and motorways reshaped cities around parking and commuting, often at the expense of walking.
• Infinite scroll removes natural stopping points; its inventor later said he regretted its effect on attention.
• Phone notifications are designed to pull you back, because attention is what many apps sell.
In 1980, Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" argued that designs can build in social choices: who gets access, who is in control.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Designer makes choices", "Tool makes some acts easy", "Users form habits", "Society changes"]}
### The other side
Critics warn against **technological determinism**: tools don't force outcomes. People resist, repurpose and regulate them. Text messaging was a minor feature that users turned into a culture.
!? Marshall McLuhan's 1964 phrase "the medium is the message" meant that a technology's effect on how we live matters more than any single piece of content it carries.
> Every design embeds choices about what people should do, so someone is always deciding, even by default.

# Privacy
Privacy isn't just about hiding secrets. In 1890 two American lawyers, Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, described it as "the right to be let alone", worried about newspaper gossip and the new portable camera.
### Why privacy matters
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Freedom", "b": "think and explore unwatched"}, {"a": "Dignity", "b": "control how others see you"}, {"a": "Relationships", "b": "share more with some people"}, {"a": "Power", "b": "limits who can manipulate you"}]}
Jeremy Bentham designed the **Panopticon** in 1791: a prison where inmates could always be watched but never knew when. The philosopher Michel Foucault argued that people who might be watched start policing themselves. This is the **chilling effect**: surveillance changes behaviour even when nothing bad is done with the data. After the 2013 Snowden revelations, one study found visits to Wikipedia articles on terrorism-related topics dropped.
### "Nothing to hide"
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Nothing to hide", "items": ["honest people safe", "privacy aids crime"]}, "r": {"t": "Replies", "items": ["rules can change", "data can be wrong", "privacy isn't secrecy"]}}
Supporters of more surveillance stress real benefits: catching criminals, public health, convenience. The debate is about balance and safeguards.
### Europe's answer: GDPR
The EU's General Data Protection Regulation took effect on 25 May 2018. It gives people the right to see their data, correct it, have it erased in some cases and take it elsewhere. Organisations need a legal basis to process personal data. In Portugal the national data protection authority (CNPD) enforces it.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2018", "l": "GDPR takes effect"}, {"v": "4%", "l": "max fine: global turnover"}, {"v": "€1.2 bn", "l": "record fine, Meta, 2023"}]}
> Privacy protects not only what you hide but your freedom to become who you choose.

# Algorithmic bias
Machine-learning systems learn patterns from past data. If the past was unfair, the system can learn the unfairness and apply it at scale, with an appearance of objectivity.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Biased past decisions", "Become training data", "Model learns pattern", "Repeats it at scale"]}
### The hiring example
In 2018 Reuters reported that Amazon had scrapped an experimental tool for rating CVs. Trained on ten years of applications, mostly from men, it learned to downgrade CVs including the word "women's", as in "women's chess club". Engineers tried to fix it, but couldn't be sure it wouldn't find other proxies.
### Where bias comes from
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Sources of bias", "items": ["Skewed training data", "Proxy variables", "Choice of target", "Unequal error rates"]}
A 2018 study, "Gender Shades", tested commercial face-analysis systems. Error rates in guessing gender were far higher for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% error, worst system (Gender Shades, 2018)", "items": [{"l": "Lighter men", "v": 0.8}, {"l": "Darker women", "v": 34.7, "hi": true}]}
### Fairness is hard to define
Should a system have equal error rates across groups, or equal accuracy of its positive predictions? Mathematicians have shown that, when groups have different base rates, these goals can't all be satisfied at once. Choosing between them is an ethical decision, not a technical one.
!? In the Netherlands, an automated risk system used in childcare benefits wrongly flagged thousands of families, many with dual nationality, as fraudsters; the government resigned over the scandal in 2021.
!! Algorithms are objective because they're maths => They inherit choices about data, targets and trade-offs made by people
> An algorithm trained on the past can automate the past's injustices unless someone checks.

# Who's responsible?
In March 2018, an Uber self-driving test car in Arizona struck and killed Elaine Herzberg as she crossed a road pushing a bicycle. The system had detected her but didn't classify her correctly in time; the safety driver was looking at a phone; emergency braking had been disabled. Who was to blame?
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Who is to blame?", "items": ["Safety driver", "Company", "Software designers", "Regulators"]}
### The problem of many hands
When many people each contribute a small part, it can seem that no one is responsible. The philosopher Dennis Thompson called this the **problem of many hands**. With machine learning, there is also a **responsibility gap**: no one programmed the specific decision; the system learned it.
### Levels of automation
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["5: drives anywhere alone", "4: alone in set areas", "3: drives, human on call", "2: assists, human watches", "0–1: human drives"]}
As cars move up these levels, responsibility shifts from driver to maker. Some manufacturers have said they will accept liability while their level 3 systems are engaged.
### How law is adapting
In 2024 the EU updated its Product Liability Directive so that software, including AI, counts as a product: makers can be liable for defects without proof of negligence. In the Uber case, prosecutors charged the safety driver, not the company; she later pleaded guilty to a lesser charge.
### Different views
Some argue only humans can be morally responsible, so a person must always be accountable. Others propose shared or strict liability schemes, like insurance, focused on compensating victims rather than finding a villain.
> When responsibility is spread thin, someone has to be designated to carry it, or no one will.

# The trolley problem, automated
In 1967 philosopher Philippa Foot imagined a runaway tram heading for five workers; you can divert it onto a track where it will kill one. Judith Jarvis Thomson added the "footbridge" version: pushing a large person off a bridge would stop it. Most people would divert, but wouldn't push.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pull the lever", "items": ["one dies as side effect", "most say yes"]}, "r": {"t": "Push the man", "items": ["one used as a means", "most say no"]}}
### The Moral Machine
MIT researchers built an online game in which players chose whom a brake-failed self-driving car should spare. Results published in *Nature* in 2018 covered about 40 million decisions from people in 233 countries and territories.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "40 million", "l": "decisions recorded"}, {"v": "233", "l": "countries and territories"}, {"v": "3", "l": "broad cultural clusters"}]}
### What they found
• Broad agreement: spare humans over animals, more lives over fewer, the young over the old.
• Clusters differed: in some regions, preferences for sparing the young were weaker.
• Preferences linked to culture and economics, such as attitudes to rule-following.
### Should votes decide?
Germany's 2017 ethics commission on automated driving said cars must not choose victims by age, sex or other personal features. Popular preference and ethical principle can clash.
### Critics of the framing
Many engineers argue real crashes are rarely clean dilemmas. A car's job is mostly to see hazards early and brake. Focusing on rare dilemmas may distract from everyday safety and from who sets the rules.
!? The original trolley problem was part of Foot's article on abortion and the "doctrine of double effect", not about transport at all.
> Automation forces us to write down moral choices that humans used to make in a split second.

# Automation and work
Fear of machines taking jobs is old. Between 1811 and 1816, English textile workers called **Luddites** smashed machines they blamed for wage cuts. In 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes predicted that by 2030 people might work about 15 hours a week. Productivity rose as he expected, but working hours fell much less.
### How many jobs are at risk?
Estimates vary hugely depending on method.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of jobs at high risk (estimates)", "items": [{"l": "Frey & Osborne (US)", "v": 47.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "OECD 2016 study", "v": 9.0}]}
The first study looked at whole occupations; the second looked at the tasks within jobs and found most jobs would change rather than vanish. Generative AI has reopened the question, now including office and creative work.
### Proposals
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Retraining", "b": "help people move to new jobs"}, {"a": "Shorter week", "b": "share the work around"}, {"a": "Basic income", "b": "cash for all, no conditions"}, {"a": "Ownership", "b": "share profits of automation"}]}
Finland gave 2,000 unemployed people €560 a month without conditions in 2017–2018. Employment rose only slightly, but participants reported better wellbeing and less stress. Portugal ran a government-backed four-day-week pilot with private companies in 2023.
### Meaning, not just money
Philosophers ask what work gives besides income: structure, skill, recognition, community. Marx worried about alienated labour; others say freedom from toil could let people pursue art, care and learning. Critics of basic income raise cost and incentives; supporters cite security and dignity.
!! Automation always destroys jobs overall => Historically it has shifted work between sectors; whether AI is different is debated
> The question isn't only what machines can do, but how a society shares the gains and what people do with their time.

# AI alignment
**Alignment** means making sure an AI system pursues the goals its designers and users actually intend. It sounds simple. The trouble is that we rarely say exactly what we mean.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["We state a goal", "AI optimises it literally", "Finds a loophole", "Unintended result"]}
### Old stories, new problem
King Midas wished that everything he touched would turn to gold, and then couldn't eat. Goethe's sorcerer's apprentice ordered a broom to fetch water and couldn't make it stop. In 1960 the mathematician Norbert Wiener warned that we'd better be sure the purpose we put into a machine is the one we really desire.
### Specification gaming
In a 2016 experiment, an AI trained to win a boat-racing game discovered it scored more points by circling forever, hitting the same targets and catching fire, than by finishing the race. It did what it was rewarded for, not what was wanted.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Reward points in the race", "r": "Agent circles, never finishes"}, {"w": "Maximise user engagement", "r": "Feed promotes outrage"}]}
### Key ideas
• **Goodhart's law**: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
• **Outer alignment**: did we specify the right goal?
• **Inner alignment**: did the system actually learn that goal, or something that merely correlated with it in training?
• Today's chatbots are trained partly with human feedback, rating which answers are helpful and harmless.
### Disagreements
Some researchers think misaligned advanced AI could pose very serious risks and deserves urgent attention. Others think such worries are speculative and distract from present harms like bias and misinformation. Many work on both.
> The hard part of instructing a powerful system is saying what you really want, including everything you'd assume went without saying.
!? Stuart Russell has argued that AI should be uncertain about human goals, so it keeps asking and accepts being switched off.

# Can AI be conscious?
In 1950 Alan Turing proposed sidestepping "Can machines think?" with a test: if a machine's conversation is indistinguishable from a human's, we have as much reason to credit it with thought as we do other people. Today's chatbots make the question practical.
### Two big camps
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Functionalism", "items": ["mind = what a system does", "silicon could do it"]}, "r": {"t": "Biological view", "items": ["mind needs living brains", "simulation isn't the thing"]}}
### The Chinese room
In 1980 John Searle imagined himself in a room, following a rulebook to answer Chinese questions without understanding Chinese. From outside it looks fluent. Searle argued computers likewise manipulate symbols without understanding. Critics reply that the whole system, room plus rulebook, might understand, even if he doesn't.
### Scientific theories differ
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Global workspace", "b": "info broadcast widely"}, {"a": "Integrated info", "b": "how unified a system is"}, {"a": "Higher-order", "b": "states about own states"}]}
A 2023 report by researchers from several fields checked AI systems against such theories. It concluded that no current system was a strong candidate for consciousness, but found no obvious technical barrier to building one.
### Why it matters
If a system could feel, causing it suffering would matter morally. Jeremy Bentham said of animals that the question is not "Can they reason?" but "Can they suffer?" Some argue for caution with systems we don't understand; others warn that wrongly attributing feelings to software could mislead users and distract from human needs.
!! Fluent talk proves an AI is conscious => Language ability and inner experience can come apart; fluency alone settles nothing
> We have no agreed test for consciousness, so we're deciding how to treat AI without knowing what it is like, if anything, to be one.

# Deepfakes and truth
"Deepfake" combines "deep learning" and "fake". The term spread from an online forum in 2017. Since then, tools to swap faces, clone voices and generate realistic video have become cheap and easy.
### Real cases
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2017", "l": "term coined online"}, {"v": "~$25 m", "l": "lost in 2024 video-call scam"}, {"v": "seconds", "l": "of audio to clone a voice"}]}
In 2024, an employee of an engineering firm's Hong Kong office transferred about US$25 million after a video call in which the "colleagues", including the finance chief, were deepfakes.
### Harm goes both ways
Fakes can make people believe false things. But there's a second harm: real evidence can be dismissed as fake. Legal scholars call this the **liar's dividend**. The philosophical issue is **testimony**: much of what we know comes from trusting others, and video was a strong form of testimony.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fakes believed", "items": ["false scandals", "scams, fraud"]}, "r": {"t": "Truth doubted", "items": ["'that video is fake'", "evidence loses force"]}}
### Defences
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Stop: check your reaction", "Investigate the source", "Find other coverage", "Trace to the original"]}
• **Provenance**: standards such as Content Credentials attach a signed record of how a file was made.
• **Labelling**: the EU's AI Act requires AI-generated or manipulated content to be disclosed.
• **Media literacy**: checking sources before sharing, especially when something makes you angry.
!? Detection tools struggle to keep up, so experts increasingly focus on proving where genuine media came from rather than spotting fakes.
> When seeing is no longer believing, trust has to come from checking where things came from.

# Questions to ask of any tech
You don't need a philosophy degree to think ethically about a new technology. A few plain questions do most of the work.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Who benefits?", "Who might be harmed?", "What if everyone used it?", "Can it be undone?", "Who decides?"]}
### Where the questions come from
"What if everyone used it?" echoes Kant's test of whether you could will a rule for all. "Who benefits, who is harmed?" is the utilitarian's ledger. The critic Neil Postman asked of each new technology: what problem does it solve, and whose problem is it?
### Worked example: a doorbell camera
ex: Benefits: owner feels safer, can see deliveries | Harmed: neighbours and passers-by recorded without consent | Everyone uses it: streets under constant private surveillance | Undo: footage may be shared with others, hard to recall | Response: point it at your own property, check data settings
### Why "can it be undone?" matters
In 1980 David Collingridge described a dilemma for controlling technology.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Early on", "items": ["easy to change", "effects unknown"]}, "r": {"t": "Later on", "items": ["effects clear", "hard to change"]}}
Reversible choices can be tested and adjusted. Irreversible ones, like releasing personal data or a technology that reshapes infrastructure, deserve more caution. Critics of heavy caution note that delay has costs too: useful medicines, safety tools or clean energy held back.
!! Asking ethical questions means being against technology => The aim is to shape tools well, not to reject them
> Before adopting a tool, ask who it serves, who it costs, and whether you could turn back.

# Words and things
Why does the sound "cat" pick out furry animals that purr? In Plato's dialogue *Cratylus*, one character argues names fit their objects naturally; another says names are just agreed conventions. Most linguists side with convention.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Portuguese", "b": "gato"}, {"a": "English", "b": "cat"}, {"a": "German", "b": "Katze"}, {"a": "Japanese", "b": "neko"}]}
### The arbitrary sign
The Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure (1916) said a word links a sound pattern to a concept, and the link is **arbitrary**: nothing about "gato" resembles a cat. Onomatopoeia, like "miau", is a partial exception, yet even animal sounds differ between languages.
### Three corners
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Word: 'cat'", "Concept in the mind", "The actual animal"]}
In 1923, Ogden and Richards drew this as a triangle: words connect to things only through thoughts. Theories differ on how the last link works.
### How does a name hook onto a thing?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Description theory", "items": ["name = description", "fits whoever matches"]}, "r": {"t": "Causal theory", "items": ["name passed on", "from an original naming"]}}
Saul Kripke argued in 1970 that names work like a chain: someone first dubbed a person "Aristotle", and others borrowed the name from them, down to us. You can refer to Aristotle even if what you believe about him is wrong.
### The translator's puzzle
W. V. Quine imagined a linguist hearing a speaker say "gavagai" when a rabbit runs past. Does it mean rabbit, rabbit-part or "rabbiting is happening"? He argued that no amount of behaviour settles it fully.
> Words don't carry meaning in their sound; it comes from the shared practices that link them to minds and the world.
!? Children learn around 10 new words a day on average during their school years, mostly from context, not definitions.

# Sense and reference
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German mathematician who helped create modern logic. In an 1892 paper he asked a deceptively simple question: why is "a = b" informative when "a = a" is not?
### Two names, one planet
Ancient astronomers named the bright "star" seen at dawn Phosphorus, and the one at dusk Hesperus. Discovering that both are Venus was a real discovery. "Hesperus is Hesperus" tells you nothing; "Hesperus is Phosphorus" tells you something.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Venus (reference)", "items": ["'Morning star': seen at dawn", "'Evening star': seen at dusk"]}
### Frege's distinction
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Reference", "b": "the thing a name picks out"}, {"a": "Sense", "b": "the way it's presented"}]}
Two expressions can share a reference but differ in sense. This explains why someone could believe the morning star is beautiful without believing the evening star is.
### Belief and substitution
Normally, swapping names for the same thing keeps a sentence true. But inside beliefs it fails. Lois Lane believes Superman can fly; she doesn't believe Clark Kent can fly, though they're the same man.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plain statement", "items": ["swap equal names", "truth stays the same"]}, "r": {"t": "Inside a belief", "items": ["swap equal names", "truth may change"]}}
### Names for nothing
"Sherlock Holmes" has a sense, so we understand it, but no reference. Frege said sentences with empty names are neither true nor false. Bertrand Russell later offered a different answer, treating such names as hidden descriptions.
> One thing can be presented in many ways, and meaning lives partly in the way, not just the thing.
!? Frege's work went largely unnoticed for years; recognition came partly through Russell and Wittgenstein, who both visited him.

# Wittgenstein's early view
Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951), from a wealthy Viennese family, studied with Bertrand Russell at Cambridge. He drafted the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* as a soldier in the First World War and finished it in an Italian prisoner-of-war camp. Published in 1921, it is fewer than 80 pages of numbered propositions.
### The picture theory
He reportedly got the idea from a Paris court case where a car accident was reconstructed with toy cars and dolls. The toys could represent the crash because their arrangement matched the arrangement of real things.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Toy car", "b": "stands for a real car"}, {"a": "Their positions", "b": "show a possible fact"}, {"a": "A true sentence", "b": "matches the facts"}]}
A meaningful sentence, he argued, is a logical picture of a possible state of affairs. It's true if things are as pictured.
### What can't be said
Ethics, aesthetics, God and the meaning of life aren't facts in the world, so sentences about them picture nothing. They can't be said, only shown. Even the *Tractatus*'s own sentences are a ladder to climb and then throw away.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Can be said", "items": ["facts, science", "true or false"]}, "r": {"t": "Only shown", "items": ["ethics, value", "logic, the mystical"]}}
### Influence and turn
The Vienna Circle took the book as a manifesto for science-only meaning, though Wittgenstein felt they missed its point: what can't be said mattered most. Believing he had solved philosophy, he left it and taught in Austrian village schools in the 1920s. He later decided the book was badly mistaken.
!! The final line means mysticism is nonsense => Wittgenstein thought the unsayable was most important; the silence was respect, not dismissal
> The early Wittgenstein drew a line between what language can picture and what it can only point towards.

# Meaning is use
After returning to Cambridge in 1929, Wittgenstein slowly dismantled his own earlier picture theory. His later ideas appeared after his death in *Philosophical Investigations* (1953).
### Language games
He imagined builders with four words: "block", "pillar", "slab", "beam". When one calls "Slab!", the other brings a slab. Here "Slab!" is an order, not a picture of a fact. Language, he said, is made of many such **language games**, each tied to an activity.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Giving and obeying orders", "Describing an object", "Telling a joke", "Thanking, greeting, praying", "Making up a story"]}
### Words as tools
Words are like tools in a toolbox: a hammer, pliers, glue. They look alike on the page, but their functions differ. For a large class of cases, he wrote, the meaning of a word is its use in the language.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thirsty person: Water!", "b": "a request"}, {"a": "Firefighter: Water!", "b": "an order"}, {"a": "Lost hiker: Water!", "b": "a discovery"}]}
### The beetle in the box
Imagine everyone has a box with a "beetle" no one else can see. The word "beetle" would work in conversation whatever was in the boxes. Wittgenstein used this to argue that words for sensations get their meaning from shared public practice, not private inner pointing. This **private language argument** is still debated.
### Objections
Critics say "use" is too vague to be a theory. Others argue there must be something stable, such as truth conditions, that explains why uses fit together. Defenders reply that Wittgenstein wasn't offering a theory, but cures for confusions.
!! Wittgenstein meant words can mean anything you like => Use is governed by shared rules and practices; you can still misuse a word
> To find out what a word means, look at what people do with it.

# Family resemblance
Try defining "game". Board games, card games, ball games, the Olympic Games, a child throwing a ball against a wall. Some have winners, some don't; some need skill, some luck; some are played alone. In the *Investigations*, Wittgenstein said: don't think, but look.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Chess", "b": "rules, skill, winner"}, {"a": "Patience", "b": "rules, luck, alone"}, {"a": "Ring-a-roses", "b": "fun, no winner"}, {"a": "Football", "b": "rules, skill, teams"}]}
### Overlapping likeness
What you find is a network of similarities, criss-crossing like the features of a family: one has the father's nose, another the mother's eyes. Or like a rope: no single fibre runs its whole length, but the fibres overlap.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Classical definition", "items": ["shared essence", "necessary + sufficient"]}, "r": {"t": "Family resemblance", "items": ["overlapping features", "no single essence"]}}
### Science catches up
In the 1970s, psychologist Eleanor Rosch found people treat categories as having typical and less typical members. A robin feels more "birdy" than a penguin, though both are birds. This **prototype theory** fits Wittgenstein's view.
### Where it's used
• Debates over defining "art" after urinals and soup cans entered galleries.
• Law: what counts as a "vehicle" in a park, or "work" for tax purposes.
• Medicine: many conditions are diagnosed by meeting some, not all, criteria.
### Objections
Critics say anything resembles anything in some way, so family resemblance needs limits. Some philosophers proposed definitions of "game" anyway, such as Bernard Suits: voluntarily trying to overcome unnecessary obstacles.
> Many useful words have no single definition, just a family of overlapping cases.
!? Suits's definition of a game, in his 1978 book *The Grasshopper*, is often cited as a reply to Wittgenstein.

# Speech acts
Philosophers long assumed sentences mainly describe the world and are true or false. J. L. Austin, an Oxford philosopher, noticed many that don't. In lectures given at Harvard in 1955, published as *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), he called them **performatives**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Constatives", "items": ["describe", "true or false", "'It's raining'"]}, "r": {"t": "Performatives", "items": ["do something", "work or fail", "'I promise'"]}}
### Not true or false, but happy or not
"I name this ship the *Santa Maria*" doesn't describe a naming; it is one. It can't be false, but it can go wrong if a passer-by says it, or the wrong ship is named. Austin called such conditions **felicity conditions**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Right words", "Right person", "Right situation", "Act is done"]}
### Three layers of any utterance
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Locution", "b": "what you literally said"}, {"a": "Illocution", "b": "what you did in saying it"}, {"a": "Perlocution", "b": "its effect on the hearer"}]}
"There's a bull in that field" is a statement (locution), may be a warning (illocution) and may make you stay out (perlocution). Austin's student John Searle classified speech acts into kinds: assertions, requests, promises, expressions of feeling and declarations.
### Everyday power
Weddings, verdicts, contracts, apologies, resignations and bets all work through words. Feminist philosophers have used speech-act theory to ask when "no" is heard as a refusal, and how some speech can silence or subordinate.
!! Words are just talk, not action => Many institutions run on speech acts that create real obligations
> Some sentences don't describe the world; they change it.
!? Austin started by contrasting performatives with statements, then concluded that every utterance does something, so even stating is a kind of act.

# Implicature
Paul Grice (1913–1988) gave lectures at Harvard in 1967 explaining how we communicate more than we literally say. He called the extra meaning **implicature**.
### The reference letter
A professor writes for a philosophy job applicant: "Mr X's command of English is excellent, and his attendance at tutorials has been regular." Literally all praise. But the reader infers: he's not good at philosophy. Grice's explanation: the writer is clearly cooperating, yet says far less than expected, so the silence must be the message.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Assume speaker cooperates", "Notice a gap or oddity", "Ask why they said that", "Infer hidden meaning"]}
### The cooperative principle
Grice proposed we expect each other to follow four maxims.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Quantity", "b": "as informative as needed"}, {"a": "Quality", "b": "say what you believe true"}, {"a": "Relation", "b": "be relevant"}, {"a": "Manner", "b": "be clear and orderly"}]}
Implicatures arise when a speaker seems to break a maxim on purpose. "Está frio aqui", said near an open window, is about temperature, but relevance suggests a request: close it.
### Cancellable meaning
Implicatures can be withdrawn. "Some students passed" suggests not all did, but you can add "in fact, all of them did" without contradicting yourself. Literal content can't be cancelled like that.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Said", "items": ["literal content", "can't be cancelled"]}, "r": {"t": "Implicated", "items": ["inferred meaning", "can be cancelled"]}}
!? Advertisers exploit implicature: "Helps fight germs" suggests a big effect without claiming one.
> Much of what we mean lives in what we leave unsaid, decoded by shared expectations.

# Language shapes thought?
Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897–1941), a fire-insurance inspector and amateur linguist, noticed workers smoking near "empty" petrol drums, which were full of explosive vapour. The word "empty" made them feel safe. Building on his teacher Edward Sapir, he argued our language shapes how we see the world.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strong version", "items": ["language fixes thought", "rejected by most"]}, "r": {"t": "Weak version", "items": ["language nudges thought", "some support"]}}
### What evidence shows
Whorf claimed the Hopi language had no concept of time; a later detailed study found plenty of time words and tenses. But careful experiments do show smaller effects.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Russian: two basic blues", "Guugu Yimithirr: north/south", "Grammatical gender, maybe", "Number words: counting"]}
• **Blues**: Russian has two basic words, *goluboy* (light blue) and *siniy* (dark blue). A 2007 study found Russian speakers distinguished shades across that boundary slightly faster. The edge vanished when they had to repeat words silently, suggesting language was actively involved.
• **Directions**: speakers of Guugu Yimithirr in Australia use north/south/east/west rather than left/right, and keep remarkable track of compass directions.
• **Numbers**: groups with few number words struggle to track exact large quantities.
### Balanced view
Language can direct attention and make some distinctions easier to remember. It doesn't prevent people thinking what their language lacks words for; they borrow or coin words.
!! Inuit have hundreds of words for snow => The claim grew by exaggeration; it depends on how you count words in languages that build long compounds
> Language doesn't lock thought in a cage, but it can nudge where we look.

# Saudade
*Saudade* is a Portuguese word for a longing for something or someone absent: a person, a place, a time, sometimes something that never existed. It mixes sadness and sweetness: the pain of absence, the pleasure of remembering.
### Where it comes from
It probably derives from the Latin *solitas*, solitude, through the medieval Galician-Portuguese *soidade*. In the 1400s, King Duarte of Portugal wrote about it in his book *Leal Conselheiro*, describing it as a feeling born of the heart rather than reason.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Latin: solitas", "Old form: soidade", "Modern: saudade"]}
### Cousins in other languages
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Welsh: hiraeth", "b": "longing for a lost home"}, {"a": "German: Sehnsucht", "b": "intense yearning"}, {"a": "Romanian: dor", "b": "longing, missing"}, {"a": "Galician: morriña", "b": "homesick melancholy"}]}
### A national identity?
In the early 20th century the poet Teixeira de Pascoaes made saudade the heart of a movement, *saudosismo*, claiming it expressed the Portuguese soul. Fado and centuries of emigration and sea voyages reinforced the idea. Critics see this as romantic myth-making: every culture knows longing.
### Untranslatable?
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Other languages can't express it", "r": "They need a phrase, not one word"}]}
Philosophers distinguish untranslatable words from untranslatable meanings. You can explain saudade in English in a sentence; you just lose the compactness and cultural echoes. A single word bundles associations that a paraphrase spreads out.
!? Brazil celebrates a "Dia da Saudade" on 30 January.
> Saudade shows how a single word can carry a whole culture's way of feeling, even though the feeling itself belongs to everyone.

# Machines and meaning
Large language models such as the systems behind today's chatbots are trained to predict the next word, or piece of a word, across vast amounts of text. Doing this well forces them to learn grammar, facts, styles and patterns of reasoning, so their output reads fluently.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Huge text corpus", "Learn word patterns", "Predict next token", "Fluent answers"]}
### An old linguistic idea
In 1957 the linguist J. R. Firth said you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Models do exactly this: words that appear in similar contexts get similar internal representations, so "cat" lands near "dog".
### Do they understand?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sceptics", "items": ["form without meaning", "no contact with world"]}, "r": {"t": "Defenders", "items": ["meaning is use", "patterns encode concepts"]}}
• **Symbol grounding**: Stevan Harnad (1990) asked how symbols get meaning if they only connect to other symbols, like a dictionary in a language you don't know.
• **Stochastic parrots**: a 2021 paper argued models stitch together language without reference to meaning.
• **Use**: if meaning is use, as the later Wittgenstein said, systems that use words appropriately may have some kind of meaning.
• **Multimodal models** linking words to images, sound and actions weaken the "no grounding" objection, though how much is debated.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Searle (1980)", "b": "symbols aren't meanings"}, {"a": "Wittgenstein", "b": "meaning is use"}, {"a": "Firth (1957)", "b": "company a word keeps"}]}
!! The debate is settled one way or the other => Philosophers and scientists still disagree about what "understanding" requires
> Language models revive an old question: is meaning in how words connect to each other, or in how they connect to the world?

# What is beauty?
Two broad camps have argued since ancient Greece. **Objectivists** say beauty is a real feature of things, like their shape or size. **Subjectivists** say it is a feeling in the viewer, projected onto the world.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "In the object", "items": ["proportion, harmony", "we can be wrong"]}, "r": {"t": "In the beholder", "items": ["a feeling we project", "no one is wrong"]}}
### The case for "in the object"
Plato thought beauty was real and that loving a beautiful body could lead us upward to beauty itself. Medieval thinkers linked beauty to proportion, harmony and clarity. And we do argue about beauty as if one side could be right, which makes little sense for pure feelings.
### The case for "in the beholder"
David Hume wrote in 1757 that beauty is no quality in things themselves but exists in the mind that contemplates them. Tastes vary across cultures and centuries: Gothic cathedrals were once dismissed as barbaric, and the word "Gothic" began as an insult.
### What science adds
Some preferences are widely shared. In studies, faces that are more symmetrical, or digital averages of many faces, are usually rated more attractive across cultures. Many people also prefer landscapes with open views, water and scattered trees. But fashion, upbringing and familiarity shift tastes a great deal.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Personal taste", "Culture and era", "Shared human tendencies"]}
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Where is beauty?", "items": ["In the object", "In the viewer", "In the relationship"]}
!! The golden ratio is the secret of beauty => Claims that the Parthenon or the Mona Lisa follow it are mostly loose fits, and tests of people's preferences are mixed
> Beauty may live neither wholly in the thing nor wholly in us, but in the meeting between them.

# Plato on art
Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) loved beauty but distrusted the arts. In Book X of the *Republic* he explains why, using a bed.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Form of Bed: true reality", "Carpenter's bed: a copy", "Painting of a bed: a copy of a copy"]}
### A copy of a copy
For Plato, the real thing is the **Form**, the perfect idea of Bed that the mind grasps. A carpenter looks to it and makes one particular bed. A painter merely copies how that bed looks from one angle. So painting stands "third from the truth": the painter needs no knowledge of beds at all.
### Art stirs the wrong part of us
Plato's deeper worry was emotional. Poetry and drama feed grief, anger and desire, which reason should govern. Homer showed gods lying and heroes weeping, poor models for future rulers. So in his ideal city, poets would be honoured but sent away, and stories for children would be censored.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Epistemic worry", "b": "art copies appearances"}, {"a": "Moral worry", "b": "art inflames the passions"}, {"a": "Political worry", "b": "bad models for citizens"}]}
### Strongest objections
• Art can reveal truths about people that literal description misses
• Plato himself wrote vivid dialogues and myths, such as the cave, to teach
• Censorship assumes someone wise enough to decide for everyone
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plato", "items": ["art imitates surfaces", "stirs passions"]}, "r": {"t": "Aristotle", "items": ["art shows the universal", "shapes emotion"]}}
!? Plato called the conflict between philosophers and poets an "ancient quarrel" even in his own day, about 2,400 years ago.
> Plato took art seriously enough to fear it: anything that shapes feelings shapes people.

# Aristotle's catharsis
Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Plato's student, answered his teacher in the *Poetics*, written around 335 BCE. Imitation, he said, is natural to humans: children learn by it, and we enjoy realistic pictures even of things painful to see.
### Why tragedy matters
Poetry, he argued, is more philosophical than history. History says what happened; poetry shows what kind of thing would happen to a certain kind of person. His model was Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hamartia", "b": "the hero's error or flaw"}, {"a": "Peripeteia", "b": "sudden reversal of fortune"}, {"a": "Anagnorisis", "b": "moment of recognition"}, {"a": "Catharsis", "b": "release of pity and fear"}]}
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hero makes an error", "Fortune reverses", "Truth is recognised", "Audience feels catharsis"]}
### What did catharsis mean?
Aristotle mentions it only once in the *Poetics*, so scholars disagree.
• **Purgation**: a medical image, draining excess emotion
• **Purification**: refining emotions so we feel them in the right way
• **Clarification**: understanding suffering better through the play
### Objections
Psychology studies of "venting" anger, for example by hitting a punchbag, find it tends to increase aggression rather than drain it. That challenges a simple purge model, though not the purification or clarification readings. Plato would add that rehearsing emotions strengthens them.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Catharsis", "items": ["Purgation", "Purification", "Clarification"]}
!? Greek tragedies were performed at public festivals for audiences of thousands; the theatre of Dionysus in Athens may have held around 15,000 people.
> Aristotle's reply to Plato: art doesn't just stir emotions, it can teach us how to feel them well.

# Hume's standard of taste
In his essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757), David Hume faced a puzzle. Taste seems subjective, yet we're sure that someone who ranks a mediocre poet above Milton is simply wrong. How can both be true?
### The ideal critic
Hume's answer: feelings are the basis, but some people are better placed to have the right ones. A "true judge" combines five qualities.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Delicacy of perception", "Practice", "Comparison across works", "Freedom from prejudice", "Good sense"]}
### The wine story
Hume borrowed a tale from *Don Quixote*. Sancho's two kinsmen taste wine from a barrel. One detects leather, the other iron. Everyone laughs, until the barrel is emptied and a key on a leather strap is found at the bottom. Fine discrimination is real, and it can be checked.
ex: Kinsman 1 tastes leather | Kinsman 2 tastes iron | Barrel emptied | Key on a leather thong: both were right
### The test of time
A second guide: works that please across centuries and cultures. Homer delighted Athens and Rome and still moves readers today, while fashionable works fade.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "years still read, approx.", "items": [{"l": "Homer", "v": 2700.0, "t": "about 2,700", "hi": true}, {"l": "Camões", "v": 450.0, "t": "about 450"}]}
### Strongest objections
**Circularity**: how do we identify true judges? By their good taste, which is what we were trying to define. Critics also note that Hume's "true judges" looked a lot like educated European men, so the standard may just reflect one group's preferences. And the test of time can preserve works for reasons of power, not merit.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Who has good taste?", "The true judges", "How to spot them?", "Their good taste"]}
> For Hume, taste is a feeling, but feelings can be trained, and trained ones deserve more weight.

# Kant on beauty
Immanuel Kant set out his theory in the *Critique of Judgement* (1790). He wanted to explain how beauty can be based on feeling yet still claim agreement from everyone.
### Three kinds of liking
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "The agreeable", "b": "pleases the senses: sugar"}, {"a": "The good", "b": "approved by a concept: useful"}, {"a": "The beautiful", "b": "pleases in contemplation"}]}
### Disinterested pleasure
If you enjoy a cake, you want to eat it. Kant calls that "interested" pleasure, tied to desire. Pleasure in beauty is **disinterested**: you take delight in a sunset without wanting to own or use it. It doesn't even matter whether the thing exists, only how it appears.
### Universal, yet without rules
When you say "this is beautiful", you speak as if everyone ought to agree. But no rule or concept can prove it; you can't argue someone into seeing a flower as beautiful. Kant explains this by a "free play" between imagination and understanding that all humans share.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "\"I like sugar\"", "items": ["personal", "no one else bound"]}, "r": {"t": "\"This is beautiful\"", "items": ["feels universal", "expects agreement"]}}
### Strongest objections
• Nietzsche mocked disinterest: art excites desire and will, and should
• The sociologist Pierre Bourdieu argued in 1979 that "pure" taste is often a marker of social class
• Kant's best examples are flowers and birdsong, which fits art awkwardly
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Perceive the form", "Free play of faculties", "Disinterested pleasure", "Claim others should agree"]}
!? Kant reportedly almost never travelled more than about 100 km from Königsberg, his home city, yet wrote some of the most influential pages ever on nature's beauty.
> Kant's insight: calling something beautiful is a private feeling that speaks with a public voice.

# The sublime
Beauty soothes. The sublime overwhelms. It's what you feel at a cliff edge, in a storm at sea or under a clear night sky: a thrill mixed with fear and a sense of your own smallness.
### Burke: delightful terror
Edmund Burke's *Philosophical Enquiry* (1757) contrasted the two. Beauty is small, smooth and gentle and inspires love. The sublime is vast, dark, powerful and obscure, and is rooted in terror. The key is safety: real danger is only frightening, but danger seen from a safe distance becomes thrilling.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Beautiful", "items": ["small, smooth", "calm pleasure"]}, "r": {"t": "Sublime", "items": ["vast, powerful", "awe and fear"]}}
### Kant: our minds exceed nature
Kant split the sublime in two. The **mathematical** sublime comes from sheer size, like the starry sky. The **dynamical** comes from power, like a hurricane. In both, imagination fails to grasp the whole, but reason can still think of infinity. So the feeling ends in a kind of pride in our own minds.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Kant's sublime", "items": ["Mathematical: size", "Dynamical: power"]}
### A Portuguese case
The 1755 Lisbon earthquake shocked Europe and prompted a young Kant to write three essays on earthquakes. Today the giant winter waves at Nazaré, where surfers have ridden faces of about 26 m, draw crowds who watch from the cliffs: safe distance, overwhelming force.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1757", "l": "Burke's Enquiry"}, {"v": "1790", "l": "Kant's third Critique"}, {"v": "~26 m", "l": "Nazaré record wave"}]}
### An objection
Some critics say the sublime flatters human self-importance and treats nature mainly as a stage for our feelings. Others ask whether it applies to art at all, or only to nature.
!? Psychologists studying awe find that it often produces a "small self" feeling and can make people more generous.
> The sublime is beauty's wilder sibling: pleasure found at the edge of fear.

# What is art?
In 1917 a new New York exhibition promised to show any work by anyone who paid the fee. Marcel Duchamp submitted a factory-made urinal, laid on its back and signed "R. Mutt 1917", titled *Fountain*. The committee refused to display it. The original was lost; the copies in museums today were made in the 1950s and 1960s.
### Competing definitions
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Imitation", "b": "art represents the world"}, {"a": "Expression", "b": "art conveys feeling"}, {"a": "Form", "b": "art has significant form"}, {"a": "Institutional", "b": "the art world confers status"}]}
Each struggles with *Fountain*. It imitates nothing, expresses no obvious feeling, and Duchamp chose it precisely because it wasn't beautiful.
### The art world
In 1964 Arthur Danto saw Andy Warhol's *Brillo Boxes*, almost identical to supermarket cartons. What made them art, he argued, wasn't anything visible but a theory and history around them. George Dickie went further: art is what the institutions of the art world accept as candidates for appreciation.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1917", "l": "Fountain rejected"}, {"y": "1956", "l": "Weitz: art indefinable"}, {"y": "1964", "l": "Warhol's Brillo Boxes"}, {"y": "2004", "l": "Experts' top artwork"}]}
### Objections
The institutional theory risks circularity and elitism: who counts as the art world? Others, following Wittgenstein's idea of "family resemblance", say art has no single definition at all, only overlapping similarities, like games.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too narrow", "items": ["art must be beautiful", "excludes Fountain"]}, "r": {"t": "Too wide", "items": ["whatever is shown", "excludes nothing"]}}

> After Duchamp, the question shifted from "is it beautiful?" to "what makes this object art?"

# Expression
Leo Tolstoy, author of *War and Peace*, published *What Is Art?* in 1897. His answer: art is the passing on of a feeling. An artist experiences an emotion and creates something that "infects" others with the same feeling.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Artist feels emotion", "Shapes it in a work", "Audience perceives it", "Audience feels it too"]}
### Tolstoy's tests
Good art, for Tolstoy, depended on how individual the feeling was, how clearly it was conveyed and above all how **sincere** the artist was. He also judged art by the feelings it spread: the best art unites people in brotherly feeling.
He applied this harshly. He dismissed much elite art, including Shakespeare and much of his own fiction, as insincere or exclusive, and praised simple folk tales and songs.
### Collingwood's refinement
In 1938 the British philosopher R. G. Collingwood argued that the artist doesn't start with a clear feeling. Making the work is how the artist discovers what they feel. Craft that simply produces a planned emotion, like advertising, is not art proper.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tolstoy", "items": ["feeling first", "then transmitted"]}, "r": {"t": "Collingwood", "items": ["feeling unclear", "clarified by making"]}}
### Strongest objections
• A composer can write a heartbreaking piece on a cheerful day; must they feel it?
• Audiences often feel something different from the artist
• Much art, such as abstract patterns or puzzles, aims at form or ideas, not emotion
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Sad song means a sad singer", "r": "The music expresses sadness"}]}

> Expression theory captures why art moves us, but not every work starts, or ends, in a feeling.

# Ugly art
From the 18th century, aesthetics focused on beauty. Yet many of the most valued artworks are harrowing. Aristotle already noticed the puzzle: we enjoy accurate images of things we'd hate to see in life, such as corpses.
### Goya's Black Paintings
Between about 1819 and 1823, Francisco Goya, old, deaf and disillusioned, painted 14 dark images directly onto the walls of his house near Madrid. They include *Saturn Devouring His Son*. They weren't meant for the public. They were transferred to canvas in the 1870s and now hang in the Prado.

# The big questions
Philosophy of religion asks questions that believers and non-believers can both investigate with arguments. It doesn't settle faith by decree; it tests reasons on both sides.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Does God exist?", "Why is there suffering?", "Can faith be rational?", "What could \"God\" mean?", "Why anything at all?"]}
### Two sources of knowledge
Thinkers have long distinguished **natural theology**, what reason and evidence alone can tell us, from **revealed theology**, what a tradition holds was revealed in scripture or experience. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) argued that reason could show God exists, but that some doctrines need revelation. Others think reason can show no such thing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Natural theology", "items": ["reason and evidence", "open to anyone"]}, "r": {"t": "Revealed theology", "items": ["scripture, tradition", "within a faith"]}}
### Puzzles about God's nature
Even the concept of God raises questions. Can an all-powerful being make a stone too heavy to lift? Many theists answer that omnipotence means power to do anything logically possible, so the puzzle describes nothing. Can God know what you'll freely choose tomorrow?
### Why it matters in daily life
In Portugal's 2021 census, about 80% of people aged 15 and over identified as Catholic and about 14% as having no religion. Questions about God shape views on death, ethics and meaning for believers and sceptics alike.

# Cosmological argument
Cosmological arguments start from a basic fact, that something exists or that things change, and reason back to a first cause or explanation.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Cosmological", "items": ["Kalam: a beginning", "Aquinas: first cause", "Leibniz: contingency"]}
### The kalam version
Developed by medieval Islamic thinkers such as al-Ghazali (11th century) and revived in 1979 by William Lane Craig.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["What begins has a cause", "The universe began", "So it has a cause", "Cause: timeless, powerful"]}
Supporters point to Big Bang cosmology, which traces our universe back about 13.8 billion years, as support for the second premise.
### Aquinas's ways
In the 1260s–70s Aquinas offered five "ways". The first three argue from motion, causation and contingency: chains of causes can't go back forever, so there's a first cause "which everyone calls God".
### Strongest objections
• **Why God?** A first cause needn't be personal, good or unique
• **What caused God?** Defenders reply only things that begin need causes; critics ask why the universe couldn't be the uncaused thing
• **Physics**: cosmologists debate whether the Big Bang was an absolute beginning or a transition from something earlier
• **Hume and Kant**: our idea of cause comes from inside the universe; it may not apply to the universe as a whole
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Supporters", "items": ["infinite regress absurd", "universe needs a reason"]}, "r": {"t": "Critics", "items": ["could be a brute fact", "gap to a personal God"]}}
> The cosmological argument asks why the chain of causes exists at all, and the debate is over where that question leads.

# Design argument
William Paley's *Natural Theology* (1802) opens on a heath. Stub your toe on a stone and you might assume it had always been there. Find a watch, with gears fitted to tell the time, and you'd infer a watchmaker. Living things, Paley argued, show far more intricate fitting of parts to purposes, such as the eye.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Watch has fitted parts", "So it has a designer", "Eye has fitted parts", "So it has a designer"]}
### Hume got there first
In his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779), published before Paley wrote, Hume raised objections.
• The analogy between universes and watches is weak; we've seen watches made, never a universe
• Design wouldn't show one designer, or a good one
• Nature also contains waste and apparent flaws
### Darwin's alternative
Charles Darwin admired Paley as a student. His *Origin of Species* (1859) offered a mechanism: random variation plus natural selection, acting over millions of years, can produce apparent design without a designer.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Variation arises", "Some survive better", "They breed more", "Traits spread"]}
### The modern version: fine-tuning
Today's version focuses on physics. Some constants of nature seem to fall in narrow ranges that allow stars, chemistry and life. Supporters see design; critics reply with the **multiverse** (many universes, we're in a life-friendly one), the anthropic principle (we could only observe such a universe), or doubt that we can measure such probabilities.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Design", "b": "constants chosen for life"}, {"a": "Multiverse", "b": "many tries, one works"}, {"a": "Brute fact", "b": "no further explanation"}]}
!? Many religious believers, including official Catholic teaching since the mid-20th century, accept evolution as compatible with belief in a creator.
> The design argument moved from watches and eyes to the laws of physics, and the debate moved with it.

# Ontological argument
Most arguments for God start from the world. The ontological argument starts from the idea of God alone. Anselm, an Italian-born monk who became Archbishop of Canterbury, set it out in his *Proslogion* around 1078.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["God: greatest conceivable", "Exists in the mind", "Real existence is greater", "So God exists in reality"]}
### Gaunilo's island
A fellow monk, Gaunilo, replied at once. Imagine the most perfect island. By the same reasoning it must exist, which is absurd. Anselm answered that the argument works only for a being whose greatness has no limit; islands have no intrinsic maximum.
### Kant's objection
In 1781 Kant argued that existence isn't a property that adds to a concept. A hundred real coins contain no more coins than a hundred imagined ones. "Exists" says that the concept has an instance, not what it is like.
ex: Concept of 100 thalers | Add "exists" | Still 100 thalers in the concept | Only your wallet changes, not the idea
### Later versions
Descartes offered his own version in 1641. In the 20th century Alvin Plantinga gave a modal version: if it is even possible that a maximally great being exists, then one exists necessarily. Critics answer that you could equally run it backwards from the possibility that no such being exists.

!? The logician Kurt Gödel wrote a formal version of the ontological argument; in 2013 computer scientists checked its logic with automated theorem provers, confirming it is valid given its axioms, though whether those axioms are true is another matter.
> The ontological argument tries to prove God from a definition, and most of the debate is over whether definitions can do that.

# Pascal's wager
Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a mathematician who helped found probability theory with Pierre de Fermat in 1654. In notes later published as the *Pensées* (1670), he applied that thinking to belief in God.
### The bet
Reason, Pascal said, can't settle whether God exists. But you must live one way or another, so treat it as a bet.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Believe, God exists", "b": "infinite gain"}, {"a": "Believe, no God", "b": "small, finite loss"}, {"a": "Disbelieve, God exists", "b": "infinite loss"}, {"a": "Disbelieve, no God", "b": "small, finite gain"}]}
ex: Any probability above zero | times an infinite reward | gives infinite expected value | so believing seems the better bet
### Strongest objections
• **Many gods**: the wager works equally for any god promising infinite reward, and they can't all be satisfied
• **Choosing belief**: you can't just decide to believe something. Pascal replied: act as believers do and belief may follow
• **The wrong motive**: would a good God reward faith adopted out of self-interest?
• **Infinity in maths**: infinite rewards make the calculation strange, since any tiny chance of any infinite prize dominates everything
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Objections", "items": ["Which god?", "Can belief be chosen?", "Is the motive worthy?", "Is infinity usable?"]}
### Its legacy
Whatever its success, the wager is an early example of **decision theory**: choosing under uncertainty by weighing chances and payoffs. Some defenders recast it more modestly, as a reason to explore religious practice rather than a proof.
!? At 19, Pascal built one of the first mechanical calculators, the Pascaline, to help his father, a tax official, with arithmetic.
> Pascal's wager turns faith into a bet under uncertainty, and critics ask whether faith is really that kind of thing.

# The problem of evil again
The problem goes back at least to ancient Greece; a version is traditionally attributed to Epicurus. If God is all-powerful, all-knowing and wholly good, why does terrible suffering exist?
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "If God is...", "items": ["All-powerful: can stop it", "All-knowing: knows of it", "All-good: wants to stop it", "Yet suffering exists"]}
### Two forms
• **Logical problem**: God and evil are flatly contradictory
• **Evidential problem**: the amount and kind of suffering make God unlikely. William Rowe's 1979 example is a fawn dying slowly in a forest fire, seen by no one
### Main responses
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Free will", "b": "love needs real choice"}, {"a": "Soul-making", "b": "growth needs challenge"}, {"a": "Sceptical theism", "b": "we can't see God's reasons"}]}
Many philosophers, including some atheists, accept that Alvin Plantinga's free-will defence answers the strict logical problem: a good God might allow evil if free creatures are worth it. The evidential problem remains hotly debated. Natural evils, like disease and earthquakes, are hardest to explain by human choice.
### Lisbon, 1755
On All Saints' Day, 1 November 1755, an earthquake, tsunami and fire destroyed much of Lisbon and killed tens of thousands. The disaster shook European thought. Voltaire wrote a poem and then *Candide* (1759) mocking Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. Rousseau replied that people, not God, built crowded multi-storey houses.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1710", "l": "Leibniz: best world"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Lisbon earthquake"}, {"y": "1756", "l": "Voltaire's poem"}, {"y": "1759", "l": "Candide"}]}
> Suffering is the hardest test for belief in a good God, and both believers and critics take it seriously.

# Faith and reason
How should faith relate to evidence and argument? Thinkers have given at least four broad answers.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Harmony", "b": "faith and reason agree"}, {"a": "Evidentialism", "b": "believe only on evidence"}, {"a": "Fideism", "b": "faith goes beyond reason"}, {"a": "Properly basic", "b": "belief needs no argument"}]}
### Harmony
Aquinas held that faith and reason come from the same God and can't truly conflict; reason can support faith up to a point. In 1998 Pope John Paul II's letter *Fides et Ratio* compared them to two wings on which the human spirit rises.
### Evidentialism
In 1877 the mathematician W. K. Clifford argued it is wrong always and everywhere to believe anything on insufficient evidence. His example: a shipowner who convinces himself an old ship is safe is guilty when it sinks, sincere or not.
### Fideism and leaps
Søren Kierkegaard, the 19th-century Danish thinker, argued that faith is a passionate commitment that reason can't reach, a "leap". Faith with proof would no longer be faith.
### Trust and basic belief
William James (1896) replied to Clifford: in some live choices, waiting for evidence is itself a choice. Alvin Plantinga argues belief in God can be rational without arguments, as belief in other minds or the past is. Many believers describe faith as trust in a person, like trusting a friend beyond what you can prove.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Faith as belief", "items": ["accept a claim", "needs support"]}, "r": {"t": "Faith as trust", "items": ["commit to a person", "risk involved"]}}
### Objections
Evidentialists say "trust" and "leaps" could justify any belief. Their critics answer that strict evidentialism would rule out much ordinary knowledge too, and may not meet its own standard.
> The debate is less about whether faith is irrational than about what kind of rationality faith requires.

# Atheism and agnosticism
The words are often confused. **Theism** is belief that God exists. **Atheism** is belief that no god exists or, in a weaker sense, lack of belief in God. **Agnosticism**, a term coined by the biologist T. H. Huxley in 1869, holds that we don't or can't know.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "About belief", "items": ["theist", "atheist"]}, "r": {"t": "About knowledge", "items": ["gnostic: claims to know", "agnostic: doesn't"]}}
On this map, someone can be an agnostic atheist (doesn't believe, doesn't claim to know) or an agnostic theist (believes without claiming knowledge).
### Russell's teapot
In 1952 Bertrand Russell imagined a china teapot orbiting the Sun between Earth and Mars, too small to see with telescopes. No one could disprove it, yet no one should feel obliged to believe it. The point: the **burden of proof** lies with whoever makes the claim.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Claim: hidden teapot", "Can't be disproved", "No evidence for it", "Belief not required"]}
### Objections to the teapot
Theists reply that God is not like a teapot. A teapot would be an odd extra object; God is proposed as an explanation of why anything exists, with arguments and reported experiences in support. Whether those count as evidence is the real dispute. Others note that "no god exists" is also a claim needing support.
### Portugal's best-known atheist
José Saramago, winner of the 1998 Nobel Prize in Literature, was an outspoken atheist. In 1992 the Portuguese government blocked his novel *The Gospel According to Jesus Christ* from a European literary prize shortlist; he moved to Lanzarote soon after.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Gnostic atheist: knows none", "Agnostic: doesn't know", "Gnostic theist: knows God"]}
!! Agnostics sit exactly halfway between belief and unbelief => Agnosticism is about knowledge; agnostics may lean either way
> The key question isn't only who believes what, but who must show their reasons.

# Religious experience
Across cultures and centuries, people report encounters with something sacred: a presence, a call, a vision, a sense of oneness. If these are genuine perceptions, they could be evidence of God. If they're products of the brain, they may not be.
### William James
The American psychologist William James gave the Gifford Lectures in Edinburgh, published in 1902 as *The Varieties of Religious Experience*. He studied first-hand accounts and judged experiences by their fruits in people's lives. He listed four marks of mystical experience.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ineffable", "b": "can't be put into words"}, {"a": "Noetic", "b": "feels like knowledge"}, {"a": "Transient", "b": "doesn't last long"}, {"a": "Passive", "b": "happens to you"}]}
### Fátima
In 1917, three shepherd children near Fátima reported visions of the Virgin Mary. On 13 October, a large crowd, estimated in the tens of thousands, gathered and many reported seeing the Sun move. The Catholic Church approved the visions in 1930. Sceptics propose optical effects of staring towards the Sun and crowd expectation. Fátima remains one of the world's major pilgrimage sites.
### The arguments

• Defenders reply that finding brain activity doesn't settle the cause: you also use your brain to see a real tree

# Why something?
In 1714 Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz asked what he called the first question: why is there something rather than nothing? After all, he noted, nothing is simpler and easier than something.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why something?", "items": ["A necessary being", "A brute fact", "Physical laws", "Question is confused"]}
### Leibniz's answer
Leibniz relied on the **principle of sufficient reason**: everything has an explanation. Contingent things, which could have failed to exist, can't explain themselves. So the ultimate explanation must lie in a necessary being, which he identified with God.
### The brute-fact answer
In a 1948 BBC radio debate, the philosopher Frederick Copleston, a Jesuit priest, pressed Leibniz's argument. Bertrand Russell replied that the universe is simply there, and that's all. Critics of Russell say this abandons the search for explanation; supporters say every chain of explanation must stop somewhere.
### Physics
Some physicists suggest a universe could arise from a quantum vacuum. Philosophers reply that a vacuum governed by laws of physics is already something, so the question just moves to why those laws exist.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Our universe", "Quantum fields and laws", "Why those laws?", "Why anything?"]}
### A question to sit with
Some philosophers think the question may be badly formed: "nothing" isn't a state that could have obtained. Others, like Martin Heidegger, called it the most fundamental question of all.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Seek explanation", "items": ["sufficient reason", "God or deep law"]}, "r": {"t": "Accept the limit", "items": ["brute fact", "question confused"]}}
!? Wittgenstein wrote in 1921 that what is mystical is not how the world is, but that it exists.
> The oldest question may also be the hardest: not what the world is like, but why there is a world at all.

# What is justice?
Plato's *Republic*, written around 375 BC, opens at a dinner party in the Piraeus, Athens' port. The guests try to define justice. Cephalus says it means paying your debts and telling the truth; Socrates quickly finds exceptions, such as returning a weapon to a friend who has gone mad. Then Thrasymachus, a sharp-tongued teacher of rhetoric, loses patience.
### The challenge
His claim is cynical but hard to dismiss. Rulers write the laws to suit themselves and call obeying them "justice". So the just person is a fool who serves someone else's advantage, while the clever tyrant who breaks the rules and gets away with it lives best.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["The strong make the laws", "Laws serve the strong", "Obeying them = 'justice'", "Justice helps the strong"]}
In Book 2, Plato's brothers Glaucon and Adeimantus sharpen the problem. Glaucon tells the myth of the **Ring of Gyges**, which makes its wearer invisible. Would anyone stay honest if they could never be caught? If not, justice is only a deal we accept out of fear.
### Plato's answer
Socrates replies with an analogy between the city and the soul. Each has three parts, and justice is each part doing its own job, in harmony.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Reason", "b": "rulers: wisdom"}, {"a": "Spirit", "b": "guardians: courage"}, {"a": "Appetite", "b": "producers: moderation"}]}
On this view, injustice is a kind of sickness of the soul, so the unjust person is worse off even if never caught. Many readers find the argument brilliant but not fully convincing, and the ideal city it describes, run by philosopher-kings, troubles modern defenders of democracy.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Thrasymachus", "items": ["justice = power", "rules are a trick"]}, "r": {"t": "Socrates", "items": ["justice = harmony", "good for its owner"]}}
> The *Republic* asks a question we still face: is being just good for you, or just good for others?
!? Glaucon's idea that justice is a bargain made by people who fear being harmed is an early version of the **social contract**, later developed by Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau.

# Distributive justice
Distributive justice asks how the benefits and burdens of social life should be shared: income, wealth, jobs, healthcare, education, and also taxes and risks. Every society answers it somehow, through tax rules, wages, inheritance law and public services, even if no one ever states the principle out loud.
### The main candidate principles
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Equality", "b": "everyone gets the same"}, {"a": "Need", "b": "more to those who need more"}, {"a": "Merit", "b": "reward effort or talent"}, {"a": "Free exchange", "b": "whatever fair trades produce"}]}
Add two more: **utilitarianism**, which picks whatever distribution produces the most total well-being, and **prioritarianism**, which gives extra weight to helping those worst off.
### Each one has a cost
• Strict equality ignores that people work different hours and make different choices
• Need is hard to measure and may reward dependence, critics say
• Merit is tangled with luck: talent, health and family background are not earned
• Free exchange can leave some people with almost nothing
Aristotle, in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, already framed the puzzle: justice means treating equals equally and unequals in proportion to their differences. The hard part is deciding which differences count.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Share a bonus by…", "items": ["equal slices", "hours worked", "family needs", "sales made"]}
ex: Team bonus of €3,000, three workers | Equality: €1,000 each | By hours (40, 30, 20 h): €1,333, €1,000, €667
In practice, most European welfare states mix principles: wages largely reward market value, progressive taxes and benefits respond to need, and schools and health services aim at equal access.
> There is no neutral way to share: every rule of distribution expresses a view about what people deserve.
!? Karl Marx's famous slogan "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs" (1875) is a need principle; he thought it could work only in a far richer future society.

# Rawls' difference principle
John Rawls (1921–2002), a Harvard philosopher, published *A Theory of Justice* in 1971. It revived political philosophy in the English-speaking world and is still one of the most cited works in the field.
### The veil of ignorance
Rawls asks you to imagine choosing the rules of society from an **original position**, behind a **veil of ignorance**. You don't know whether you will be rich or poor, talented or not, healthy or ill, which sex or religion you will have. Since you can't rig the rules for yourself, whatever you choose should be fair.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hide your place in society", "Choose the basic rules", "Can't favour yourself", "Rules come out fair"]}
### Two principles
Rawls argued people would choose:
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["1. Equal basic liberties", "2a. Fair equality of opportunity", "2b. Difference principle"]}
The first comes first: you can't trade away free speech or the vote for more money. The **difference principle** then allows inequality only if it makes the worst-off group better off than they would be under equality.
ex: Society A: poorest earn €900, richest €1,000 | Society B: poorest earn €1,200, richest €4,000 | Rawls prefers B, because the worst off do better
The reasoning is sometimes called **maximin**: maximise the minimum. If you might end up at the bottom, you protect the bottom.
### Critics from several sides
• Utilitarians such as John Harsanyi said rational people might gamble for a higher average
• Robert Nozick said it ignores how holdings came about
• G. A. Cohen, from the left, asked why talented people should need incentives at all
• Communitarians like Michael Sandel said we cannot choose as people stripped of all ties
> Rawls tests a rule by asking how it looks to someone who might end up at the bottom.
!! Rawls wanted everyone to have the same income => He accepted inequality, provided it improves the position of the least advantaged

# Nozick's entitlement
Robert Nozick (1938–2002), Rawls' colleague at Harvard, replied in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974). Where Rawls looks at the final pattern of who has what, Nozick looks at history: how did people come to have it?
### The entitlement theory
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Just acquisition of unowned things", "Just transfer: gift, trade, wages", "Rectify past injustice"]}
If every step in a chain of holdings was fair, the result is fair, whatever it looks like. Nozick called theories like Rawls' "patterned" or "end-state", and his own "historical".
### The Wilt Chamberlain argument
Imagine society starts with your favourite fair distribution. Then a million fans each happily pay 25 cents to watch the basketball star Wilt Chamberlain play. He ends up with $250,000, far more than anyone else.
ex: 1,000,000 fans × $0.25 | = $250,000 for Chamberlain | Every transfer was voluntary
Each step was free and fair, so how can the result be unjust? Nozick's point: liberty upsets patterns. Keeping any pattern would require constantly interfering with people's free choices.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rawls", "items": ["looks at the outcome", "helps the worst off"]}, "r": {"t": "Nozick", "items": ["looks at history", "protects free choice"]}}
### Taxation and the minimal state
Nozick argued that taxing earnings to redistribute them is "on a par with forced labour": if the state takes the income from some of your working hours, it is in effect making you work those hours for others. He defended only a minimal "night-watchman" state that protects against force, theft and fraud.
### Objections
Critics point out that few real holdings have clean histories: land was often taken by conquest, colonisation or fraud, so the principle of rectification could demand sweeping redistribution. Others say taxes are not like forced labour, because you can choose how much and where to work. Nozick himself later wrote that parts of his early view were "seriously inadequate".
> For Nozick, justice is about how you got what you have, not whether the overall pattern looks equal.
!? Nozick's book also includes the "experience machine" thought experiment, now a classic argument against the idea that pleasure is all that matters.

# Human rights
The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** was adopted by the UN General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948. It came in direct response to the Second World War and the Holocaust, which showed that a state could legally strip its own citizens of everything.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1789", "l": "French Declaration"}, {"y": "1948", "l": "UDHR adopted in Paris"}, {"y": "1950", "l": "European Convention"}, {"y": "1966", "l": "Two UN Covenants"}]}
### Who wrote it
A drafting committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt included the French lawyer René Cassin, the Chinese philosopher P. C. Chang, the Lebanese thinker Charles Malik and the Canadian John Humphrey, who wrote the first draft. They argued over whether rights came from God, nature or human agreement, and deliberately left the foundation open so that people of many beliefs could sign up.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "30", "l": "articles"}, {"v": "48", "l": "votes in favour"}, {"v": "8", "l": "abstentions, none against"}]}
### What it contains
• Civil and political rights: life, liberty, fair trial, free speech, religion, voting
• Economic and social rights: work, fair pay, rest, education, health care, social security
• A ban on torture and slavery, and equality without distinction of race, sex or religion
The Declaration itself is not a treaty, so it is not legally binding. It inspired binding treaties, such as the two UN Covenants of 1966 and the **European Convention on Human Rights** (1950), enforced by the court in Strasbourg. Portugal, under the Estado Novo dictatorship in 1948, joined the UN only in 1955; after the 1974 revolution, its 1976 Constitution explicitly says rights must be read in line with the Declaration.
### Debates
Some critics argue that "universal" rights reflect Western ideas; defenders reply that the drafters came from many traditions and that people across cultures appeal to them. Others question whether economic rights are true rights or goals.
> The UDHR made a radical claim: you have rights simply because you are human, not because a state grants them.
!? The UDHR has been translated into over 500 languages, more than almost any other document.

# Rights and duties
A right is not just a nice thing to have. If you have a right, someone else is bound to act, or refrain from acting, in a certain way. Rights and duties come in pairs.
### Hohfeld's map of rights
The American legal theorist Wesley Hohfeld (around 1913–1917) showed that "right" covers several different relationships.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Claim", "b": "others must act or refrain"}, {"a": "Liberty", "b": "you're free to act"}, {"a": "Power", "b": "you can change rules"}, {"a": "Immunity", "b": "others can't change yours"}]}
For example, your right to be paid is a claim against your employer; your right to walk in a public park is a liberty; the right to make a will is a power.
### Negative and positive rights
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Negative rights", "items": ["don't torture me", "don't silence me"]}, "r": {"t": "Positive rights", "items": ["educate me", "treat me when ill"]}}
**Negative rights** require others to hold back. **Positive rights** require someone to provide something, which costs money and needs institutions. Portugal, for instance, has made schooling compulsory until age 18, which turns the right to education into concrete duties for the state, schools and parents.
The distinction is useful but blurry. Protecting even a negative right, like freedom from assault, needs police and courts paid for by taxes.
### Who holds the duty?
The philosopher Onora O'Neill warned that a right with no clear duty-bearer is only a "manifesto right": a statement of hope. If everyone has a right to food, who exactly must feed whom? Defenders of welfare rights reply that states and the international community are the duty-bearers.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["You claim a right", "Someone owes a duty", "An institution enforces it"]}
> Ask of any right: who must do what, for whom, and who makes sure they do?
!! Rights come free => Every right creates duties, and many rights need money and institutions to be real

# Punishment
Punishment means deliberately making someone suffer a loss, such as prison, a fine or community work, for breaking a rule. Since that would normally be wrong, it needs a justification. Philosophers group the answers into a few families.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why punish?", "items": ["Retribution", "Deterrence", "Protection", "Reform"]}
### Looking back or looking forward
**Retributivists**, such as Kant, look back at the crime: the offender deserves punishment in proportion to the wrong. **Consequentialists**, such as Bentham, look forward: punishment is justified only if it prevents more harm than it causes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Retribution", "items": ["looks back", "fits the crime"]}, "r": {"t": "Deterrence", "items": ["looks forward", "prevents more crime"]}}
Each view faces a hard case. Pure deterrence could, in principle, justify punishing an innocent scapegoat if it scared others. Pure retribution can demand suffering even when it helps no one.
### What deters?
The Italian thinker Cesare Beccaria argued in *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) that punishment should be certain, prompt and proportionate, not cruel. Modern research broadly agrees that the chance of being caught deters more than the harshness of the sentence.
• **Incapacitation**: prison stops someone offending while inside
• **Rehabilitation**: education and therapy aim to make reoffending less likely
• **Expression**: punishment publicly states that society condemns the act
### A Portuguese story
Portugal was an early abolitionist. It ended the death penalty for civil crimes in 1867 and life imprisonment in 1884. Today the maximum prison sentence is 25 years.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1764", "l": "Beccaria's book"}, {"y": "1867", "l": "Portugal ends death pen."}, {"y": "1884", "l": "Portugal ends life terms"}]}
> Every theory of punishment must explain why it is right to harm someone on purpose.
!? Victor Hugo wrote a letter in 1867 congratulating Portugal on abolishing the death penalty, calling it an example to Europe.

# Restorative justice
Ordinary criminal justice asks three questions: what law was broken, who did it, and what punishment do they deserve? Restorative justice asks different ones: who was harmed, what do they need, and who should repair it?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Retributive", "items": ["crime against state", "what's the penalty?"]}, "r": {"t": "Restorative", "items": ["harm to people", "how to repair it?"]}}
### How it works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Offender admits the harm", "Both agree to meet", "Victim explains impact", "Agree how to make amends"]}
A trained facilitator prepares both sides. Meetings are voluntary, and the offender must accept responsibility. Repair might mean an apology, paying for damage, unpaid work or a commitment to treatment. Families and community members often join in.
### Where it comes from
Modern practice is often traced to a 1974 case in Kitchener, Canada, where two young vandals met their victims to apologise and pay back damage. New Zealand made family group conferences central to its youth justice system in 1989, drawing on Māori traditions. Howard Zehr's book *Changing Lenses* (1990) gave the movement its theory. Portugal introduced victim-offender mediation for some less serious crimes in 2007.
### What the evidence suggests
Reviews of trials in the UK, Australia and the US, such as those led by Lawrence Sherman and Heather Strang, found that victims who took part were generally more satisfied and less fearful than those who went through court alone. Effects on reoffending are more modest and vary by crime and setting.
• Strength: victims get answers to questions like "why me?"
• Strength: offenders face the human cost of what they did
• Limit: not suitable when a victim feels unsafe or pressured
• Limit: critics worry it can seem soft or inconsistent
> Restorative justice treats crime as harm to people and relationships, not only as a broken rule.
!! Restorative justice means no punishment => It often runs alongside a sentence, and can be more demanding than a fine

# Global justice
In 1971 millions of refugees fled East Bengal (now Bangladesh) amid war and famine. The Australian philosopher Peter Singer responded with an essay, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), that is still assigned in ethics classes everywhere.
### The argument
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Suffering is bad", "We can prevent some cheaply", "Distance doesn't matter", "So we ought to give"]}
The core principle: if you can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it. Ruining expensive shoes to save a drowning child is obvious. Singer argues that a child dying of a preventable disease far away is morally the same case, since today we can help at a distance just as surely.
### Charity or duty?
Singer's most radical claim is that giving is not generous extra credit but a duty. Spending money on luxuries while children die of preventable causes is, on his view, wrong. His ideas helped inspire the **effective altruism** movement, which asks which charities save the most lives per euro.
ex: Rough estimates by charity evaluators | A few thousand euros can save a life | via malaria nets or vaccines
### Replies
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Demandingness", "b": "morality can't ask that much"}, {"a": "Special ties", "b": "family and nation come first"}, {"a": "Institutions", "b": "fix rules, not just give"}]}
Some argue a moral theory that asks you to give until you are nearly as poor as those you help is too demanding to be believable. Others, like Thomas Pogge, shift the focus: rich countries help shape trade rules and debt that keep others poor, so the duty is not just to rescue but to stop causing harm. Still others defend special duties to fellow citizens.
> Singer's pond asks whether a child's distance from you changes how much their life matters.
!? Singer later proposed a gentler sliding scale, starting with a small percentage of income; the pledge group Giving What We Can asks members to give 10%.

# Fair sharing
"I cut, you choose" is one of the oldest fairness tricks. A version appears in the Book of Genesis, where Abraham divides the land and lets Lot choose his part. It works with no referee and no trust.
### Why it works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cutter splits in two", "Halves look equal to them", "Chooser takes the best", "Neither envies the other"]}
The cutter has every reason to make the halves equal by their own standards, since they will get whichever is left. The chooser picks the piece they prefer, so they can't feel cheated either. Mathematicians call this **envy-free**: nobody would rather swap.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Proportional", "b": "each gets at least 1/n"}, {"a": "Envy-free", "b": "no one wants another's share"}, {"a": "Efficient", "b": "no swap helps everyone"}]}
### More than two people
Sharing fairly gets much harder with more people. Hugo Steinhaus and his Polish colleagues worked on it in the 1940s. A neat envy-free method for three people was found around 1960 by John Selfridge and John Conway. A procedure for any number of people came only in 1995, from Steven Brams and Alan Taylor, and it can take a very large number of steps.
### Real uses
The same idea is written into the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (1982): a company applying to mine the deep seabed proposes two areas of similar value, and the international authority picks one to reserve for developing countries. Divorce settlements and inheritance disputes sometimes use related methods.
### Link to philosophy
"I cut, you choose" is a tiny version of Rawls' veil of ignorance: if you don't know which piece you'll get, you have reason to make every piece fair.
> Good procedures can create fairness even between people who don't trust each other.
!! Fair means everyone values the pieces the same way => Envy-free methods work even when people value pieces differently, like icing versus sponge

# What is time?
Augustine's famous admission comes from Book 11 of his *Confessions* (about 400). It captures a strange fact: we use time every moment, measure it to the billionth of a second, and still can't say what it is.
### Ancient answers
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Aristotle", "b": "the number of change"}, {"a": "Augustine", "b": "a stretching of the mind"}, {"a": "Newton", "b": "flows on by itself"}, {"a": "Leibniz", "b": "just the order of events"}]}
Aristotle argued that time cannot exist without change: if nothing at all happened, there would be no time. Augustine located time in the soul, as memory, attention and expectation. Isaac Newton disagreed with both: for him, absolute time flows evenly whether or not anything happens. His rival Leibniz replied that time is only the order of events, with no existence of its own.
### McTaggart's puzzle
In 1908 the British philosopher J. M. E. McTaggart pointed out two ways of ordering events.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "A-series", "items": ["past, present, future", "keeps changing"]}, "r": {"t": "B-series", "items": ["earlier, later than", "never changes"]}}
Your birth is now past, but it was once future: that changes. Yet your birth is always earlier than your first day of school: that never changes. McTaggart argued that time needs the A-series, but that the A-series is contradictory, so time is unreal. Few accept his conclusion, but his two series still frame the debate.
### What physics says
Since Einstein, time is part of a four-dimensional **spacetime**, and clocks run at different rates depending on speed and gravity. GPS satellites must correct for this every day, or positions would drift by kilometres.
> We measure time with astonishing precision, yet philosophers and physicists still disagree about what it is.
!? Since 1967, the second has been defined by atoms: 9,192,631,770 oscillations of radiation from caesium-133.

# Presentism vs eternalism
Do the Romans exist? Does your future grandchild? The answer depends on which theory of time you hold. There are three main options.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Presentism: only now exists", "Growing block: past and now", "Eternalism: all times exist"]}
### Presentism
Presentism matches common sense: dinosaurs *did* exist, tomorrow *will* exist, but only the present *does*. Its main problem is truth: if the past no longer exists, what makes "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" true now?
### Eternalism and the block universe
Eternalists say all times are equally real, just as all places are. "Now" is like "here": it picks out where you are, not a special place. The universe is a four-dimensional **block**, and nothing in it comes into or out of existence.
### Why relativity matters
Einstein's special relativity (1905) showed that whether two distant events are simultaneous depends on how you move. There is no single, universal "now" across space.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["No universal 'now'", "Others' 'now' differs", "Includes our future", "So future is real?"]}
Philosophers such as Hilary Putnam argued in the 1960s that this pushes us towards eternalism: if someone moving past you counts as present an event in your future, that event must already be real. Presentists reply that physics describes what we can measure, not what exists, or they pick out a preferred frame.
### The growing block
C. D. Broad proposed a middle way in 1923: the past and present exist, the future does not yet. Reality grows, slice by slice. Critics ask how you would know you are at the edge, and not stuck somewhere in the middle.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Presentism", "items": ["fits experience", "past is gone"]}, "r": {"t": "Eternalism", "items": ["fits relativity", "no flow of time"]}}
> The debate is about whether "now" is a feature of reality or just a label for where you happen to be.
!! Eternalism means the future is fixed by fate => It says the future exists, but your choices are part of what it contains

# The arrow of time
Film a billiard ball bouncing and play it backwards: it looks fine. Film an egg falling and smashing, then reverse it: everyone laughs. The basic laws of motion work in both directions, yet everyday processes clearly go one way. Why?
### Entropy
The answer most physicists give is **entropy**, roughly a measure of how many microscopic arrangements match what we see. A smashed egg has vastly more possible arrangements than an intact one, so random jostling almost always leads from order to disorder. The **second law of thermodynamics** says the total entropy of an isolated system tends to increase.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Few ordered states", "Random motion mixes", "Many disordered states", "Almost never goes back"]}
ex: Shuffle a sorted deck of 52 cards | About 8 × 10^67 possible orders | Chance of landing back in order: almost zero
### Why did it start low?
This only explains the arrow if the universe began in a very low-entropy state. The Big Bang was hot but extremely smooth, which counts as highly ordered when gravity is involved. Why the start was so special is unsolved; the philosopher David Albert calls the assumption the **Past Hypothesis**.
### Several arrows
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Thermodynamic", "b": "entropy increases"}, {"a": "Psychological", "b": "we remember the past"}, {"a": "Cosmological", "b": "the universe expands"}, {"a": "Causal", "b": "causes come first"}]}
Many think the others follow from the thermodynamic one. Memory, for example, is a physical record, and making a record increases entropy, so records can only be of the past.
> Time's direction may come not from the laws of physics but from the very special state the universe started in.
!? The phrase "arrow of time" was coined by the British astronomer Arthur Eddington in 1927.

# Zeno's arrow
Zeno of Elea (about 490–430 BC) was a student of Parmenides. He invented paradoxes to defend his teacher's claim that change is an illusion. About 40 were known in antiquity; we know a handful mainly through Aristotle's *Physics*.
### The arrow
At any single instant, an arrow in flight occupies a space exactly its own size. In that instant it is not moving: there's no time to move in. But the flight is made only of instants. So, Zeno concludes, the arrow never moves.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Time is made of instants", "At each, arrow is still", "Still + still + still…", "So no motion at all?"]}
### His other famous paradoxes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dichotomy", "b": "halfway, then half again…"}, {"a": "Achilles", "b": "can't catch the tortoise"}, {"a": "Stadium", "b": "half a time equals double"}]}
### The calculus answer
Calculus, developed by Newton and Leibniz in the late 1600s and made rigorous in the 1800s, defines speed at an instant as a **limit**: the distance covered divided by time, as the time interval shrinks towards zero. Motion is not something that happens *in* an instant; it is a relation between positions at different instants.
= speed at t = limit of (change in position ÷ change in time) as time interval → 0
ex: Arrow moves 6 m in 0.1 s | 0.6 m in 0.01 s | Ratio stays 60 m/s, even at a single instant
Likewise, infinitely many steps can add up to a finite total: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + … = 1. So Achilles does catch the tortoise.
### Is it fully solved?
Many philosophers think the maths dissolves the puzzle. Others say it only describes motion and doesn't explain what makes an object at one instant "the same thing moving". Bertrand Russell called the solution an "at-at" theory: to move is just to be at different places at different times.
> Zeno's arrow forced thinkers to explain how something can change when every snapshot is still.
!? Physicists have found a real "quantum Zeno effect": watching an unstable system very often can slow down or stop its change.

# Heraclitus' river
Heraclitus of Ephesus (about 535–475 BC), on the coast of modern Turkey, wrote a single book that is now lost. About 130 fragments survive, quoted by later writers. His style was so cryptic that the ancients called him "the Obscure".
### Everything flows
His central idea is that change is not a defect of the world but its very nature. Fire was his favourite image: a flame stays "the same flame" only because fuel is constantly being consumed. Stability is a balance of opposing processes.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Fire", "Becomes sea", "Becomes earth", "Back to fire"]}
### What he probably said
The slogan "everything flows" (*panta rhei*) comes from later writers, not Heraclitus himself. Plato reports the river saying in the dialogue *Cratylus*. Many scholars think the original fragment said something subtler: different waters flow over those who step into the *same* rivers. The river stays the same river precisely because its water keeps changing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Popular version", "items": ["nothing stays the same", "no fixed identity"]}, "r": {"t": "Subtler reading", "items": ["same river", "because water flows"]}}
### Unity of opposites
Heraclitus also taught that opposites belong together: the road up and the road down are one and the same; sea water is good for fish but deadly for people. Behind all change lies the **logos**, a rational order or measure that most people fail to notice.
### Echoes today
The same puzzle reappears in the **Ship of Theseus**: if every plank is replaced over time, is it the same ship? Your body raises it too: many cells and molecules are replaced over the years, yet you remain you.
> For Heraclitus, things keep their identity not despite change but through it.
!! All your cells are replaced every seven years => Turnover varies hugely; some cells, such as most neurons and eye lens cells, last a lifetime

# Parmenides' stillness
Parmenides of Elea, a Greek town in southern Italy, was active around 500–450 BC. He wrote a poem, *On Nature*, of which about 160 lines survive. In it, a goddess shows a young man two paths: the Way of Truth and the Way of Opinion.
### The argument
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nothing can't be thought", "So nothing can't exist", "Change needs 'not yet'", "So change is impossible"]}
Parmenides reasons that "what is not" cannot even be thought or spoken about. But change means something coming from what it was not, and motion needs empty space, which is "nothing". So, by pure logic, there can be no coming into being, no destruction, no motion and no plurality. Reality is **one**, whole, unchanging and eternal. The world of the senses, full of change, is the Way of Opinion.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Heraclitus", "items": ["all is change", "trust the flux"]}, "r": {"t": "Parmenides", "items": ["change is illusion", "trust reason"]}}
### Why it mattered
Parmenides is often called the first philosopher to build an argument step by step and follow it wherever it led, even against the evidence of his eyes. Later thinkers tried to rescue change while respecting his logic.
• **Atomists** (Leucippus, Democritus): unchanging atoms move in a void; things change by rearrangement
• **Plato**: true reality is unchanging Forms; the changing world is a lesser copy
• **Empedocles**: four eternal elements that mix and separate
### A modern echo
Some see a resemblance between Parmenides and the eternalist "block universe", in which all times exist at once and nothing truly comes into being. Physics' conservation laws, which say energy is neither created nor destroyed, also echo his idea that nothing comes from nothing.
> Parmenides showed how far pure reasoning can push us, even to deny what we see every day.
!? Plato's dialogue *Parmenides* imagines the old philosopher, then about 65, meeting a young Socrates and tearing apart his theory of Forms.

# Why is there now?
In the equations of physics, every moment is on an equal footing. There is a time coordinate, *t*, but nothing marks one value as "now", just as nothing on a map marks one place as "here". Yet in experience, the present feels utterly special, and seems to move.
### Two views
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "A-theory", "items": ["now is objective", "time really passes"]}, "r": {"t": "B-theory", "items": ["now is like 'here'", "passage is felt"]}}
A-theorists hold that there is an objective present and a genuine flow, even if physics doesn't capture it. B-theorists say "now" is just the time at which a thought occurs, as "here" is where the speaker is; the sense of flow comes from how minds work.
### How the brain builds "now"
Your experienced present isn't an instant. Light, sound and touch reach the brain at different speeds and are processed at different rates, yet the brain binds them together.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Senses arrive at delays", "Brain waits and binds", "Feels like one moment", "Memory makes it 'past'"]}
• William James (1890) called this window the **specious present**
• Psychologists often estimate it at around 2 to 3 seconds, though figures vary
• What you see as "now" is already a fraction of a second old
### Is passage an illusion?
Some philosophers argue the feeling of flow arises because memories pile up in one direction: at each moment you remember more of the past and none of the future. That asymmetry, tied to entropy, could explain why time *feels* like it moves, even if the world itself is a static block. Others insist that our experience of change is too basic to explain away.
> The puzzle is not how long time lasts but why one moment, this one, feels special.
!? Rudolf Carnap recalled that Einstein told him the problem of the Now worried him seriously, because physics seemed unable to capture it.

# Possible worlds
"You could have become an astronaut." "Two plus two could not have been five." Statements like these talk about what is **possible** and **necessary**. Philosophers call this modality, and "possible worlds" are their main tool for thinking about it.
### The basic idea
A possible world is a complete way things could have been. The actual world is one of them. Then:
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Possible", "b": "true in at least one world"}, {"a": "Necessary", "b": "true in every world"}, {"a": "Impossible", "b": "true in no world"}, {"a": "Contingent", "b": "true in some, false in some"}]}
### From Leibniz to Kripke
Leibniz (1710) said God surveyed all possible worlds and created the best one. Voltaire mocked this in *Candide* (1759), partly in response to the 1755 Lisbon earthquake. In the late 1950s and 1960s, the logician Saul Kripke used possible worlds to give precise meaning to modal logic, and his lectures *Naming and Necessity* (1970) showed how they clarify what names refer to.
### Are they real?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "David Lewis", "items": ["worlds are real", "just not ours"]}, "r": {"t": "Most others", "items": ["worlds are models", "like descriptions"]}}
David Lewis (1986) argued, startlingly, that all possible worlds exist as concretely as ours; "actual" just means "this one". Most philosophers prefer to treat them as abstract descriptions or useful fictions, like ideal models in physics.
### Everyday uses
• Counterfactuals: "If I'd left earlier, I'd have caught the train"
• Law and blame: would the harm have happened anyway?
• Decision-making: comparing the futures your choices could lead to
ex: "If the 1755 earthquake hadn't happened…" | Imagine the nearest world where it didn't | Ask what follows there
> Possible worlds turn vague talk about "could have been" into something we can reason about clearly.
!! Possible worlds are the same as the multiverse => Physics' many-worlds idea is a claim about reality; possible worlds are mainly a logical tool

# Death and the self
Epicurus (341–270 BC) ran a school in Athens called the Garden. His goal was **ataraxia**, freedom from mental disturbance, and he thought fear of death was one of its biggest enemies.
### Epicurus' argument
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bad = bad experience", "Dead = no experience", "So death isn't bad for you", "So don't fear it"]}
In his *Letter to Menoeceus*, he argues that good and bad lie in sensation. Death is the end of sensation. So while you exist, death is absent; when death is present, you don't exist. There is never a subject for death to harm.
### The symmetry argument
His Roman follower Lucretius added a second argument: you don't feel distress about the endless time before you were born, so why feel it about the endless time after you die?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before birth", "items": ["you didn't exist", "no one fears it"]}, "r": {"t": "After death", "items": ["you won't exist", "why fear it?"]}}
### The deprivation reply
Thomas Nagel, in his essay "Death" (1970), argued that death can harm you even though you never experience it. It takes away the good things you would otherwise have had. A person betrayed behind their back is harmed even if they never find out; likewise, death harms by **deprivation**. That would explain why an early death seems worse than a late one.
### Would immortality be better?
Bernard Williams (1973) argued the opposite worry: an endless life would eventually become unbearably boring, as your desires and character ran out. Others think that a good enough life could keep renewing itself.
• Epicurus: death is nothing to us
• Nagel: death is bad because it deprives
• Williams: mortality may be what gives life shape
> Whether death harms you depends on whether harm needs to be felt.
!? Some Roman tombstones bear the Epicurean-style epitaph "I was not, I was, I am not, I don't care", often shortened to its Latin initials.

# Living in time
Lucius Annaeus Seneca (about 4 BC – AD 65) was a Stoic philosopher, playwright and adviser to the emperor Nero. Around AD 49 he wrote *On the Shortness of Life* to his friend Paulinus. Its message is blunt: we don't have a short time to live, we waste a lot of it.
### Where the time goes
Seneca lists the culprits: chasing promotions, hosting people we don't like, worrying about the future, and letting others take our hours as if they were free. People guard their money and property, he observes, but give away their time without a thought.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Money", "items": ["counted carefully", "guarded"]}, "r": {"t": "Time", "items": ["rarely counted", "given away freely"]}}
### Doing the sums
ex: A life of about 81 years (Portugal) | 81 × 52 = about 4,200 weeks | Sleep takes roughly a third
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "weeks (rough, 81-year life)", "items": [{"l": "Whole life", "v": 4200.0, "t": "≈4,200"}, {"l": "Awake", "v": 2800.0, "t": "≈2,800", "hi": true}]}
The writer Oliver Burkeman built a whole book, *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021), on this arithmetic. The point is not panic but clarity: you can't do everything, so choose what matters.
### Seneca's advice
• Live now: postponing life until retirement gambles on a future you may not get
• Read and think: the great writers of the past are company anyone can keep
• Guard your attention: busy is not the same as living
• Use each day as a whole life, beginning and ending it with intention
### A fair note
Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome while praising simple living, and critics in his own time called him a hypocrite. He knew it, and replied that he was a student of virtue, not a master. His end was grim: in AD 65 Nero ordered him to take his own life.
> Life feels short mostly because of how we spend it, not how long it lasts.
!? Seneca's *Letters to Lucilius* begin with exactly this theme: claim back your time, because most of it is stolen or slips away unnoticed.
