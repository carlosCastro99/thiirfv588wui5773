== ph-energy

# Energy changes form
The rule that energy is never created or destroyed is called the **conservation of energy**, or the first law of thermodynamics. It was pinned down in the 1840s, when James Joule showed that a fixed amount of mechanical work always produces the same amount of heat. Since then no experiment has ever broken it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Chemical (battery)", "Electrical (wire)", "Light + heat (bulb)", "Heat in the room"]}
### Where the energy ends up
Follow any chain of conversions far enough and it ends as low-grade heat, spread thinly through the surroundings. The light from a lamp hits the walls and warms them slightly. The energy still exists, but it is too spread out to do useful work again.
### Efficiency
Efficiency is useful output divided by total input. A filament bulb gets about 15 lumens of light per watt; a good LED gets over 100. That is why switching a 60 W bulb for a 9 W LED gives the same light.
= efficiency = useful energy out ÷ total energy in × 100%
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. % of input that is useful", "items": [{"l": "Filament bulb", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Car petrol engine", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "LED lamp", "v": 35.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Electric motor", "v": 90.0}]}
ex: Bulb uses 60 J each second | 5% becomes light = 3 J | The other 57 J leaves as heat
!! Energy gets "used up" => It only changes form; what we lose is the useful, concentrated kind
> Energy is like money moving between accounts: the total never changes, but some of it always leaks into the "heat" account.
!? Nothing reaches 100%: even large electric motors, among the best machines we have, lose a few per cent as heat.

# Three ways heat moves
Heat always flows from hotter to colder. The three routes differ in what carries the energy: vibrating particles, moving fluid, or light itself.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Conduction", "b": "particles jostle neighbours"}, {"a": "Convection", "b": "warm fluid carries heat"}, {"a": "Radiation", "b": "infrared light, no medium"}]}
### Conduction
In a solid, hot atoms vibrate harder and pass that jiggle to their neighbours. Metals conduct best because their free electrons carry energy quickly. Copper conducts heat about 16,000 times better than still air, which is why pan bases are metal and handles are often wood or plastic.
### Convection
Liquids and gases expand when warmed, become less dense and rise; cooler fluid sinks to take their place. This sets up loops called convection currents. A radiator mostly heats a room this way, and the same process drives sea breezes and much of the weather.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Fluid heated at bottom", "Expands and rises", "Cools at the top", "Sinks back down"]}
### Radiation
Every warm object gives off infrared light. It needs no material at all, which is how the Sun's heat crosses 150 million km of empty space. Dark, matt surfaces emit and absorb it well; shiny ones reflect it.
> Conduction needs contact, convection needs a fluid that can flow, and radiation needs nothing at all.
!? A cool tiled floor and a rug at the same temperature feel different because tile conducts heat away from your feet much faster.

# Heat pumps move heat
A heat pump is a fridge run in reverse for your house. Instead of pulling heat out of a small box and dumping it into the kitchen, it pulls heat out of the outside air, ground or water and dumps it indoors.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cold refrigerant takes heat", "Compressor squeezes it", "Hot gas releases heat inside", "Expansion valve cools it"]}
### Why it beats a heater
An electric heater turns 1 kWh of electricity into exactly 1 kWh of heat. A heat pump uses that electricity only to move heat, so most of what it delivers is "free" heat taken from outside. The ratio of heat delivered to electricity used is the **coefficient of performance (COP)**.
= COP = heat delivered ÷ electricity used
ex: Heat pump uses 1 kWh | Takes 2.5 kWh from outside air | Delivers 3.5 kWh: COP = 3.5
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "kWh of heat per kWh of electricity", "items": [{"l": "Electric heater", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Heat pump, cold day", "v": 2.5}, {"l": "Heat pump, mild day", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}]}
### What lowers the COP
• The bigger the gap between outside and inside temperature, the harder it works
• Low-temperature radiators or underfloor heating suit heat pumps best
• Portugal's mild winters are kind to air-source heat pumps; they are also widely used here for hot water
!! Heat pumps don't work in winter => Even air at −10 °C holds heat they can extract, though less efficiently
> A heat pump is cheaper than a heater because it moves heat rather than making it.

# Specific heat
Specific heat capacity tells you how much energy one kilogram of a material needs to warm by one degree. Materials with a high value warm slowly and cool slowly; low-value ones change temperature fast.
= Q = m × c × ΔT
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Q = m × c × ΔT", "items": [{"s": "Q", "m": "energy, joules"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass, kg"}, {"s": "c", "m": "specific heat, J/kg°C"}, {"s": "ΔT", "m": "temperature rise, °C"}]}
ex: Kettle with 1 kg of water, 20 → 100 °C | Q = 1 × 4186 × 80 ≈ 335,000 J | A 2 kW kettle needs about 170 s, nearly 3 minutes
### Water is a champion
Water's value is about four times that of air (per kilogram) and roughly ten times that of iron. Its molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds, which soak up energy before the molecules can move faster.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "J per kg per °C (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Water", "v": 4186.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Olive oil", "v": 2000.0}, {"l": "Aluminium", "v": 900.0}, {"l": "Iron", "v": 450.0}]}
### Why it matters
• The sea warms slowly in spring and stays warm into autumn, which is why coastal towns like Viana do Castelo have milder winters than inland Trás-os-Montes
• Central heating uses water to carry heat around the house
• A hot-water bottle stays warm for hours
> High specific heat means slow to warm and slow to cool, which makes water a superb store of heat.
!? Hot oil in a pan heats up roughly twice as fast as the same mass of water on the same burner, because its specific heat is about half.

# Forms of energy
Physicists group energy by where it is stored or how it moves. These labels are bookkeeping: the same joule can sit in one store and then shift to another.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Energy", "items": ["Kinetic: moving things", "Potential: height, springs", "Internal: heat, chemical", "Radiant: light"]}
### Two formulas worth knowing
Kinetic energy grows with the square of speed, so doubling speed gives four times the energy. Gravitational potential energy grows simply with height.
= kinetic energy = ½ × m × v²   |   potential energy = m × g × h
ex: 1,000 kg car at 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) | ½ × 1000 × 13.9² ≈ 97,000 J | Same as dropping it from about 10 m
### Following the energy
A hydroelectric dam, such as Alqueva in the Alentejo, shows a chain clearly. Water held high has potential energy; falling, it gains kinetic energy; turbines turn that into electrical energy; your kettle turns it into thermal energy.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Water high up: potential", "Falling: kinetic", "Turbine: electrical", "Your home: heat, light"]}
### Nuclear and mass
Einstein's E = mc² shows mass itself is a store of energy. Nuclear power stations release a tiny fraction of it; a uranium fuel pellet the size of a fingertip holds roughly as much energy as a tonne of coal.
> Energy has many names but one currency: the joule.
!! Heat and temperature are the same thing => Temperature is how hot; heat is energy moving because of a temperature difference

# Food energy
A food "Calorie" with a capital C is a kilocalorie: the energy to warm 1 kg of water by 1 °C. In Europe labels must also show kilojoules, so a label might read "250 kcal / 1046 kJ".
= 1 kcal ≈ 4.2 kJ   |   1 kWh = 3,600 kJ ≈ 860 kcal
ex: 2,000 kcal × 4.184 kJ = 8,370 kJ | ÷ 3,600 = about 2.3 kWh per day | Spread over 86,400 s ≈ 97 W
### Where it goes
Most of the energy in food ends up as body heat. A resting adult gives off roughly 80–100 W, which is why a crowded room warms up quickly. Only a modest share goes into mechanical work, because muscles are only about 20–25% efficient.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 100 W", "l": "a resting person's output"}, {"v": "≈ 2.3 kWh", "l": "in 2,000 kcal of food"}, {"v": "≈ 25%", "l": "muscle efficiency"}]}
### Food versus fuel
Per kilogram, fats store about 37 kJ per gram, while carbohydrates and protein store about 17. Petrol stores about 46 kJ per gram, not that far above butter.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "kJ per gram (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Petrol", "v": 46.0}, {"l": "Fat", "v": 37.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Carbohydrate", "v": 17.0}, {"l": "Protein", "v": 17.0}]}
!? Food energy values were first measured by burning food in a sealed "bomb calorimeter" and measuring how much it warmed surrounding water.
> Your body runs on about the same power as an old 100 W light bulb.

# Thermal expansion in daily life
When a solid is heated its atoms vibrate harder and push each other slightly further apart, so it grows. The change is small per degree but adds up over long lengths and big temperature swings.
= change in length = original length × expansion coefficient × temperature rise
ex: 1 km of steel rail, coefficient ≈ 12 millionths per °C | Warms by 40 °C, cold morning to hot afternoon | Grows by 1000 × 0.000012 × 40 ≈ 0.48 m
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "expansion, millionths per °C", "items": [{"l": "Glass (Pyrex)", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Steel", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Aluminium", "v": 23.0, "hi": true}]}
### How engineers cope
• Bridges have expansion joints, the toothed metal strips you drive over
• Modern rails are welded and laid under tension, so they stretch rather than gap
• Pyrex glass expands little, so it survives oven-to-table temperature changes
### Water's odd behaviour
Water is densest at about 4 °C. Cool it further and it expands; when it freezes it grows by about 9%. Ice therefore floats, and lakes freeze from the top down, letting fish survive underneath.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Most materials", "items": ["shrink when cooled", "solid sinks in liquid"]}, "r": {"t": "Water", "items": ["expands below 4 °C", "ice floats"]}}
!? The Eiffel Tower is typically about 15 cm taller on a hot summer day than in winter.
> Heat makes most things grow, so good engineering always leaves room to move.

# Insulation and the Thermos
The vacuum flask was invented by the Scottish scientist James Dewar in 1892 to keep liquefied gases cold. A German company sold it to the public from 1904 under the name Thermos, which became the everyday word.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Stopper: blocks convection", "Silvered inner surface", "Vacuum gap", "Double glass or steel wall"]}
### How each layer works
• **Vacuum**: with almost no gas molecules, there is nothing to conduct heat or to carry it by convection
• **Silvered walls**: shiny surfaces reflect infrared radiation back towards the drink
• **Stopper**: stops warm air rising out of the top
### The weak spots
Heat still leaks slowly through the neck, where inner and outer walls join, and through the stopper. That is why a good flask keeps tea hot for perhaps 12 hours but not for days.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Conduction", "b": "blocked by the vacuum"}, {"a": "Convection", "b": "vacuum and stopper"}, {"a": "Radiation", "b": "reflected by silvering"}]}
### Same ideas, bigger scale
House insulation uses the same tricks. Wool, foam and cork trap air in tiny pockets so it can't circulate. Double glazing traps a gas layer between panes. Portugal is the world's largest cork producer, and cork is an excellent natural insulator for exactly this reason.
!! A Thermos keeps things hot => It slows heat flow either way, so it keeps cold drinks cold too
> Stop conduction, convection and radiation together and heat barely moves.

# Evaporation cools
In any liquid, molecules move at a range of speeds. Only the fastest have enough energy to break free from the surface. When they leave, the average energy of those left behind drops, and lower average energy means lower temperature.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fastest molecules escape", "Slower ones stay behind", "Average energy drops", "Liquid and surface cool"]}
### How much heat it takes
Evaporating water absorbs a lot of energy, about 2.4 million joules per kilogram at skin temperature. That is over five times the energy needed to heat the same water from 0 to 100 °C.
ex: You sweat 1 litre on a hot walk | 1 kg × 2.4 MJ/kg ≈ 2.4 MJ | About 0.67 kWh of heat removed from your body
### What speeds it up
• Higher temperature
• Wind or a fan, which carries away the moist air
• Dry air: in humid weather sweat drips off instead of evaporating, so you feel hotter
• Larger surface area, like a wet towel spread out
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Sweat cooling your skin", "Porous clay jugs (bilhas)", "Power-station cooling towers", "Shivering after a swim"]}
### Everyday examples
Traditional Portuguese clay water jugs seep a little water through their walls; as it evaporates, the water inside stays noticeably cooler. A dog pants because it has few sweat glands, so it evaporates water from its tongue instead.
!! A fan cools the air => It doesn't cool the room; it speeds up evaporation from your skin
> When water evaporates, it takes heat with it.

# Where Earth's energy comes from
The Sun delivers about 170,000 terawatts to the top of Earth's atmosphere. Humanity's total energy use averages around 20 terawatts, so sunlight brings roughly 10,000 times what we use.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "The Sun", "items": ["Plants → food, wood", "Weather → wind, rain, hydro", "Sunlight → solar panels", "Ancient plants → fossil fuels"]}
### Fossil fuels are old sunlight
Coal formed mostly from swamp forests around 300 million years ago. Oil and gas formed mainly from marine plankton. In both cases the energy was originally captured from sunlight by photosynthesis.
### The exceptions
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Nuclear", "b": "atoms made in old stars"}, {"a": "Geothermal", "b": "Earth's inner heat"}, {"a": "Tidal", "b": "Moon's gravity + Earth's spin"}]}
• Geothermal heat comes mostly from radioactive decay deep inside Earth, plus heat left over from its formation
• The Azores use it: geothermal plants on São Miguel supply a large share of the island's electricity
• France's La Rance tidal power station has run since 1966
### Even wind is solar
The Sun heats the equator more than the poles and land more than sea. These differences create pressure differences, and air moving between them is wind. Portugal now gets a large share of its electricity from wind and hydro, both driven by the Sun.
!? Uranium was forged in exploding stars before the Solar System existed, so nuclear power uses energy from older stars than ours.
> Almost every joule you use started out as sunlight.

== ph-waves

# Anatomy of a wave
A wave is a travelling disturbance. The material it passes through only wobbles about a fixed position; it's the pattern and the energy that move along. A cork on the sea bobs up and down as waves pass but stays roughly where it is.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Wavelength (λ)", "b": "crest to crest, metres"}, {"a": "Amplitude", "b": "height from the middle"}, {"a": "Frequency (f)", "b": "waves per second, Hz"}, {"a": "Period (T)", "b": "time for one wave, s"}]}
### Frequency and period
Frequency and period are two views of the same thing. A wave with a frequency of 50 Hz completes one cycle every 1/50 s, or 0.02 s. The European mains supply alternates at exactly this rate.
= period = 1 ÷ frequency
### Amplitude and energy
The energy a wave carries goes up with the square of its amplitude. Double the height of a sea wave and it carries four times the energy, which is why storm waves on the Nazaré coast are so destructive.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Amplitude", "items": ["sets loudness", "sets brightness"]}, "r": {"t": "Frequency", "items": ["sets pitch", "sets colour"]}}
### One equation for all waves
= wave speed = frequency × wavelength
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = f × λ", "items": [{"s": "v", "m": "wave speed, m/s"}, {"s": "f", "m": "frequency, Hz"}, {"s": "λ", "m": "wavelength, m"}]}
!! Waves carry water along with them => The water mostly goes round in small circles; the energy travels on
> A wave moves energy from place to place while the medium stays put.

# Try it: how long is a sound?
Rearranging the wave equation gives wavelength as speed divided by frequency. Because sound travels at a fixed speed in air, the frequency alone decides how long each wave is.
= wavelength = speed ÷ frequency
ex: Concert A at 440 Hz | 343 ÷ 440 ≈ 0.78 m | Each wave is about the length of your arm
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "wavelength in air, metres", "items": [{"l": "Lowest audible 20 Hz", "v": 17.2, "hi": true}, {"l": "Bass guitar 41 Hz", "v": 8.4}, {"l": "Concert A 440 Hz", "v": 0.78}, {"l": "Whistle 3 kHz", "v": 0.11}]}
### From tiny to huge
The range of human hearing covers wavelengths from about 17 m down to about 1.7 cm, a factor of 1,000. Few other senses span such a range.
### Why it matters
• Big loudspeakers (woofers) are needed to push long bass waves; small tweeters handle short ones
• Bass passes through walls and around corners more easily, so you hear the neighbours' music as a thump
• Organ pipes must be roughly half a wavelength long, so the lowest notes need pipes several metres tall
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low notes", "items": ["long waves", "pass through walls"]}, "r": {"t": "High notes", "items": ["short waves", "easily blocked"]}}
### Temperature changes the answer
Sound travels faster in warm air, about 0.6 m/s faster per °C. At 0 °C it's about 331 m/s; at 20 °C it's 343 m/s. Wind instruments go slightly sharp as they warm up for this reason.
> At a fixed speed, higher frequency always means shorter waves.
!? A bat's ultrasonic call at 50 kHz has a wavelength of under 7 mm, small enough to bounce off a moth.

# How far away is the storm?
Lightning and thunder happen at the same moment. Lightning heats a narrow channel of air to around 30,000 °C in a split second; the air expands explosively and makes the boom. The difference you notice is entirely down to travel time.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Light", "items": ["300,000 km/s", "arrives at once"]}, "r": {"t": "Sound", "items": ["343 m/s", "1 km every 3 s"]}}
### The 3-second rule
Light covers a few kilometres in about one hundred-thousandth of a second, so you can treat it as instant. Sound takes about 2.9 s per kilometre.
ex: Flash, then you count 9 seconds | 9 ÷ 3 = 3 | Storm is about 3 km away
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["See the flash", "Count the seconds", "Divide by 3", "Distance in km"]}
### Safety
• If the gap is under about 30 seconds (10 km), you're within reach of a strike
• If the gap is shrinking, the storm is coming towards you
• Wait about 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back outside
### Why thunder rumbles
A lightning bolt can be several kilometres long. Sound from the nearest part reaches you first and from the farthest parts later, so a single crack stretches into a long rumble.
!! Lightning never strikes the same place twice => Tall structures are hit many times a year
> Light arrives almost at once, so the delay before thunder is a measure of distance.
!? Thunder is rarely heard more than about 15–20 km away, so you can see "silent" lightning from storms far beyond that.

# The electromagnetic spectrum
Electromagnetic waves are linked electric and magnetic fields that keep creating each other as they travel. They need no medium, which is why sunlight crosses space. James Clerk Maxwell predicted them in the 1860s; Heinrich Hertz made radio waves in his lab in 1887.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Gamma rays: < 0.01 nm", "X-rays: 0.01–10 nm", "Ultraviolet: 10–400 nm", "Visible: 400–700 nm", "Infrared, microwaves, radio"]}
### Same speed, different wavelength
All of them travel at about 300,000 km/s in a vacuum. Using v = f × λ, a higher frequency simply means a shorter wavelength.
ex: Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz | 300,000,000 ÷ 2,400,000,000 | Wavelength = 0.125 m = 12.5 cm
### Energy per photon
Light comes in packets called photons, and each photon's energy rises with frequency. One UV photon has enough energy to break chemical bonds in DNA; a radio photon has millions of times too little, however many there are.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "3 m", "l": "FM radio wave"}, {"v": "12.5 cm", "l": "Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz"}, {"v": "500 nm", "l": "green light"}]}
### Uses across the spectrum
• Radio: broadcasting, mobile phones, GPS
• Microwaves: ovens (2.45 GHz), radar, satellite links
• Infrared: remote controls, thermal cameras
• X-rays: medical imaging, airport scanners
!! Mobile phones and Wi-Fi emit dangerous radiation like X-rays => They are non-ionising: each photon is far too weak to break chemical bonds
> Radio, light and X-rays are one family of waves; only the wavelength changes.

# Transverse and longitudinal
Both kinds of wave carry energy forward, but the medium moves differently. Picture a Slinky: shake one end sideways and a transverse wave runs along; push and pull the end and a longitudinal wave of squashed and stretched coils travels along it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Transverse", "items": ["side-to-side wobble", "crests and troughs", "light, guitar strings"]}, "r": {"t": "Longitudinal", "items": ["back-and-forth", "squeezes and stretches", "sound, ultrasound"]}}
### Why sound can only be longitudinal in air
A gas can be squeezed, so it can carry pressure waves. It can't resist being sheared sideways, so it can't carry a transverse wave. Solids can do both.
### Earthquakes show both kinds
• **P-waves** (primary) are longitudinal, travel at around 6 km/s in rock and arrive first
• **S-waves** (secondary) are transverse, slower, and cannot pass through liquid
• Because S-waves vanish on the far side of the planet, seismologists deduced in the early 1900s that Earth's outer core is liquid
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "0 s", "l": "Earthquake starts"}, {"y": "~17 s", "l": "P-wave arrives (100 km)"}, {"y": "~29 s", "l": "S-wave arrives (100 km)"}]}
### Light is special
Light is a transverse wave, but nothing physical moves sideways: it's the electric and magnetic fields that oscillate at right angles to the direction of travel. That's why light can be polarised, the trick used by polarising sunglasses.
!? The great Lisbon earthquake of 1755 was felt across much of Europe and North Africa; studying its effects helped launch modern seismology.
> Transverse waves wobble across their path; longitudinal waves squeeze along it.

# Reflection and echoes
When a wave meets a boundary, part of it bounces back. Hard, smooth surfaces such as rock, concrete or water reflect sound strongly; soft, porous ones absorb it.
### Hearing an echo
Your brain merges two sounds arriving less than about 0.1 s apart. In that time sound travels about 34 m, so a wall must be at least about 17 m away to give a separate echo.
ex: Shout at a cliff, echo returns in 2 s | Sound went there and back: 343 × 2 = 686 m | Cliff is about 343 m away
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Send a pulse", "Pulse hits object", "Echo returns", "Distance = speed × time ÷ 2"]}
### Using echoes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sonar", "b": "seabed depth, fish, submarines"}, {"a": "Medical ultrasound", "b": "babies, hearts, organs"}, {"a": "Bats and dolphins", "b": "echolocation for hunting"}, {"a": "Parking sensors", "b": "distance to the bumper"}]}
### How ultrasound sees inside you
A probe sends pulses of a few million hertz into the body. Each time they cross a boundary between tissues, such as fluid and muscle, part reflects. A computer times all the echoes and builds an image. Because it uses sound, not radiation, it's safe for pregnancy scans.
!! Echoes only happen in caves and canyons => Every room reflects sound; most echoes are too quick to hear separately
> An echo is a reflected wave, and timing it tells you how far away the reflector is.

# Interference
When two waves overlap, their effects simply add at each point. This is called **superposition**. If crest meets crest, you get a bigger wave (constructive interference). If crest meets trough, they cancel (destructive interference).
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Constructive", "items": ["crest + crest", "bigger wave"]}, "r": {"t": "Destructive", "items": ["crest + trough", "waves cancel"]}}
### Light is a wave: Young's experiment
In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits. Instead of two bright lines, he saw a pattern of bright and dark bands. Only waves could do this, as light from the two slits reinforced in some places and cancelled in others.
### Noise-cancelling headphones
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Microphone hears noise", "Chip inverts the wave", "Speaker plays the inverse", "Noise cancels at the ear"]}
They work best on steady, low sounds such as aircraft engine drone, where the wave is predictable and long. Sudden or high-pitched sounds, like voices, are harder to cancel in time.
### Everyday interference
• The rainbow colours on soap bubbles and oil films
• "Dead spots" in a room where two speakers' sounds partly cancel
• Wi-Fi signals reflecting off walls and interfering, giving weak spots a metre away from strong ones
• Musicians tuning by listening for "beats", a slow wobble when two notes are nearly the same
!? Two sounds can add up to silence, but the energy isn't destroyed: it is redistributed to places where the waves reinforce.
> Overlapping waves add: crests on crests build up, crests on troughs cancel out.

# Diffraction
Diffraction is the spreading of waves as they pass through a gap or around an edge. The effect is strongest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. Much bigger gaps let the wave pass almost straight through.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sound", "items": ["waves about 1 m long", "bends round doorways"]}, "r": {"t": "Light", "items": ["waves under 0.001 mm", "casts sharp shadows"]}}
### Seen in the harbour
Sea waves passing through a harbour entrance spread out in arcs on the other side, reaching boats tucked behind the harbour wall. You can watch this from above at many Portuguese fishing ports.
### Radio and hills
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. wavelength, metres", "items": [{"l": "Long-wave radio", "v": 1500.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Medium-wave AM", "v": 300.0}, {"l": "FM radio", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Mobile 4G", "v": 0.3}]}
Long radio waves bend around hills and over the horizon; short ones need a clearer line of sight. That's why AM stations reach further than FM, and why mobile networks need many masts.
### A limit on seeing
Diffraction also limits how sharp an image can be. Light spreading through a lens or the pupil of the eye blurs fine detail. Telescopes are made wider partly to reduce this blurring.
!! Light always travels in perfectly straight lines => Light diffracts too, but only noticeably around very tiny gaps
> Waves bend around obstacles roughly their own size, which is why you can hear round corners but not see round them.
!? The rainbow sheen on a CD comes from diffraction by its tracks, which are only about 1.6 thousandths of a millimetre apart.

# Resonance
Every object that can vibrate has one or more natural frequencies, set by its size, stiffness and mass. Push it in step with one of these and each push adds to the last, so the vibration builds up.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Small regular pushes", "Match natural frequency", "Energy builds each cycle", "Large vibration"]}
### Useful resonance
• A guitar body resonates to make the strings' quiet vibration loud
• Radios are tuned by setting a circuit to resonate at one station's frequency
• MRI scanners use resonance of atomic nuclei in a magnetic field
• Microwave ovens heat food by making water molecules jiggle at about 2.45 GHz
### Dangerous resonance
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1831", "l": "Marching soldiers"}, {"y": "1940", "l": "Tacoma Narrows falls"}, {"y": "2000", "l": "London bridge wobbles"}]}
In 1831 soldiers marching in step helped collapse the Broughton suspension bridge near Manchester, and armies have ordered troops to break step on bridges ever since. London's Millennium Bridge swayed alarmingly on opening day in 2000 as walkers fell into step with its motion; it was fixed with dampers.
!! Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell from simple resonance => It was aeroelastic flutter: wind and the twisting deck fed each other
> Small pushes at the right rhythm can build huge vibrations.
!? A trained singer can, occasionally, shatter a wine glass by holding its exact natural note loudly enough.

# The Doppler effect
Christian Doppler, an Austrian physicist, described the effect in 1842. If a source moves towards you, each wave crest is emitted a bit closer to you than the last, so crests arrive more often: higher frequency. Moving away, they're spread out: lower frequency.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Approaching", "items": ["waves bunched up", "higher pitch"]}, "r": {"t": "Moving away", "items": ["waves stretched", "lower pitch"]}}
### Tested with a train
In 1845 the Dutch scientist Buys Ballot put trumpeters on a train and musicians by the track to judge the pitch change. It matched Doppler's prediction.
### Uses
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Police radar", "b": "speed of a car"}, {"a": "Weather radar", "b": "wind inside storms"}, {"a": "Doppler ultrasound", "b": "blood flow in veins"}, {"a": "Astronomy", "b": "stars and galaxies' motion"}]}
### The expanding universe
Light from distant galaxies is shifted towards red, meaning longer wavelengths. In the 1920s Edwin Hubble and others found the farther the galaxy, the bigger the redshift. This was the key evidence that the universe is expanding.
ex: Siren at 700 Hz, ambulance at 25 m/s | Approaching ≈ 700 × 343 ÷ (343 − 25) ≈ 755 Hz | Leaving ≈ 700 × 343 ÷ (343 + 25) ≈ 652 Hz
!! The siren gets louder and that's the Doppler effect => Doppler changes the pitch, not the loudness; loudness changes with distance
> Motion towards you squeezes waves to a higher frequency; motion away stretches them lower.

# Speed
Speed tells you how much distance is covered in each unit of time. Most journeys speed up and slow down, so the simple formula gives an **average** speed. The speedometer shows your **instantaneous** speed, the speed at that very moment.
= speed = distance ÷ time
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = d ÷ t", "items": [{"s": "v", "m": "speed, m/s or km/h"}, {"s": "d", "m": "distance, m or km"}, {"s": "t", "m": "time, s or h"}]}
### Converting units
One kilometre per hour is 1,000 m in 3,600 s, which is 1 ÷ 3.6 m/s. So divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s, and multiply m/s by 3.6 to go back.
ex: Motorway limit in Portugal: 120 km/h | 120 ÷ 3.6 ≈ 33.3 m/s | You cover a football pitch every 3 seconds
### Some speeds for scale
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "km/h (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Walking", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Usain Bolt (peak)", "v": 44.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Motorway car", "v": 120.0}, {"l": "Alfa Pendular top", "v": 220.0}]}
Usain Bolt's 9.58 s world record over 100 m, set in Berlin in 2009, averages about 37.6 km/h. His fastest stretch, mid-race, touched about 44 km/h.
### Average speed can mislead
A trip from Viana do Castelo to Porto that takes an hour has an average of about 70 km/h, even if you spent time at 120 and time stuck at 0. Average speed says nothing about what happened in between.
!! Average speed is the average of the speeds => It's total distance ÷ total time; 60 km/h out and 40 km/h back averages 48, not 50
> Speed is distance per unit time, and km/h ÷ 3.6 gives m/s.

# Speed vs velocity
Speed is a **scalar**: just a size. Velocity is a **vector**: a size and a direction. "50 km/h" is a speed; "50 km/h heading north" is a velocity.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Speed", "items": ["how fast", "size only", "e.g. 50 km/h"]}, "r": {"t": "Velocity", "items": ["how fast + which way", "size and direction", "e.g. 50 km/h north"]}}
### Why the difference matters
Physics cares about velocity because forces change velocity. Turning a corner at a steady 40 km/h still needs a force from the tyres, because your direction is changing. Without that force the car would carry straight on.
### Round trips
If you run once round a 400 m track in 80 s, your average speed is 5 m/s. But you end where you started, so your displacement is zero and your average velocity is zero too.
ex: 400 m lap in 80 s | Average speed = 400 ÷ 80 = 5 m/s | Average velocity = 0 ÷ 80 = 0 m/s
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Distance", "b": "total path length"}, {"a": "Displacement", "b": "straight line, start to end"}, {"a": "Speed", "b": "distance ÷ time"}, {"a": "Velocity", "b": "displacement ÷ time"}]}
### Velocities add
On a moving walkway at an airport, your velocity relative to the floor is your walking velocity plus the walkway's. Pilots do the same sum with wind: a headwind of 100 km/h takes 100 km/h off their ground speed.
!? The Moon travels around Earth at roughly constant speed, about 3,700 km/h, yet its velocity is always changing because its direction keeps turning.
> Velocity is speed with a direction, so changing direction means changing velocity.

# Acceleration
Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Its unit, metres per second per second (m/s²), means "how many m/s faster each second". Speeding up, slowing down and turning are all accelerations.
= acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "a = (v − u) ÷ t", "items": [{"s": "a", "m": "acceleration, m/s²"}, {"s": "v", "m": "final velocity, m/s"}, {"s": "u", "m": "starting velocity, m/s"}, {"s": "t", "m": "time, s"}]}
ex: Car 0 → 100 km/h in 8 s | 100 ÷ 3.6 = 27.8 m/s | 27.8 ÷ 8 ≈ 3.5 m/s²
### Feeling it in g
It's often handy to compare with gravity, g ≈ 9.81 m/s². An ordinary car pulls about 0.35 g; hard braking on a dry road reaches nearly 1 g.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "acceleration, in g (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Family car 0–100", "v": 0.35}, {"l": "Emergency braking", "v": 0.9}, {"l": "Fastest electric car", "v": 1.3, "hi": true}, {"l": "F1 car braking", "v": 5.0}]}
### Deceleration
Slowing down is just acceleration in the opposite direction to motion, often given a minus sign. A car braking from 27.8 m/s to rest in 4 s has an acceleration of about −7 m/s².
!! A fast car is accelerating => At a steady speed in a straight line, acceleration is zero, however fast you go
> Acceleration is how quickly velocity changes, not how fast you are going.
!? Trained fighter pilots, wearing special suits, can briefly tolerate around 9 g; untrained people tend to black out at around 4–6 g.

# Free fall
Near Earth's surface gravity gives every falling object the same acceleration, about 9.81 m/s², usually called g. Each second, the falling speed goes up by 9.81 m/s, but the distance fallen grows much faster, with the square of time.
= speed = g × t   |   distance = ½ × g × t²
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "distance fallen, metres (no air)", "items": [{"l": "After 1 s", "v": 4.9}, {"l": "After 2 s", "v": 19.6}, {"l": "After 3 s", "v": 44.1, "hi": true}, {"l": "After 4 s", "v": 78.5}]}
ex: Drop a stone down a well, splash after 2 s | ½ × 9.81 × 2² ≈ 19.6 m | (A little less really, as the sound takes time to come back)
### Speeds pile up quickly
After 1 s you're falling at 9.81 m/s, about 35 km/h. After 3 s, 29.4 m/s, about 106 km/h. That's why even modest falls are dangerous.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["0 s: 0 km/h", "1 s: 35 km/h", "2 s: 71 km/h", "3 s: 106 km/h"]}
### g isn't quite the same everywhere
• Near the equator it's about 9.78 m/s²; near the poles about 9.83 m/s²
• It's slightly weaker on mountain tops, being further from Earth's centre
• On the Moon it's 1.62 m/s², about a sixth of Earth's
!! Things fall at a steady speed => In free fall they speed up every second until air resistance catches up
> In free fall, speed grows steadily with time but distance grows with time squared.

# Galileo's insight
For nearly 2,000 years people followed Aristotle, who taught that heavy objects fall faster than light ones. It seemed obvious: a stone drops quicker than a leaf. Galileo Galilei, working in Italy around 1590–1638, argued that the difference is caused by air, not weight.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Heavier things fall faster", "r": "All fall alike without air"}, {"w": "A leaf is too light to fall fast", "r": "Air resistance slows the leaf"}]}
### How he tested it
Falling objects move too fast to time with the clocks of his day. Galileo rolled balls down gentle ramps to slow the motion, timing them with water clocks and musical beats. He found the distance grew with the square of the time, whatever the ball's mass.
### A clever argument
Galileo also used pure reasoning. Tie a heavy and a light stone together. If light things fall slower, the light stone should hold back the heavy one, so the pair falls slower. But the pair is heavier, so it should fall faster. Both can't be true, so the idea must be wrong.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~350 BC", "l": "Aristotle: heavy = faster"}, {"y": "1638", "l": "Galileo's Two New Sciences"}, {"y": "1687", "l": "Newton explains why"}, {"y": "1971", "l": "Hammer and feather, Moon"}]}
### On the Moon
On 2 August 1971, Apollo 15 commander David Scott dropped a geologist's hammer and a falcon feather on the airless Moon. They hit the ground together, live on television.
!! Galileo dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa => The story comes from a later biographer; historians doubt it happened
> Without air, a hammer and a feather fall side by side.
!? In 2014 the BBC filmed a bowling ball and feathers dropped together inside the world's largest vacuum chamber, in Ohio, and they landed at the same instant.

# Terminal velocity
Air resistance, or drag, grows rapidly with speed, roughly with its square. A falling object speeds up until drag equals its weight. Then the forces balance, acceleration stops and it falls at a steady **terminal velocity**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Jump: drag is zero", "Speed up: drag grows", "Drag equals weight", "Steady terminal speed"]}
### What sets it
• Heavier objects need more drag to balance, so they fall faster
• Bigger area facing the air means more drag and a slower terminal speed
• Thinner air (higher up) means less drag and a faster fall
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "terminal velocity, km/h (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Large raindrop", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "Open parachute", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Skydiver, belly", "v": 200.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Skydiver, head down", "v": 300.0}]}
### Opening a parachute
A canopy has dozens of times the area of a body. When it opens, drag suddenly far exceeds weight, so the skydiver decelerates sharply until a new, much lower terminal velocity of about 20 km/h is reached.
!? In 2012 Felix Baumgartner jumped from about 39 km up. In the very thin air he reached around 1,350 km/h, faster than the speed of sound.
!! Raindrops would kill us without air resistance => True in theory, but drag limits even big drops to about 30 km/h
> Terminal velocity is reached when air resistance grows to match weight, so the fall stops speeding up.

# Stopping distance
Stopping a car happens in two stages. First your brain notices and your foot moves; the car is still at full speed. Then the brakes slow it. The total is the stopping distance.
= stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hazard appears", "Reaction: car at full speed", "Brakes on: car slows", "Car stops"]}
### Why braking distance grows with the square
The kinetic energy to get rid of is ½mv². Brakes remove energy at a roughly fixed rate per metre, so braking distance follows v². Double the speed and the braking distance quadruples.
= braking distance = v² ÷ (2 × deceleration)
ex: 50 km/h = 13.9 m/s, deceleration 7 m/s² | Braking: 13.9² ÷ 14 ≈ 14 m, thinking (1 s): 14 m | Total ≈ 28 m; at 100 km/h: 28 + 55 ≈ 83 m
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "total stopping distance, m (dry, 1 s reaction)", "items": [{"l": "30 km/h", "v": 13.0}, {"l": "50 km/h", "v": 28.0}, {"l": "90 km/h", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "120 km/h", "v": 113.0, "hi": true}]}
### What makes it worse
• Wet roads can roughly double braking distance; ice can make it ten times longer
• Worn tyres and poor brakes
• Tiredness, alcohol and phones lengthen thinking distance
!! A little faster makes little difference => At 60 instead of 50 km/h, braking distance rises by about 44%
> Braking distance grows with the square of speed, so small speed increases have big consequences.

# Reaction time
Reaction time is the delay between something happening and you responding. For a simple, expected signal, like a starting gun, it can be under 0.2 s. On the road, where you must notice a hazard, decide and move your foot, about 1 s is typical, and longer in real traffic.
ex: Motorway at 120 km/h = 33.3 m/s | 1 s reaction | 33 m travelled before braking starts
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "distance before braking, m (1.5 s reaction)", "items": [{"l": "50 km/h", "v": 21.0}, {"l": "90 km/h", "v": 38.0}, {"l": "120 km/h", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}]}
### Try the ruler test
Have a friend hold a 30 cm ruler above your open fingers and drop it without warning. The distance it falls before you catch it gives your reaction time, using the free-fall formula.
ex: Ruler falls 20 cm = 0.2 m | t = √(2 × 0.2 ÷ 9.81) | t ≈ 0.20 s
### What slows reactions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Looking at or holding a phone", "Alcohol, even below the limit", "Tiredness and long drives", "Complex or unexpected hazards"]}
In Portugal the drink-drive limit is 0.5 g/l of blood, and 0.2 g/l for new and professional drivers, reflecting how alcohol slows and blurs reactions.
!? Athletics rules treat any reaction under 0.1 s after the starting gun as a false start, on the basis that humans can't genuinely react faster.
> In the second it takes to react, a car at motorway speed covers about a third of a football pitch.

# Relative motion
Every measurement of motion is made relative to something. Usually we measure against the ground, but that is a choice, not a law of nature. Galileo made this point in 1632 with a ship: below decks, in a smoothly moving ship, flies, fish and dripping water all behave as on land.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Seen from the train", "items": ["cup is at rest", "speed 0 km/h"]}, "r": {"t": "Seen from the field", "items": ["cup moves with train", "speed 200 km/h"]}}
### Combining velocities
Relative velocities add or subtract. Two cars approaching head-on at 90 km/h each close the gap at 180 km/h. A car overtaking at 130 km/h past a lorry at 90 gains on it at only 40 km/h.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "your speed, km/h (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Earth's spin, Lisbon", "v": 1300.0}, {"l": "Earth round the Sun", "v": 107000.0, "hi": true}]}
### You are never still
Sitting in a chair in Portugal, you are carried round by Earth's rotation at about 1,300 km/h, and round the Sun at about 107,000 km/h. The Sun itself orbits the galaxy. You feel none of it, because steady motion can't be felt; only changes in motion can.
### From Galileo to Einstein
Einstein's special relativity (1905) started from the same principle: the laws of physics are the same for everyone moving steadily. Adding one twist, that light has the same speed for everyone, led to time dilation and E = mc².
!! There is one true "at rest" => All steady motion is relative; no experiment can single out absolute rest
> Motion only makes sense relative to something, and steady motion feels exactly like standing still.

# Graphs of motion
Graphs turn a journey into a picture. Two kinds are used most: distance–time and velocity–time. They look similar but you read them in completely different ways.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Distance–time", "items": ["slope = speed", "flat = stopped"]}, "r": {"t": "Velocity–time", "items": ["slope = acceleration", "area = distance"]}}
### Reading a distance–time graph
• A straight sloping line means steady speed; steeper means faster
• A flat line means not moving
• A curve getting steeper means speeding up
### Reading a velocity–time graph
• A flat line means steady velocity (zero acceleration)
• A line sloping up means accelerating; down means braking
• The area under the line is the distance travelled
ex: Car goes 0 → 20 m/s in 10 s on a straight line | Area of triangle = ½ × 10 × 20 | Distance = 100 m

### Where you see them
Fitness apps plot your pace through a run; car data loggers and train systems record speed against time; investigators use a vehicle's recorded speed graph to reconstruct crashes.
!! A flat line always means stopped => Only on a distance–time graph; on a velocity–time graph it means steady speed
> On a distance–time graph slope is speed; on a velocity–time graph slope is acceleration and area is distance.

# First law: inertia
Inertia is an object's resistance to changes in its motion. The more mass, the more inertia. A full supermarket trolley is harder to start and to stop than an empty one.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "At rest", "items": ["stays at rest", "until a force acts"]}, "r": {"t": "Moving", "items": ["keeps same velocity", "until a force acts"]}}
### A radical idea
Aristotle believed that moving things need a constant push to keep going. Galileo and then Descartes realised that, without friction, motion would simply continue. Newton made it his first law in 1687.
### The key word is "net"
Many forces can act at once. If they balance, the net force is zero and the object behaves as if no force acts. A car cruising at a steady 100 km/h has engine force exactly balancing drag and friction.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Passengers lurch when a bus brakes", "Tablecloth pulled from under plates", "Headrests protect against whiplash", "Shaking water off wet hands"]}
### Why headrests matter
In a rear-end crash, the seat shoves your body forward. Your head, by inertia, stays behind and snaps backwards. A headrest pushes your head along with your body, preventing whiplash.
ex: Bus brakes from 50 km/h | Bus slows, your body keeps going at 50 km/h | You lurch forward until a seat or handle stops you
!! Moving things naturally slow down and stop => They slow because friction and drag act; with no net force they keep going
> Objects resist changes to their motion; only an unbalanced force changes it.
!? Voyager 1, launched in 1977, has been coasting for decades with its engine off, still moving at about 17 km/s.

# Second law: F = m × a
The second law gives a number to the first. It says exactly how much an object accelerates when a net force acts. In fact, it defines the unit of force: 1 newton (N) accelerates 1 kg at 1 m/s².
= force = mass × acceleration
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = m × a", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "net force, newtons"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass, kg"}, {"s": "a", "m": "acceleration, m/s²"}]}
ex: 1,500 kg car accelerating at 3 m/s² | F = 1,500 × 3 | 4,500 N of net forward force
### How big is a newton?
A small apple of about 100 g weighs about 1 N. A typical adult weighs roughly 700 N. A family car's engine might deliver a few thousand newtons through the tyres.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 1 N", "l": "weight of a small apple"}, {"v": "≈ 700 N", "l": "weight of an adult"}, {"v": "≈ 35 MN", "l": "Saturn V thrust at launch"}]}
### Rearranging it
• a = F ÷ m: a lighter car accelerates faster with the same engine
• m = F ÷ a: astronauts measure their mass in orbit by timing how a known spring force accelerates them
### Net force, again
If you push a heavy box with 300 N and friction pushes back with 250 N, only the 50 N difference accelerates it. Push with 250 N and it moves at a steady speed or not at all.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Double the force", "items": ["double acceleration"]}, "r": {"t": "Double the mass", "items": ["half acceleration"]}}
!! A constant force gives a constant speed => A constant net force gives constant acceleration; speed keeps rising
> Force causes acceleration, and the more mass, the less acceleration for the same force.

# Third law: action and reaction
Forces always come in pairs. If A pushes on B, then B pushes on A with the same strength in the opposite direction. The pair acts at the same instant; neither comes first.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "You push the ground back", "b": "ground pushes you forward"}, {"a": "Rocket pushes gas down", "b": "gas pushes rocket up"}, {"a": "Swimmer pushes water", "b": "water pushes swimmer"}, {"a": "Earth pulls the apple", "b": "apple pulls the Earth"}]}
### Why they don't cancel
The two forces act on different objects, so they can't cancel each other out. When a horse pulls a cart, the cart pulls back on the horse. The horse still moves because the ground pushes it forward harder than the cart pulls back.
### Equal forces, unequal effects
The force pair is equal but the masses aren't. When you jump, you and Earth push each other equally. You accelerate noticeably; Earth, about 10²³ times heavier, accelerates by an immeasurably small amount.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Feet push ground backward", "Ground pushes feet forward", "You accelerate forward"]}
### Walking needs friction
On ice you can't walk well because your foot can't push backwards on the ground, so the ground can't push you forwards. Grip is how you get the reaction force you need.
ex: Skater (60 kg) pushes a friend (40 kg) on ice | Both feel the same force, say 120 N | She gets 2 m/s², he gets 3 m/s², in opposite directions
!! The bigger object pushes harder => A lorry and a car in a collision push on each other with equal force; the car's lower mass makes it suffer more acceleration
> Forces come in equal, opposite pairs acting on two different objects.

# Mass and weight again
In everyday speech "weight" means kilograms. In physics weight is a force, measured in newtons, and mass is measured in kilograms. Mass tells you how much stuff there is and how hard it is to accelerate. Weight tells you how hard gravity pulls on it.
= weight = mass × gravitational field strength (W = m × g)
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Mass", "items": ["kilograms", "same everywhere"]}, "r": {"t": "Weight", "items": ["newtons", "depends on gravity"]}}
ex: A 70 kg person | On Earth: 70 × 9.81 ≈ 687 N | On the Moon: 70 × 1.62 ≈ 113 N
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "weight of a 70 kg person, N", "items": [{"l": "Moon", "v": 113.0}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 260.0}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 687.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Jupiter (cloud tops)", "v": 1730.0}]}
### Your bathroom scale
A bathroom scale actually measures force: how hard you press on it. It then divides by Earth's g to show kilograms. Take it to the Moon and it would claim you'd lost five-sixths of your "weight", though your mass hadn't changed.
### Inertia doesn't change
On the Moon a loaded wheelbarrow is easier to lift, but just as hard to push sideways to get it moving, because that depends on mass, not weight.
!! Astronauts in orbit have no weight => Gravity still pulls them; they feel weightless because they are falling freely
> Mass is how much matter you have; weight is how hard gravity pulls on it.
!? Since 2019 the kilogram is defined using a fixed value of Planck's constant, replacing a metal cylinder kept near Paris since 1889.

# Seat belts and airbags
In a crash, your body has to go from the car's speed to zero. Newton's second law can be written in terms of momentum: force equals the change in momentum divided by the time it takes. Stretch the time, and the force falls.
= force = mass × change in velocity ÷ time taken
ex: 70 kg person stops from 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) | In 0.01 s against the dashboard: 70 × 13.9 ÷ 0.01 ≈ 97,000 N | In 0.1 s with belt and airbag: ≈ 9,700 N
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "peak force, kN (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Hitting dashboard", "v": 97.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Belt + airbag", "v": 9.7}]}
### How they work together
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Crash: car stops fast", "Belt locks and stretches", "Airbag inflates in ~30 ms", "Body slows more gently"]}
• The belt spreads force across the strong bones of the pelvis and ribcage
• Pre-tensioners pull the belt tight in the first milliseconds
• Airbags inflate in about 30 thousandths of a second, then vent gas to cushion
• Without a belt, an airbag can do harm: it's designed to work alongside one
### A gift to the world
Swedish engineer Nils Bohlin designed the three-point seat belt for Volvo in 1959. Volvo made the patent freely available, and the design is now standard worldwide. It's credited with saving a very large number of lives.
!! You can brace yourself with your arms => At 50 km/h the forces are many times your body weight; arms cannot hold them
> Seat belts and airbags save lives by making the stop take longer, which shrinks the force.

# Circular motion
Moving in a circle at steady speed is still accelerating, because the direction changes all the time. That acceleration points towards the centre of the circle, and so must the net force causing it, the **centripetal force**.
= centripetal force = m × v² ÷ r
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = m × v² ÷ r", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "force to centre, N"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass, kg"}, {"s": "v", "m": "speed, m/s"}, {"s": "r", "m": "radius, m"}]}
ex: 1,200 kg car at 36 km/h (10 m/s), bend radius 20 m | F = 1,200 × 10² ÷ 20 | 6,000 N from tyre friction
### Speed matters most
The force needed grows with the square of speed. Take a bend twice as fast and you need four times the grip. On a wet or icy road, available friction drops, so the car slides outwards along a straighter path.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Car on a bend", "b": "tyre friction"}, {"a": "Moon round Earth", "b": "gravity"}, {"a": "Ball on a string", "b": "tension in the string"}, {"a": "Electron (old model)", "b": "electric attraction"}]}
### Banked curves
Racing tracks and some motorway slip roads tilt inwards. Part of the road's push then points towards the centre, so cars rely less on friction to turn.
!! Let go of a whirling ball and it flies outward => It flies off in a straight line along the tangent, the way it was heading
> Circular motion needs a constant pull towards the centre; take it away and the object goes straight.
!? For a rotating space station to give "gravity" of 1 g with a radius of 100 m, it would need to turn about three times a minute.

# "Centrifugal force"
Sitting in a car as it turns left, you feel thrown to the right. Watched from outside, nothing pushes you outwards: your body is trying to continue in a straight line, and the car door swings into your path and pushes you round the corner.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "From outside", "items": ["body goes straight", "door pushes you inward"]}, "r": {"t": "From inside", "items": ["you feel flung out", "seems like a force"]}}
### A "fictitious" force
Physicists call centrifugal force a fictitious or inertial force. It appears only when you describe motion from a rotating viewpoint. Engineers sometimes use it as a handy shortcut, but there is no object producing it.
### The spin dryer
In a washing machine's spin cycle, the drum pushes the clothes inwards, making them travel in a circle. Water drops pass through the holes, where nothing pushes them inwards, so they fly off in straight lines and leave the drum.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Drum spins fast", "Drum pushes clothes inward", "Water slips through holes", "Water flies off straight"]}
### Other examples
• Mud flying off a bicycle tyre leaves in a straight line from where it lets go
• A salad spinner dries lettuce the same way
• Centrifuges in labs separate blood into layers; denser parts move furthest out
!! Centrifugal force throws things outward => Nothing pulls them out; they just keep going straight while their surroundings turn
> The outward "force" you feel when turning is your own inertia seen from a turning seat.

# Friction and Newton
Friction is the force that resists surfaces sliding over each other. Even surfaces that feel smooth have microscopic bumps that catch and briefly bond. Air and water resistance are the fluid versions.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Motion runs out naturally", "r": "Friction removes it"}, {"w": "Friction is always bad", "r": "Walking needs friction"}]}
### Why Aristotle was fooled
Everything in daily life slows to a stop: a rolling ball, a pushed cart, a coasting bicycle. It's easy to conclude that rest is the natural state. Only by imagining away friction, as Galileo did, does the first law become visible.
### How friction compares
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "friction coefficient (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Tyre on dry road", "v": 0.8, "hi": true}, {"l": "Tyre on wet road", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "Wood on wood", "v": 0.3}, {"l": "Steel on ice", "v": 0.02}]}
The friction coefficient is roughly friction force divided by the force pressing the surfaces together. Lower numbers mean slipperier contact.
### Friction we rely on
• Tyres gripping the road, and brakes gripping discs
• Shoes on pavements; Portugal's polished calçada stones become notoriously slippery when wet
• Knots holding, nails staying in wood, matches lighting
### In space
With no air, a spacecraft keeps coasting after its engines stop. Probes travel for years using only short engine bursts to change course.
ex: Ice hockey puck at 10 m/s on ice, μ ≈ 0.02 | Deceleration ≈ 0.02 × 9.81 ≈ 0.2 m/s² | Glides about 10² ÷ (2 × 0.2) ≈ 250 m
> Things stop because friction and drag act on them, not because motion naturally fades.

# Newton's laws in sport
Every sport is applied Newtonian physics. Athletes and coaches use the three laws, often without naming them, to get more speed, power and control.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "First law", "b": "heavy players hard to stop"}, {"a": "Second law", "b": "more force, more acceleration"}, {"a": "Third law", "b": "push ground back, go forward"}]}
### Kicking a ball
Foot and ball are in contact for only about a hundredth of a second. During that time the force on the ball can exceed a thousand newtons. Following through keeps the foot pushing slightly longer, giving more change in momentum.
ex: 0.43 kg football kicked to 30 m/s (108 km/h) | Contact lasts 0.01 s | Average force = 0.43 × 30 ÷ 0.01 ≈ 1,300 N
### Sprint starts
Sprinters push hard backwards against starting blocks. The blocks push back, giving a large forward force from the very first step. Leaning forward helps direct that force horizontally.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Sprinters push back on blocks", "Swimmers push water backwards", "Rowers pull oars against water", "Jumpers push down to go up"]}
### Soft landings
Bending your knees when you land from a jump makes the stop take longer, so the force on your joints is smaller. Gymnastics mats, padded gloves and cushioned running shoes use the same idea.
!! A bigger swing always means a harder hit => Speed at impact and clean contact matter more than effort
> Sport is a contest of forces: push back to go forward, push longer to go faster, bend to land softly.

# Principia, 1687
The full title is *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, "Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy". Written in Latin, it set out three laws of motion and a law of universal gravitation, then used them to explain a huge range of phenomena.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1665–66", "l": "Plague years at home"}, {"y": "1684", "l": "Halley visits Newton"}, {"y": "1687", "l": "Principia published"}, {"y": "1758", "l": "Halley's comet returns"}]}
### How it came about
In 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley asked Newton what path a planet would follow under an inverse-square pull. Newton said an ellipse, and that he had already calculated it. Halley urged him to publish and paid for the printing himself.
### What it explained
• Why planets follow Kepler's elliptical orbits
• The tides, from the pull of the Moon and Sun
• The paths of comets
• The slightly flattened shape of the spinning Earth
### Its legacy
Newton's laws predicted the return of Halley's comet in 1758 and, in 1846, guided astronomers to discover Neptune from its pull on Uranus. They are still used to send spacecraft to other planets.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Newton (1687)", "items": ["everyday speeds", "ordinary gravity"]}, "r": {"t": "Einstein (1905–15)", "items": ["near light speed", "very strong gravity"]}}
!! An apple hit Newton on the head => Newton said watching an apple fall made him think about gravity; nothing hit him
> The Principia showed that the same laws rule a falling apple and the orbiting Moon.
!? Newton later ran England's Royal Mint and personally pursued counterfeiters.

# Universal gravitation
Newton's great leap was to say that the force pulling an apple down is the same force holding the Moon in orbit, and that it acts between every pair of masses in the universe. He published this law in 1687.
= F = G × m₁ × m₂ ÷ r²
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = G m₁ m₂ ÷ r²", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "pull between them, N"}, {"s": "m₁, m₂", "m": "the two masses, kg"}, {"s": "r", "m": "centre-to-centre distance, m"}, {"s": "G", "m": "constant, 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹"}]}
### The inverse square
Gravity spreads out in all directions, like light from a bulb. At twice the distance the same pull is spread over four times the area, so it's a quarter as strong. At ten times the distance it's a hundredth.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative pull vs distance", "items": [{"l": "1 × distance", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "2 × distance", "v": 0.25}, {"l": "3 × distance", "v": 0.11}, {"l": "4 × distance", "v": 0.06}]}
### Gravity is very weak
G is tiny, so only enormous masses produce noticeable pulls. Two people standing 1 m apart attract each other with a force of about a third of a millionth of a newton. A small fridge magnet beats the whole Earth's pull on a paperclip.
ex: Two 70 kg people, 1 m apart | F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 70 × 70 ÷ 1² | ≈ 0.00000033 N
### Weighing the Earth
In 1798 Henry Cavendish measured the tiny attraction between lead balls in his laboratory. That gave G, and with it the first accurate value for Earth's mass, about 6 × 10²⁴ kg.
!! Gravity stops at some distance => Its reach is unlimited; it just gets weaker and weaker
> Every mass pulls on every other, and the pull falls with the square of the distance.

# Orbits are falling
An orbit isn't a balance between gravity and some outward force. It is pure falling. The satellite falls towards Earth all the time, but it's moving sideways so fast that Earth's surface curves away just as quickly as it falls.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fire slow: lands nearby", "Fire faster: lands further", "Fast enough: misses Earth", "Keeps falling round: orbit"]}
### Newton's cannon
Newton imagined a cannon on a very high mountain. Each faster shot lands further away, until at one speed the ball falls round the curve of the Earth and comes back to the mountain. He drew this in a work published in 1728, after his death.
### The magic number
Earth's surface curves down by about 5 m for every 8 km you travel. In the first second of falling, anything drops about 5 m. So something moving sideways at about 8 km/s falls 5 m while the ground drops 5 m: it never gets closer.
ex: Sideways speed 8 km/s | In 1 s it travels 8 km and falls 5 m | Ground has also dropped 5 m: it stays at the same height
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Too slow", "items": ["curves down", "hits the ground"]}, "r": {"t": "About 8 km/s", "items": ["falls round Earth", "orbits for ever"]}}
### Why satellites still come down
Low satellites skim the outer edge of the atmosphere. The thin air drags on them, slowly lowering their orbit. Without occasional engine boosts, the ISS would fall back within a year or two.
!! Satellites orbit because they're above gravity => Gravity is what keeps them in orbit; without it they'd fly off in a straight line
> An orbit is a fall that keeps missing the ground.
!? The Moon is falling towards Earth too, by about 1.4 mm every second, while moving sideways about 1 km.

# Orbital speeds

# Weightlessness
The feeling of weight is really the feeling of the floor pushing up on you. In free fall, nothing pushes on you, because you and everything around you fall together. That's the floating feeling of orbit.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "gravity strength, % of surface", "items": [{"l": "Ground level", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "ISS, 400 km", "v": 89.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "GPS satellites", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Moon's distance", "v": 0.03}]}
ex: Earth's radius 6,371 km, ISS radius 6,771 km | (6,371 ÷ 6,771)² ≈ 0.89 | Gravity there is about 89% of ground level
### Free fall on Earth
You feel brief weightlessness at the top of a rollercoaster hill or in a lift that drops suddenly. ESA trains astronauts and runs experiments on parabolic flights from Bordeaux in France: the aircraft flies arcs that give about 20 seconds of free fall each time.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Standing on Earth", "items": ["floor pushes up", "you feel weight"]}, "r": {"t": "In orbit", "items": ["all fall together", "nothing pushes you"]}}
### Effects on the body
• Without load, bones lose roughly 1% of their density per month in some areas
• Muscles weaken, so ISS crews exercise about two hours a day
• Fluid shifts to the head, giving a puffy face and blocked sinuses
• The spine stretches, and astronauts can return a few centimetres taller, though this fades
!! There's no gravity in space => Gravity is almost as strong at the ISS; astronauts float because they are falling
> Weightlessness is what falling feels like when everything falls with you.

# Geostationary orbit
Orbits get slower as they get higher. At one particular height, about 35,786 km above the equator, one orbit takes exactly as long as one turn of the Earth. A satellite there seems to hang still in the sky.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "35,786 km", "l": "height above the equator"}, {"v": "23 h 56 min", "l": "time for one orbit"}, {"v": "≈ 3 km/s", "l": "orbital speed"}]}
### Why it's so useful
A fixed dish on your roof can point at one satellite for years without moving. That's why satellite TV dishes in Portugal all point roughly south, towards the equator.
### The catch: distance and delay
The signal has to travel up nearly 36,000 km and down again. Even at the speed of light that takes about a quarter of a second, which is why satellite phone calls and old satellite internet felt laggy.
ex: Up + down ≈ 2 × 35,786 km ≈ 71,600 km | ÷ 300,000 km/s | ≈ 0.24 s delay each way
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "TV broadcasting", "b": "fixed dish, wide coverage"}, {"a": "Weather (Meteosat)", "b": "watches Europe constantly"}, {"a": "Communications", "b": "ships, aircraft, remote areas"}]}
### A crowded ring
Because there is only one such ring, slots in it are allocated internationally. Europe's Meteosat satellites, run by EUMETSAT, sit there and provide the cloud images on weather forecasts.
!? The idea of using geostationary satellites for communication was popularised by science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke in 1945, nearly 20 years before the first one was launched.
!! Geostationary satellites aren't moving => They travel at about 11,000 km/h, exactly keeping pace with Earth's spin
> At one special height, an orbit lasts exactly a day, so the satellite stays over the same spot.

# Tides
The Moon's pull weakens with distance. The side of Earth facing the Moon is pulled harder than Earth's centre, and the centre harder than the far side. The result is a stretch: the oceans bulge towards the Moon and also on the opposite side.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Near side pulled most", "Centre pulled less", "Far side pulled least", "Two ocean bulges"]}
### Why tides come about 50 minutes later each day
Earth spins under the two bulges, giving two highs and two lows. But the Moon moves along its orbit too, so it takes about 24 hours 50 minutes to return overhead. High tides are about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.
### Spring and neap tides
The Sun raises tides as well, a little under half as strong as the Moon's. When Sun and Moon line up, at new and full moon, their effects add: big **spring tides**. At half moon they partly cancel: small **neap tides**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Spring tides", "items": ["new and full moon", "Sun and Moon aligned", "biggest range"]}, "r": {"t": "Neap tides", "items": ["half moon", "at right angles", "smallest range"]}}
### Range varies hugely
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical spring tide range, m (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Mediterranean", "v": 0.4}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 3.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mont-Saint-Michel", "v": 13.0}, {"l": "Bay of Fundy", "v": 16.0}]}
The shape of coasts and seabeds amplifies or damps tides. Portugal's Atlantic coast has moderate ranges; the nearly enclosed Mediterranean barely has tides at all.
!? Tidal friction is slowly braking Earth's spin, lengthening the day by roughly 2 thousandths of a second per century, while the Moon drifts about 3.8 cm further away each year.
> Tides come from the difference in the Moon's pull across Earth, which stretches the oceans into two bulges.

# Escape velocity
Throw a ball up and it comes back. Throw it faster and it goes higher. Escape velocity is the launch speed at which an object, with no further push, would keep slowing forever but never stop and fall back.
= escape velocity = √(2 × G × M ÷ r)
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "escape velocity, km/s", "items": [{"l": "Moon", "v": 2.4}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 11.2, "hi": true}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 59.5}]}
### It doesn't depend on your mass
A pebble and a spacecraft need the same speed to escape, because heavier things feel more gravity but also need more force to slow them down; the two effects cancel, just as in free fall.
### Rockets don't need it at the start
Escape velocity is for something fired like a bullet and then left alone. A rocket keeps firing, so it can climb at any speed. In practice rockets rise slowly through the thick lower air, then accelerate horizontally for several minutes. Missions to the Moon or Mars burn up to nearly 11 km/s only once they're already in orbit.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Orbit (low)", "b": "about 7.8 km/s sideways"}, {"a": "Escape Earth", "b": "about 11.2 km/s"}, {"a": "Escape the Sun", "b": "about 42 km/s from Earth"}]}
### Black holes
If an object is compact enough, its escape velocity exceeds the speed of light. Nothing, not even light, can get out: that's a black hole.
ex: 11.2 km/s × 3,600 | ≈ 40,300 km/h | About 12 times faster than a rifle bullet
!! You need escape velocity to reach space => Reaching space or orbit needs less; escape is for leaving Earth's pull for good
> Escape velocity is the speed that lets an unpowered object coast away from a planet for ever.

# Kepler's laws
Johannes Kepler worked with the most precise naked-eye observations ever made, collected by the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe. Struggling for years to fit the orbit of Mars, he finally abandoned perfect circles.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1. Ellipses", "b": "Sun at one focus"}, {"a": "2. Equal areas", "b": "faster when closer to Sun"}, {"a": "3. Harmony", "b": "period² ∝ distance³"}]}
### The third law in action
If distance is measured in astronomical units (Earth–Sun distance) and time in Earth years, the square of a planet's year equals the cube of its distance.
ex: Mars is 1.52 AU from the Sun | 1.52³ ≈ 3.51, √3.51 ≈ 1.87 | A Martian year ≈ 1.88 Earth years
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "length of year, Earth years", "items": [{"l": "Mercury", "v": 0.24}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 1.88}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 11.9}]}
### Earth's own ellipse
Earth's orbit is nearly circular, but it's closest to the Sun in early January, about 147 million km, and farthest in early July, about 152 million km. Moving faster near the Sun, Earth crosses the winter half of its orbit quicker: the northern winter is a few days shorter than summer.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1609", "l": "First two laws"}, {"y": "1619", "l": "Third law"}, {"y": "1687", "l": "Newton explains them"}]}
!! Seasons happen because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer => Earth is closest in January; seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis
> Kepler found the rules of planetary motion; Newton later showed gravity was the reason for them.

# Gravity assists
Flying close to a planet, a spacecraft is pulled in and swung round. Relative to the planet it leaves at the same speed it arrived. But the planet itself is moving around the Sun, and the craft can borrow some of that motion.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Approach the planet", "Gravity bends the path", "Leave in a new direction", "Faster relative to Sun"]}
### The train analogy
Throw a ball at 30 km/h at a train coming towards you at 50 km/h. Relative to the train the ball hits at 80 km/h and bounces back at 80 km/h. Add the train's own 50 km/h and the ball now flies away at 130 km/h.
ex: Ball 30 km/h, train 50 km/h towards it | Bounces off at 80 km/h relative to the train | 80 + 50 = 130 km/h relative to the ground
### Nothing for free
The planet loses exactly the energy the probe gains, but a planet is so massive that its orbit changes by an immeasurably tiny amount.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1977", "l": "Voyagers launched"}, {"y": "1979", "l": "Jupiter flybys"}, {"y": "1981", "l": "Voyager 2 at Saturn"}, {"y": "1989", "l": "Voyager 2 at Neptune"}]}
### The Grand Tour
A rare alignment of the outer planets, recurring roughly every 175 years, let Voyager 2 visit Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune using each as a slingshot to the next. Without assists, the trip to Neptune would have taken decades longer.
### Braking too
Assists can also slow a craft down. ESA's BepiColombo, launched in 2018 to Mercury, used nine flybys of Earth, Venus and Mercury mostly to shed speed as it falls towards the Sun. ESA's JUICE is using several flybys on its way to Jupiter.
> A gravity assist steals a sliver of a planet's orbital motion to speed up or redirect a probe.

# Gravitational waves
In 1916 Albert Einstein predicted that accelerating masses send out ripples in space-time itself. They travel at the speed of light, stretching and squeezing space as they pass. He doubted they could ever be detected, because the effect is so tiny.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1916", "l": "Einstein predicts them"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "LIGO detects first"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "Nobel Prize; neutron stars"}]}
### How LIGO measures them
LIGO uses two L-shaped detectors in the USA, each with arms 4 km long. Laser beams bounce along the arms and recombine. A passing wave lengthens one arm and shortens the other by a minuscule amount, shifting how the beams interfere.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "4 km", "l": "each LIGO arm"}, {"v": "0.2 s", "l": "length of the 2015 chirp"}, {"v": "1.3 bn", "l": "light-years to source"}]}
### The first signal
On 14 September 2015, both detectors picked up a chirp lasting a fraction of a second. It came from two black holes, about 36 and 29 times the Sun's mass, spiralling together. About three Suns' worth of mass was converted into gravitational-wave energy.
### A new kind of astronomy
• Europe's Virgo detector near Pisa, Italy, joined the search in 2017
• In August 2017 a merger of neutron stars was seen in both gravitational waves and light, and showed such collisions forge heavy elements like gold
• ESA plans a space detector, LISA, with arms millions of kilometres long, for the 2030s
!! Gravitational waves are just very strong gravity => They're ripples in space-time itself, so faint that measuring them took a century
> Accelerating masses ripple space-time, and since 2015 we can hear those ripples.

# Momentum
Momentum is "mass in motion": mass times velocity. Its unit is the kilogram metre per second (kg·m/s). Like velocity, it has a direction. Newton actually wrote his second law in terms of momentum: force is the rate at which momentum changes.
= momentum = mass × velocity (p = m × v)
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "p = m × v", "items": [{"s": "p", "m": "momentum, kg·m/s"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass, kg"}, {"s": "v", "m": "velocity, m/s"}]}
### Big and slow vs small and fast
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "momentum, kg·m/s (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Lorry, 40 t, 5 km/h", "v": 56000.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Car, 1.2 t, 50 km/h", "v": 16700.0}, {"l": "Rugby player", "v": 800.0}, {"l": "Rifle bullet", "v": 9.0}]}
ex: 40,000 kg lorry creeping at 1.4 m/s | p = 40,000 × 1.4 = 56,000 kg·m/s | A rifle bullet (0.01 kg at 900 m/s) has only 9
### Momentum vs kinetic energy
A bullet has small momentum but a lot of energy concentrated in a tiny area, which is why it does damage. A slow lorry has huge momentum, which is why it takes so long to stop and can push almost anything aside. Momentum grows with v; kinetic energy grows with v².
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Momentum", "items": ["m × v", "has a direction"]}, "r": {"t": "Kinetic energy", "items": ["½ × m × v²", "no direction"]}}
!! A bullet has more momentum than a car => A 50 km/h car has nearly 2,000 times a bullet's momentum; the bullet has concentrated energy
> Momentum is mass times velocity: the harder something is to stop, the more momentum it has.

# Conservation of momentum
Newton's third law says colliding objects push on each other equally and oppositely for the same time. So whatever momentum one gains, the other loses. Add them up and the total never changes, as long as no outside force interferes.
= total momentum before = total momentum after
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Add up momentum before", "Objects collide", "Equal, opposite forces", "Same total momentum after"]}
ex: 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s hits a stopped 1,500 kg car; they lock together | Before: 1,000 × 20 = 20,000 kg·m/s | After: 20,000 ÷ 2,500 = 8 m/s
### Crash investigation
Police crash investigators work backwards. Skid marks and final resting positions give speeds just after impact; the masses are known; momentum conservation, applied in two directions, reveals speeds before the crash.
### Directions matter
Momentum to the right counts as positive, to the left negative. Two identical cars hitting head-on at the same speed have a total momentum of zero, so if they lock together the wreck stops dead.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Skaters push apart", "b": "total stays zero"}, {"a": "Snooker break", "b": "spread out, same total"}, {"a": "Crash wreckage", "b": "moves in the impact's direction"}]}
### Why "no outside force"
Friction from the road acts during a crash, but the collision itself lasts only a fraction of a second while the impact forces are huge. Over that brief moment, outside forces are tiny in comparison, so momentum is very nearly conserved.
!? Conservation of momentum is one of the deepest laws in physics: it follows from the fact that the laws of nature are the same everywhere in space.
> In any collision, the total momentum is the same before and after.

# Elastic and inelastic
Momentum is always conserved in collisions, but kinetic energy isn't. The difference between collisions is how much kinetic energy survives as motion, and how much turns into heat, sound and bent metal.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Elastic", "items": ["objects bounce apart", "kinetic energy kept"]}, "r": {"t": "Inelastic", "items": ["deform or stick", "energy to heat, sound"]}}
### A spectrum, not two boxes
Truly elastic collisions only happen between atoms and molecules. Everything else loses some energy. How much is captured by how well things bounce.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "bounce height, % of drop (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Superball", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Tennis ball", "v": 55.0}, {"l": "Football", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Lump of clay", "v": 0.0}]}
### Where the energy goes in a crash
ex: Car crash from the previous card | Before: ½ × 1,000 × 20² = 200 kJ | After: ½ × 2,500 × 8² = 80 kJ, so 120 kJ became heat, sound and damage
### Why it matters
• In car crashes, inelastic is good: the car absorbs energy instead of bouncing the passengers
• Sports balls are tested for bounce: official tennis balls dropped from 2.54 m must bounce to about 1.35–1.47 m
• Gas molecules collide elastically, which is why a gas doesn't simply run down and settle
!! In inelastic collisions momentum is lost => Momentum is always conserved; only kinetic energy is lost
> Elastic collisions keep the kinetic energy; inelastic ones turn some of it into heat, sound and deformation.

# Impulse
Impulse is force multiplied by the time it acts. It equals the change in momentum. It's measured in newton seconds (N·s), which is the same as kg·m/s.
= impulse = force × time = change in momentum
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Short time", "items": ["huge force", "hammer on a nail"]}, "r": {"t": "Long time", "items": ["small force", "catching an egg"]}}
### Same change, different force
To stop a moving thing, you must remove all its momentum. You choose how: a big force briefly or a gentle force for longer. When you want to protect something, stretch the time. When you want a big force, shorten it.
ex: Catch a 0.15 kg cricket ball at 30 m/s | Change in momentum = 4.5 N·s | Stiff hands (0.01 s): 450 N; hands moving back (0.1 s): 45 N
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Bending knees when landing", "Pulling hands back to catch", "Padded gloves and helmets", "Bubble wrap round parcels"]}
### When you want a short time
A hammer delivers its momentum to a nail in a millisecond or two, giving a force of thousands of newtons with a modest swing. Karate breaks and the "thwack" of a golf drive both rely on very short contact times.
### Follow-through
In tennis, golf and football, coaches stress following through. Keeping the racket or foot pushing for longer increases the impulse, so the ball leaves faster.
!! Catching something gently is about reflexes => It's about stretching the stopping time to shrink the force
> The same change in momentum can come from a big force briefly or a small force for longer.

# Crumple zones
The front and rear of a modern car are designed to collapse in a controlled way, folding like a concertina. This lengthens the time and distance over which the passenger compartment stops, so the force on the people inside is much smaller.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Crumple zone: front", "Rigid safety cell: passengers", "Crumple zone: rear"]}
### Numbers
ex: Car at 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) stops in 0.6 m of crumpling | Deceleration = 13.9² ÷ (2 × 0.6) ≈ 160 m/s² ≈ 16 g | Stopping in 5 cm instead would mean about 190 g
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "deceleration in g (illustrative, 50 km/h)", "items": [{"l": "Rigid car, 5 cm", "v": 190.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Crumple zone, 60 cm", "v": 16.0}]}
### Strong in the middle
Crumple zones only work if the passenger cell itself stays intact. The middle of the car uses high-strength steel so that the energy is absorbed in front of you, not by you. The engine is often designed to drop down under the cabin rather than push into it.
### History
Hungarian-born engineer Béla Barényi at Mercedes-Benz patented the idea in 1952, and it reached production cars in 1959. Today the independent Euro NCAP programme, started in 1997, crash-tests new cars sold in Europe and rates how well they protect occupants.
!! Old, rigid cars were safer because they didn't dent => They passed the force straight to the people inside
> A car that crumples protects its passengers by taking longer to stop.
!? A crumpled car after a minor crash may look worse than an older car's, but that damage is energy that didn't go into its occupants.

# Recoil
Before a cannon fires, the cannon and shell are at rest: total momentum zero. After firing, the shell has forward momentum, so the cannon must have equal backward momentum to keep the total at zero.
= mass of gun × recoil speed = mass of shell × shell speed
ex: 1,000 kg cannon fires a 10 kg ball at 300 m/s | Ball's momentum: 10 × 300 = 3,000 kg·m/s | Cannon recoils at 3,000 ÷ 1,000 = 3 m/s
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Shell", "items": ["small mass", "very fast forward"]}, "r": {"t": "Cannon", "items": ["large mass", "slow backward"]}}
### Taming the kick
Old warship cannons rolled back on wheeled carriages, held by thick ropes. Modern artillery uses hydraulic recoil systems that let the barrel slide back and return. Rifles are held firmly against the shoulder so the shooter's body adds mass and absorbs the kick.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Garden hose pushing back on you", "Fire hoses need two people", "Squid and octopus jet swimming", "Stepping off a small boat"]}
### Jumping from a boat
Step from a small rowing boat onto a jetty, and the boat shoots backwards. Your forward momentum must be matched by the boat's backward momentum. Many people have ended up in the water this way.
!? The same equation lets a squid escape predators: it squirts water backwards and shoots forwards.
!! Recoil means the gun pushes harder than the bullet => The forces are equal and opposite; the gun moves slower only because it's heavier
> When something is pushed out one way, whatever pushed it moves the other way with equal momentum.

# Newton's cradle
Despite the name, Newton didn't invent it. The desk toy became popular in the late 1960s. It's a neat demonstration of two conservation laws at work together.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ball 1 swings in", "Hits the row of balls", "Pulse passes through", "Last ball swings out"]}
### Why only one ball flies out
In principle, two balls could leave at half the speed and still carry the same momentum. But that would halve the kinetic energy. The only outcome that conserves both momentum and kinetic energy for steel balls is one ball out at the same speed.
ex: One 0.1 kg ball at 1 m/s | Momentum 0.1, energy 0.05 J | Two balls at 0.5 m/s: momentum 0.1 ✓, energy 0.025 J ✗
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lift 1 ball", "b": "1 ball swings out"}, {"a": "Lift 2 balls", "b": "2 balls swing out"}, {"a": "Lift 3 of 5", "b": "3 out, middle one swings"}]}
### Why it eventually stops
Each collision loses a little energy as sound (the clicks), heat and slight deformation of the steel. Over time the motion spreads out, the balls start moving together and eventually they all come to rest.
### The snooker version
Hit a snooker ball dead centre into a stationary ball of the same mass, with no spin, and the first stops dead while the second moves off at the same speed. Players call it a stun shot.
!! Momentum travels by the balls moving => The middle balls barely move; a compression wave passes through them
> Newton's cradle obeys both momentum and energy conservation, which is why exactly as many balls fly out as went in.

# Angular momentum
Angular momentum is the rotational version of momentum. It depends on how fast something spins and how its mass is arranged around the axis. Mass far from the axis counts for much more than mass near it.
= angular momentum = moment of inertia × spin rate
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Arms out", "items": ["mass far from axis", "slow spin"]}, "r": {"t": "Arms in", "items": ["mass close in", "fast spin"]}}
### The spinning skater
With no outside twisting force, angular momentum stays the same. Pulling the arms in reduces the moment of inertia, so the spin rate must go up to compensate. An ice skater can spin several times faster this way.
### Everywhere you look
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Divers tuck to somersault faster", "Office chair spins faster, arms in", "Collapsing stars become pulsars", "Cats twist to land on their feet"]}
### Stars that spin hundreds of times a second
When a massive star's core collapses to a neutron star only about 20 km across, its spin speeds up enormously. Some pulsars rotate over 700 times a second.
### Earth and Moon
Tides are slowly transferring angular momentum from Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit. Earth's day gets slightly longer, and the Moon moves about 3.8 cm further away each year.
!! A spinning skater speeds up by pushing harder => No push is needed; drawing mass inward alone makes the spin faster
> Bring mass closer to the axis and a spinning body spins faster, because angular momentum is conserved.
!? A helicopter needs its tail rotor because the main rotor's spin would otherwise make the body spin the opposite way.

# Gyroscopes
A gyroscope is a spinning wheel or disc mounted so its axis can point freely. Because of angular momentum, a fast-spinning gyroscope keeps pointing in the same direction in space unless a twisting force acts on it.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rigidity", "b": "axis keeps its direction"}, {"a": "Precession", "b": "a twist makes it swivel"}]}
### Precession
Push on a spinning gyroscope's axis and it doesn't simply tip over. It swivels sideways at right angles to your push. A spinning top leaning over traces a slow circle instead of falling for this reason.
### Uses
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Gyroscopes", "items": ["Ship gyrocompasses", "Aircraft instruments", "Telescope pointing (Hubble)", "Phone motion sensors"]}
• French physicist Léon Foucault named the gyroscope in 1852, using one to show Earth's rotation
• Ship gyrocompasses, from the early 1900s, point to true north and ignore magnetism
• The Hubble Space Telescope uses gyroscopes to hold its aim steady
• Your phone's gyroscope is a tiny vibrating structure on a chip, not a spinning wheel; it senses rotation from the Coriolis effect
### And bicycles?
A spinning wheel resists tipping a little, but research shows the main reasons a moving bicycle stays up are its steering geometry and the rider steering into any lean. In 2011, researchers built a bike with no gyroscopic effect that still balanced itself.
!! Gyroscopes are what keep bicycles upright => They help slightly; steering geometry and the rider do most of the work
> A spinning object resists having its axis turned, which makes gyroscopes superb direction-keepers.

# Rockets in space
A rocket carries both fuel and oxidiser. Burning them makes hot gas that shoots out of the nozzle at several kilometres per second. By conservation of momentum, the rocket gains equal momentum in the opposite direction. No air is needed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Burn fuel and oxidiser", "Hot gas blasts backward", "Gas pushes rocket forward", "Rocket gains speed"]}
### Even better in a vacuum
In the lower atmosphere, air pressure pushes back on the exhaust and air resistance drags on the rocket. In space neither happens, so the same engine produces more thrust, and every bit of it goes into speed.
### Mostly fuel
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 90%", "l": "of a launch rocket is fuel"}, {"v": "≈ 3 km/s", "l": "typical exhaust speed"}, {"v": "≈ 9 min", "l": "to reach low orbit"}]}
A rocket must carry fuel to accelerate fuel it will burn later. The Russian teacher Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out the rocket equation in 1903: final speed depends on exhaust speed and on how much of the starting mass is fuel. That's why rockets are built in stages, dropping empty tanks along the way.
### Gentle but efficient
Ion engines push out charged atoms at around 30 km/s. Their thrust is tiny, about the weight of a sheet of paper, but they run for months. ESA's SMART-1 probe used one to reach the Moon in 2004.
!! Rockets push against the air => They push against their own exhaust; in 1920 a newspaper mocked this idea and only corrected itself in 1969
> A rocket moves forward by throwing mass backward, so it needs nothing to push against.

== ph-gases

# Molecules in motion
The idea that a gas is a swarm of tiny, fast-moving particles is called the **kinetic theory of gases**. It explains pressure, temperature and much more with one simple picture.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 500 m/s", "l": "air molecule speed, 20 °C"}, {"v": "2.5 × 10²²", "l": "molecules in a litre of air"}, {"v": "≈ 70 nm", "l": "travel between collisions"}]}
### Faster than a jet airliner
At room temperature an average nitrogen molecule moves at about 500 m/s, faster than a cruising passenger jet. But it never gets far: in air at sea level it hits another molecule after travelling only about 70 nanometres, billions of times a second.
### Pressure is countless tiny impacts
Each molecule bouncing off a wall gives it a minute push. Billions upon billions of pushes every second add up to a steady force. Pressure is that force per square metre, measured in pascals (Pa).
= pressure = force ÷ area
ex: Sea-level pressure ≈ 101,000 Pa = 101,000 N per m² | ÷ 9.81 ≈ 10,300 kg | Like 10 tonnes resting on every square metre
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Molecules fly about", "They hit the walls", "Each hit gives a push", "Pushes add up: pressure"]}
### Why we aren't crushed
Air pushes equally in every direction, including from inside your body, lungs and ears. Forces balance, so we don't notice. Remove the air from one side, as in a vacuum-packed bag, and its force becomes obvious.
!? The jittery dance of pollen grains in water, seen by Robert Brown in 1827, was explained by Einstein in 1905 as molecules bombarding them; this helped convince scientists that atoms are real.
> Gas pressure is the combined push of countless fast molecules hitting a surface.

# Temperature and pressure
Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules. Warm a gas in a rigid container and its molecules hit the walls faster and more often, so pressure rises in proportion to the absolute temperature.
= pressure ÷ absolute temperature = constant (fixed volume)
### Kelvin, the absolute scale
The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, −273.15 °C, where molecular motion is at its minimum. Gas law calculations must use kelvin: 20 °C is about 293 K. Using Celsius gives nonsense, because 0 °C isn't "no heat".
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 400.0, "unit": "kelvin", "items": [{"l": "Absolute zero", "v": 0.0}, {"l": "Liquid nitrogen", "v": 77.0}, {"l": "Water freezes", "v": 273.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Water boils", "v": 373.0}]}
### Tyres after a long drive
ex: Tyre at 2.2 bar on the gauge, 15 °C | Absolute pressure 3.2 bar × (313 K ÷ 288 K) ≈ 3.48 bar | Warm tyre at 40 °C reads about 2.5 bar
This is why manufacturers tell you to check pressures with cold tyres. Letting air out of hot tyres leaves them under-inflated once they cool.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Tyres read higher after driving", "Aerosols: keep below 50 °C", "Footballs go soft in the cold", "Pressure cookers build pressure"]}
!! Absolute zero is like a very cold freezer => It's −273.15 °C, a limit that can be approached but never quite reached
> Heat a sealed gas and its pressure rises in step with the absolute temperature.

# Boyle's law
In 1662 Robert Boyle, working with his assistant Robert Hooke, showed that if you squeeze a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, its pressure rises in exact proportion. Half the volume, double the pressure.
= pressure × volume = constant (fixed temperature)
### Why it works
Squeeze the same molecules into half the space and each square centimetre of wall gets hit twice as often. Their speed hasn't changed, because the temperature hasn't.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Large volume", "items": ["fewer hits per wall", "low pressure"]}, "r": {"t": "Half the volume", "items": ["twice the hits", "double pressure"]}}
### Diving
Water adds about 1 bar of pressure for every 10 m of depth. A breath of air shrinks as a diver descends and expands as they rise.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "volume of 1 litre of surface air, L", "items": [{"l": "Surface (1 bar)", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "10 m (2 bar)", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "20 m (3 bar)", "v": 0.33}, {"l": "30 m (4 bar)", "v": 0.25}]}
Scuba divers breathing compressed air must never hold their breath while surfacing: the air in their lungs would expand and could cause serious injury. The rule taught in every diving course is to breathe normally and ascend slowly.
### Everyday Boyle
• Ears pop on a plane or in a fast lift as air pressure changes
• A sealed bag of crisps puffs up on a flight or a mountain road
• A bicycle pump squeezes air until its pressure exceeds the tyre's and the valve opens
ex: Syringe holds 20 ml of air at 1 bar | Push plunger (outlet blocked) to 10 ml | Pressure rises to 2 bar
> Halve the space a gas has and its pressure doubles, as long as the temperature stays the same.

# The ideal gas law
Three separate discoveries, Boyle's law (1662), Charles's law (1787, volume and temperature) and Gay-Lussac's law (1802, pressure and temperature), combine into one equation. Avogadro's idea, that equal volumes of gas hold equal numbers of molecules, completes it.
= pV = nRT
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "pV = nRT", "items": [{"s": "p", "m": "pressure, Pa"}, {"s": "V", "m": "volume, m³"}, {"s": "n", "m": "amount, moles"}, {"s": "T", "m": "temperature, K (R = 8.31)"}]}
### What's a mole?
A mole is a fixed count of particles, about 6 × 10²³. One mole of air molecules has a mass of about 29 g. The ideal gas law says one mole of any gas takes up about 24 litres at room temperature and normal pressure, whether it's hydrogen or carbon dioxide.
ex: n = 1 mol, T = 293 K, p = 101,325 Pa | V = 1 × 8.31 × 293 ÷ 101,325 | V ≈ 0.024 m³ = 24 litres
### Why "ideal"?
The law assumes molecules are points that don't attract each other. That's an excellent approximation for air in everyday conditions. It breaks down at very high pressures or near the temperature where a gas turns liquid, when molecules are close and start to stick.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fixed T", "b": "Boyle: p × V constant"}, {"a": "Fixed p", "b": "Charles: V grows with T"}, {"a": "Fixed V", "b": "Gay-Lussac: p grows with T"}]}
### Who uses it
Engineers size gas cylinders and car airbags with it; meteorologists use it in weather models; chemists use it to count molecules by measuring volume.
!? A typical car airbag fills with around 60 litres of gas in about 30 milliseconds, produced by a rapid chemical reaction.
> Pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas are tied together by one simple equation.

# Air pressure and altitude
Air pressure is the weight of all the air above you pressing down. Climb higher and there's less air above you, so pressure falls. Air is compressible, so the lower layers are squeezed denser and pressure drops fastest near the ground.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "air pressure, % of sea level (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Sea level", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Serra da Estrela", "v": 79.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Pico, Azores", "v": 76.0}, {"l": "Mont Blanc", "v": 56.0}, {"l": "Everest", "v": 33.0}]}
### Pascal's mountain
In 1648 Blaise Pascal asked his brother-in-law to carry a mercury barometer up the Puy de Dôme in France. The mercury column was several centimetres shorter at the top, proving that air has weight and thins with height.
### Breathing up high
The air at altitude is still about 21% oxygen, but each breath holds fewer molecules. On Everest each breath brings in only about a third as much oxygen as at sea level, which is why most climbers there use bottled oxygen.
### Planes
Airliners cruise at about 11 km, where pressure is under a quarter of sea level. Cabins are pressurised to the equivalent of roughly 1,800–2,400 m, a bit higher than the top of Serra da Estrela.
ex: Pressure halves every ≈ 5.5 km | At 5.5 km: ≈ 50% | At 11 km: ≈ 25%
!! Mountain air has less oxygen in it => It has the same 21% oxygen; there's simply less air in each breath
> Air pressure is the weight of the air above you, so it falls as you climb.

# Boiling and altitude
A liquid boils when bubbles of vapour can form inside it. That needs the liquid's vapour pressure to equal the pressure of the air above. Lower the air pressure and the water reaches that point at a lower temperature.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "boiling point of water, °C (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Pressure cooker", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Sea level", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Serra da Estrela", "v": 93.0}, {"l": "Everest summit", "v": 71.0}]}
### Cooking up a mountain
Cooking depends on temperature, not on bubbles. At about 93 °C on the top of Serra da Estrela, pasta and eggs take noticeably longer. On Everest, water boiling at about 71 °C barely cooks food at all.
### The pressure cooker
A sealed pressure cooker lets steam build up to about 1 bar above atmospheric pressure. Water then boils at about 120 °C, so beans, stews and chickpeas cook in perhaps a third of the usual time.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Lid sealed, water heats", "Steam raises pressure", "Boiling point rises", "Food cooks hotter, faster"]}
### History
The French physicist Denis Papin invented the "steam digester", the ancestor of the pressure cooker, in 1679. Its safety valve inspired later steam engines.
!! Turning up the heat makes boiling water hotter => Open-pan boiling water stays at its boiling point; more heat just boils it away faster
> Water boils when its vapour pressure matches the surrounding pressure, so lower pressure means a lower boiling point.
!? In a vacuum chamber, a glass of room-temperature water will boil, and cool itself until it freezes.

# Balloons and density
Any object in a fluid feels an upward push, or **upthrust**, equal to the weight of the fluid it pushes aside. This is Archimedes' principle. If the upthrust beats the object's weight, it rises.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "density, kg per m³", "items": [{"l": "Cold air, 0 °C", "v": 1.29}, {"l": "Air, 20 °C", "v": 1.2}, {"l": "Hot air, 100 °C", "v": 0.95, "hi": true}, {"l": "Helium", "v": 0.17}]}
### Hot-air balloons
Heating air makes it expand, so a cubic metre of it weighs less. The difference from the surrounding air gives the lift. That's why balloons fly best on cold, calm mornings.
ex: Each m³ of 100 °C air is ≈ 0.25 kg lighter than 20 °C air | Balloon of 2,800 m³ | Lift ≈ 700 kg for basket, burner, envelope and people
### A Portuguese first
In 1709 the Portuguese-Brazilian priest Bartolomeu de Gusmão demonstrated a small hot-air balloon to King João V in Lisbon, more than 70 years before the Montgolfier brothers' famous crewed flight in Paris in 1783.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1709", "l": "Gusmão's balloon, Lisbon"}, {"y": "1783", "l": "Montgolfier crewed flight"}, {"y": "1937", "l": "Hindenburg disaster"}]}
### Floating in water
• A submarine floods ballast tanks to sink and blows them out with compressed air to rise
• Fish adjust gas in a swim bladder
• Ships float because their hulls displace a large volume of water
!! Helium balloons are pulled up => They're pushed up by the heavier air around them
> Things rise when they're less dense than the fluid around them.

# Suction isn't pulling
Gases can only push; there's no such thing as a pulling force from a vacuum. "Suction" always means lowering the pressure on one side so that the higher pressure on the other side pushes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["You expand your mouth", "Pressure in mouth drops", "Air pushes on the drink", "Drink rises up the straw"]}
### The 10-metre limit
Even a perfect vacuum at the top of a straw can only let the atmosphere push water up until the water column's weight matches air pressure. That happens at about 10.3 m.
ex: Atmospheric pressure ≈ 101,000 Pa | Height = 101,000 ÷ (1,000 kg/m³ × 9.81) | ≈ 10.3 m of water
### How it was discovered
In the 1600s, Italian well-diggers found their suction pumps couldn't lift water more than about 10 m. Evangelista Torricelli realised why and in 1643 replaced water with mercury, which is 13.6 times denser. The mercury stood about 76 cm high: the first barometer.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Water column", "items": ["about 10.3 m", "held up by air"]}, "r": {"t": "Mercury column", "items": ["about 76 cm", "13.6 × denser"]}}
### Magdeburg's hemispheres
In 1654 Otto von Guericke pumped the air out of two joined copper hemispheres. Teams of horses pulling in opposite directions couldn't separate them. Let the air back in, and they fell apart.
!! Vacuum cleaners suck up dirt => A fan lowers the pressure inside, and outside air rushes in, carrying dust
> Suction is really the atmosphere pushing into a region of lower pressure.

# Compressed air
Pushing a piston into a gas does work on the molecules: each one bounces off the advancing piston faster than it arrived, so the gas heats up. When a gas expands and pushes something outwards, its molecules lose speed and it cools.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Compress", "items": ["work done on gas", "temperature rises"]}, "r": {"t": "Expand", "items": ["gas does work", "temperature falls"]}}
### Diesel engines
Rudolf Diesel patented his engine in the 1890s. It squeezes air to roughly a fifteenth or twentieth of its volume, heating it to over 500 °C. Fuel sprayed in then ignites without any spark.
### Weather
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rising air", "b": "expands, cools, forms clouds"}, {"a": "Sinking air", "b": "compressed, warms, dries"}, {"a": "Föhn winds", "b": "warm, dry wind down mountains"}]}
Rising air expands and cools by roughly 10 °C per kilometre until it becomes saturated. That cooling turns water vapour into the droplets of clouds.
### Fridges and heat pumps
A refrigerant is compressed (getting hot) and gives its heat away through coils behind the fridge. It then passes through a valve, expands and turns cold, picking up heat from inside the fridge. The cycle repeats.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Compress: gets hot", "Release heat outside", "Expand: gets cold", "Absorb heat inside"]}
!? A CO₂ fire extinguisher's horn can frost over when used, because the escaping gas expands and cools dramatically.
> Squeezing a gas warms it and letting it expand cools it, and that's the heart of every fridge.

# Weather and pressure
Average sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa. Weather maps show lines of equal pressure called **isobars**. Regions of higher and lower pressure drive the winds and shape the weather.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "High pressure", "items": ["air sinks and warms", "clear, settled skies"]}, "r": {"t": "Low pressure", "items": ["air rises and cools", "cloud, wind, rain"]}}
### Why the wind spirals
Air flows from high pressure towards low, but Earth's rotation deflects it. In the northern hemisphere winds circle clockwise around highs and anticlockwise around lows. Closely packed isobars mean strong winds.
### Portugal's weather maker
The **Azores High** is a semi-permanent high-pressure area over the Atlantic. In summer it usually extends towards Iberia, blocking rain and bringing Portugal's dry, sunny months. In winter it often retreats south, letting Atlantic low-pressure systems bring rain.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 860.0, "hi": 1090.0, "unit": "hPa", "items": [{"l": "Record low", "v": 870.0}, {"l": "Average", "v": 1013.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Record high", "v": 1084.0}]}
### Reading a barometer
• A slow rise: improving, settled weather
• A steady reading: little change
• A fast fall, several hPa in three hours: wind and rain on the way
ex: 1 hPa = 100 Pa = 1 millibar | Sea-level pressure ≈ 1013 hPa | Strong storm centre ≈ 960 hPa or lower
!? Since 2017 Portugal's weather service, IPMA, names major storms together with the Spanish and French services.
> High pressure brings settled weather; low pressure brings rising air, cloud and rain.

== ph-light

# Light travels fast and straight
The speed of light in a vacuum is exactly 299,792,458 m/s. It's exact because since 1983 the metre has been defined by it: a metre is the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "time for light to arrive", "items": [{"l": "Moon", "v": 1.3, "t": "1.3 s"}, {"l": "Sun", "v": 500.0, "t": "8 min 20 s", "hi": true}, {"l": "Jupiter (avg)", "v": 2600.0, "t": "≈ 43 min"}]}
### Measuring it
In 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer noticed that eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io came early when Earth was near Jupiter and late when far away. He realised light takes time to cross the extra distance. In 1849 Hippolyte Fizeau measured it on Earth using a spinning toothed wheel and a mirror 8 km away.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1676", "l": "Rømer: light has a speed"}, {"y": "1849", "l": "Fizeau's toothed wheel"}, {"y": "1983", "l": "Metre defined by light"}]}
### Straight lines make shadows
Because light travels in straight lines in a uniform material, opaque objects cast shadows with clear edges. Solar and lunar eclipses are shadows on a cosmic scale. A pinhole camera works the same way: rays from the top of a scene pass through the hole to the bottom of the screen, giving an upside-down image.
### Slower in materials
Light slows down inside transparent materials: about 225,000 km/s in water and about 200,000 km/s in glass. This slowing is what makes lenses work.
ex: Sun to Earth ≈ 150,000,000 km | ÷ 300,000 km/s | ≈ 500 s = 8 min 20 s
!? When you look at a star, you see light that left it years or even thousands of years ago; you see the past.
> Light is the fastest thing there is, and it travels in straight lines unless something bends it.

# Reflection
The law of reflection is simple: the angle at which light hits a surface equals the angle at which it leaves, both measured from a line at right angles to the surface (the "normal").
= angle of incidence = angle of reflection
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Smooth (specular)", "items": ["mirror, still water", "clear image"]}, "r": {"t": "Rough (diffuse)", "items": ["paper, walls", "no image, even glow"]}}
### Why paper isn't a mirror
Every tiny bit of a surface obeys the same law. On paper, the fibres point every which way, so rays scatter in all directions. That diffuse reflection is actually what lets us see most objects from any angle.
### Mirror images
A flat mirror's image appears as far behind the glass as you are in front. It looks left-right reversed, though strictly it's front-to-back reversed. To see your whole body, the mirror only needs to be half your height, wherever you stand.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of light reflected (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Fresh snow", "v": 85.0}, {"l": "Mirror", "v": 95.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Grass", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Asphalt", "v": 7.0}]}
### Bouncing light back
• Road cat's eyes and bike reflectors return light straight to its source
• Apollo astronauts left reflectors on the Moon; observatories still bounce lasers off them to measure the Moon's distance to within millimetres
• Fresh snow reflects so much sunlight that skiers need sunglasses
!! A mirror swaps left and right => It swaps front and back; your raised right hand stays on the right side of the image
> Light bounces off a surface at the same angle it arrives.

# Refraction
When light passes at an angle from one material to another where it travels at a different speed, it changes direction. Entering a slower material, it bends towards the normal; leaving into a faster one, it bends away.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Air", "b": "n ≈ 1.00"}, {"a": "Water", "b": "n ≈ 1.33"}, {"a": "Glass", "b": "n ≈ 1.5"}, {"a": "Diamond", "b": "n ≈ 2.42"}]}
= refractive index n = speed in vacuum ÷ speed in material
### The marching analogy
Imagine soldiers marching diagonally from tarmac onto sand. The first to reach the sand slow down first, so the whole row swings round. Light waves behave the same way at a boundary.
### Pools look shallow
Light from the bottom of a pool bends away from the normal as it leaves the water. Your brain assumes light travels straight, so it traces the rays back to a point higher than the real bottom.
ex: Pool 2 m deep, seen from straight above | Apparent depth ≈ real depth ÷ 1.33 | Looks about 1.5 m deep
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Straws look bent in a glass", "Pools look shallower", "Road mirages on hot days", "Sun visible just after it sets"]}
### Atmospheric refraction
Air bends light a little too. Near the horizon it lifts the setting Sun's image by about its own width, so you see the Sun for a couple of minutes after it has geometrically set. Hot air over a road bends skylight upwards, creating the "wet road" mirage.
!! Light bends because it hits the surface => It bends because it changes speed; light hitting straight on slows but doesn't bend
> Light bends when it changes speed crossing from one material into another.

# Total internal reflection
When light travels from a slow material like glass into a faster one like air, it bends away from the normal. Make the angle shallower and shallower, and at the **critical angle** the light skims along the surface. Beyond that, none escapes: all of it reflects back inside.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "critical angle, degrees", "items": [{"l": "Water", "v": 49.0}, {"l": "Glass", "v": 42.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Diamond", "v": 24.0}]}
### Optical fibres
An optical fibre is a glass strand thinner than a hair, with a core surrounded by cladding of slightly lower refractive index. Light zigzags along the core, reflecting completely at every bounce, even round gentle bends.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Laser pulses data", "Light enters glass core", "Reflects at every bounce", "Received thousands of km on"]}
### The internet runs on light
Undersea fibre cables carry the vast majority of data between continents. Portugal is an important landing point: the EllaLink cable, opened in 2021, links Sines directly to Brazil. Signals are boosted by amplifiers every 50–100 km or so.
### Other uses
• Endoscopes let doctors see inside the body through a flexible tube
• Diamonds sparkle because their small critical angle traps and bounces light inside before it escapes
• Binoculars use prisms that reflect light totally, better than mirrors
!? Charles Kao shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for showing in the 1960s that ultra-pure glass could carry light over long distances.
> Light hitting a surface beyond the critical angle can't escape, so glass fibres can trap it for thousands of kilometres.

# Lenses
A lens uses refraction at two curved surfaces to bend rays in a controlled way. Its strength is described by its focal length: the distance from the lens to the point where parallel rays meet.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Convex (converging)", "items": ["thicker in middle", "brings rays together"]}, "r": {"t": "Concave (diverging)", "items": ["thinner in middle", "spreads rays apart"]}}
### Dioptres
Opticians measure lens power in dioptres: one divided by the focal length in metres. Positive numbers are converging lenses, negative are diverging.
= power (dioptres) = 1 ÷ focal length (m)
ex: Reading glasses of +2.5 dioptres | Focal length = 1 ÷ 2.5 | = 0.4 m, or 40 cm
### Real and virtual images
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Object far away", "b": "small, upside down (camera)"}, {"a": "Object near focus", "b": "large, upside down (projector)"}, {"a": "Object inside focus", "b": "magnified, upright (magnifier)"}]}
### A short history
Eyeglasses for reading appeared in Italy around 1286. Spectacle makers in the Netherlands built the first telescopes around 1608, and Galileo turned one to the sky in 1609, discovering Jupiter's moons. Microscopes followed soon after, revealing cells and bacteria.
!! A magnifying glass makes things bigger => It makes rays enter your eye as if from a larger, virtual object
> Convex lenses bring light together to a focus; concave lenses spread it apart.

# The eye
The eye is a living camera. About two-thirds of its focusing is done by the curved, clear **cornea** at the front. The **lens** behind it fine-tunes focus by changing shape, pulled by tiny muscles.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cornea bends light", "Iris sets pupil size", "Lens fine-tunes focus", "Retina detects light"]}
### The retina
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rods", "items": ["about 120 million", "dim light, no colour"]}, "r": {"t": "Cones", "items": ["about 6 million", "bright light, colour"]}}
The cones are packed into a small central spot, the fovea, which gives sharp, coloured vision where you look directly. Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no receptors at all: a blind spot that your brain fills in.
### The image is upside down
Like a camera, the eye projects an inverted image on the retina. Your brain interprets it so the world appears the right way up. Experiments with image-flipping goggles show people can adapt to an upside-down view within days.
### Adapting to light
• The pupil changes from about 2 mm in sunshine to about 8 mm in the dark
• Full dark adaptation, as rods become more sensitive, takes 20–30 minutes
• That's why astronomers use dim red torches: red light barely disturbs the rods
!? Underwater, everything looks blurry because the cornea loses most of its focusing power when water, not air, touches it; goggles restore an air gap.
!! Carrots give you night vision => Vitamin A is needed for healthy eyes, but extra carrots won't improve normal vision
> The cornea and lens focus an upside-down image on the retina, and the brain does the rest.

# Short and long sight
For sharp vision, light must focus exactly on the retina. Often the eyeball is a little too long or too short, or the cornea too curved or too flat, and the focus lands in the wrong place.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Short sight (myopia)", "items": ["focus in front of retina", "far things blurry", "concave lens (−)"]}, "r": {"t": "Long sight", "items": ["focus behind retina", "near things blurry", "convex lens (+)"]}}
### Reading a prescription
Glasses prescriptions are in dioptres. A minus number, such as −2.00, means myopia; a plus number means long sight. A separate "cylinder" value corrects **astigmatism**, where the cornea is curved more in one direction than another.
### A growing epidemic
Myopia is becoming far more common. Roughly a third of the world's people are short-sighted, and some projections suggest half will be by 2050. In some East Asian cities over 80% of young adults are myopic.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["More close-up work and screens", "Less time outdoors in daylight", "Inherited from parents"]}
Strong evidence shows that children who spend more time outdoors are less likely to become short-sighted, probably because of bright daylight and looking at distant things.
### After 40
In middle age the eye's lens stiffens and can't change shape enough to focus up close. This is **presbyopia**, and it's why most people need reading glasses in their forties, even with otherwise perfect eyesight.
> Short-sighted eyes focus too early and need diverging lenses; long-sighted eyes focus too late and need converging ones.

# Cameras
Like the eye, a camera uses a lens to form an image on a light-sensitive surface. Instead of rods and cones, a digital sensor has millions of tiny light detectors called pixels, each recording how much light falls on it.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Aperture", "b": "hole size: light, depth of field"}, {"a": "Shutter speed", "b": "exposure time: motion blur"}, {"a": "ISO", "b": "sensor gain: grainy noise"}]}
### f-numbers
Aperture is given as an f-number: the focal length divided by the hole's diameter. Smaller numbers mean wider holes. Each standard "stop" (f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8) halves the light.
ex: 50 mm lens at f/2 | Aperture diameter = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 mm | At f/4 it's 12.5 mm, letting in a quarter of the light
### Freezing motion
A shutter speed of 1/1000 s freezes a running dog. Half a second lets moving water blur into silk. Too long a handheld exposure and the whole picture blurs from camera shake.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1826", "l": "First lasting photo"}, {"y": "1888", "l": "Kodak roll-film camera"}, {"y": "1969", "l": "CCD sensor invented"}, {"y": "2000s", "l": "Cameras in phones"}]}
### Phone cameras
Phone sensors are small, so each pixel gathers little light. Phones make up for it with computing: they capture several frames in a burst and merge them, reducing noise and balancing bright and dark areas.
!? The first surviving photograph, taken by Nicéphore Niépce in France around 1826–27, needed an exposure of many hours, possibly days.
> A camera balances aperture, shutter speed and sensitivity to collect just the right amount of light.

# Rainbows
Each raindrop acts like a tiny prism with a mirror at the back. Sunlight refracts as it enters, reflects off the back of the drop, and refracts again leaving. Violet bends a little more than red, so the colours fan out.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sunlight enters drop", "Refracts, colours split", "Reflects off the back", "Refracts out at about 42°"]}
### Where to look
The rainbow is a circle centred on the point directly opposite the Sun, the shadow of your head. Red light comes out at about 42° from that point, violet at about 40°. So you need the Sun behind you and fairly low, which is why rainbows appear mostly in the morning and late afternoon.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Primary bow", "items": ["about 42°", "one reflection", "red on the outside"]}, "r": {"t": "Secondary bow", "items": ["about 51°", "two reflections", "colours reversed"]}}
### Your own private rainbow
Each person sees light from a different set of drops, so no two people see exactly the same rainbow. You can never reach the end of one; it moves as you move. From a plane, you can sometimes see the full circle.
### Who explained it
René Descartes worked out the 42° angle in 1637 by tracing rays through a glass globe of water. Newton later explained the colours, showing that white light is a mix that refraction separates.
!! A rainbow has seven distinct colours => The spectrum is continuous; Newton chose seven names, partly to match the seven notes of a musical scale
> Raindrops refract, reflect and refract sunlight, sending each colour back at a slightly different angle.
!? The sky between a primary and secondary rainbow is noticeably darker; it's called Alexander's dark band.

# Lasers
"Laser" stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Einstein described stimulated emission in 1917: a photon passing an excited atom can trigger it to release an identical photon. Theodore Maiman built the first working laser, using a ruby crystal, in 1960.

### What makes laser light special

### Laser light in your life

# White light is a mix
In 1666, the young Isaac Newton darkened his room, let a thin beam of sunlight through a hole in the shutter and passed it through a glass prism. A band of colours appeared on the far wall. Many people thought the glass was somehow adding the colour. Newton's key step was to pass one colour through a second prism: it stayed the same colour. Then he recombined the whole band and got white again.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["White sunlight", "Enters a glass prism", "Each colour bends differently", "Spectrum on the wall"]}
### Why a prism splits light
Light slows down in glass, and the slowdown bends it, an effect called **refraction**. Short wavelengths such as violet slow slightly more than long ones such as red, so they bend more. The colours fan out by wavelength. Raindrops do the same thing, which is how rainbows form, each at about 42° from the point opposite the Sun.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 380.0, "hi": 750.0, "unit": "wavelength, nm", "items": [{"l": "Violet", "v": 420.0}, {"l": "Green", "v": 530.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Yellow", "v": 580.0}, {"l": "Red", "v": 680.0}]}
### How small are these waves?
A nanometre (nm) is a millionth of a millimetre. Visible light spans about 380 to 750 nm, so roughly 2,000 waves of green light would fit across one millimetre.
= speed of light = frequency × wavelength
ex: Green light, 530 nm | 300,000,000 m/s ÷ 0.000 000 53 m | ≈ 570 trillion waves per second
!? Newton named seven colours, including indigo, partly to match the seven notes of a musical scale; most people today can't pick out indigo as a separate band.
> White light isn't pure: it's a blend of every visible wavelength, and a prism simply sorts them.

# Objects absorb and reflect
An object has no colour of its own in the dark. Its colour is the light it sends back to your eyes. Molecules in the surface absorb some wavelengths, turning their energy into heat, and reflect or scatter the rest.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["White light hits a leaf", "Red and blue absorbed", "Green reflected", "Your eye sees green"]}
### Leaves and chlorophyll
**Chlorophyll** uses red and blue light to power photosynthesis, and reflects much of the green, which is less useful to it. In autumn, deciduous trees break down their chlorophyll and recycle its nutrients. The yellow and orange pigments that were there all along, called carotenoids, show through.
### Black, white and heat
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Black shirt", "items": ["absorbs most light", "warms up fast"]}, "r": {"t": "White shirt", "items": ["reflects most light", "stays cooler"]}}
This is why houses in the Alentejo and Algarve are traditionally whitewashed: a white wall can reflect over 80% of sunlight, keeping the rooms inside cooler.
### Colour depends on the light
• A red car under the yellow light of an old sodium street lamp looks dark brown or grey: there is little red light to reflect
• A strawberry lit only with blue light looks almost black
• Clothes that match in a shop can clash in daylight, because each light source contains a different mix of wavelengths
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Red apple", "b": "reflects red, absorbs the rest"}, {"a": "White paper", "b": "reflects all colours"}, {"a": "Black coal", "b": "absorbs nearly all"}]}
!! An object "is" red => It reflects red light; change the lighting and its colour changes too
> The colour you see is the light an object doesn't absorb.

# Mixing light vs mixing paint
There are two completely different ways of mixing colour. Light sources **add** wavelengths together. Paints, inks and dyes **subtract** wavelengths, because each pigment absorbs part of the light that falls on it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Additive (light)", "items": ["red, green, blue", "all three make white"]}, "r": {"t": "Subtractive (ink)", "items": ["cyan, magenta, yellow", "all three make dark"]}}
### Additive mixing
Shine red, green and blue spotlights onto a white wall, overlapping:
• Red + green = yellow
• Green + blue = cyan
• Blue + red = magenta
• All three = white
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Red + green", "b": "yellow"}, {"a": "Green + blue", "b": "cyan"}, {"a": "Red + blue", "b": "magenta"}, {"a": "All three", "b": "white"}]}
### Subtractive mixing
Cyan ink absorbs red, magenta absorbs green and yellow absorbs blue. Overlap cyan and yellow and only green survives both, so you see green. Mix all three and almost nothing is reflected. In practice that gives a muddy dark brown, which is why printers add a separate black ink, the K in **CMYK**. Black ink is also cheaper and prints sharper text.
ex: Cyan ink removes red | Yellow ink removes blue | Only green is left: cyan + yellow = green
### What about red, yellow and blue?
Painters have used red, yellow and blue as primaries for centuries, and they work reasonably well. But cyan, magenta and yellow can mix a wider range of colours, which is why modern printing uses them.
!? Brown doesn't appear in the rainbow: it's really dark orange, seen next to brighter colours.
> Light adds colours towards white; pigments take colours away towards black.

# Why the sky is blue
Sunlight passing through the atmosphere hits nitrogen and oxygen molecules, which are far smaller than the wavelength of light. They make the light's electric field jiggle their electrons, which then re-radiate light in all directions. This is **Rayleigh scattering**, named after Lord Rayleigh, who worked out the maths in 1871.
### Strongly favouring blue
Scattering by tiny particles depends on the fourth power of wavelength: halve the wavelength and the scattering rises 16 times.
= scattering ∝ 1 ÷ wavelength⁴
ex: Red 700 nm, blue 450 nm | (700 ÷ 450)⁴ ≈ 1.56 × 1.56 × 1.56 × 1.56 | Blue scattered about 6 times more
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "scattering, relative to red", "items": [{"l": "Red, 700 nm", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Green, 530 nm", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Blue, 450 nm", "v": 5.9, "t": "6", "hi": true}]}
### Why not violet?
Violet is scattered even more, but sunlight contains less violet than blue, some is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are much less sensitive to it. The mix of scattered colours that reaches us looks sky blue.
### Clouds and space
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tiny molecules", "items": ["scatter blue most", "blue sky"]}, "r": {"t": "Cloud droplets", "items": ["scatter all colours", "white clouds"]}}
Water droplets in clouds are much bigger than the wavelength of light, so they scatter all colours equally and look white or grey. On the Moon there's no air to scatter light, so the sky is black even in daylight.
!? On Mars the daytime sky is butterscotch-coloured because of fine dust, while sunsets there look bluish.
> The sky is blue because air molecules scatter blue sunlight across the whole sky far more than red.

# Red sunsets
When the Sun is overhead, its light crosses roughly 10 km of dense air. When it sits on the horizon, the light travels a slanting path through air equivalent to almost 40 times as much. Along that long path, blue and green are scattered out to the sides, lighting up skies elsewhere, and mainly red and orange get through.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Midday", "items": ["short path through air", "white Sun, blue sky"]}, "r": {"t": "Sunset", "items": ["about 40 × more air", "red-orange Sun"]}}
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Low Sun", "Long path through air", "Blue scattered away", "Reds and oranges remain"]}
### Dust, smoke and volcanoes
Particles in the air can make sunsets more vivid or turn the Sun deep red in the middle of the day.
• Saharan dust blown over Portugal and Spain can turn skies orange and leave dust on cars
• Wildfire smoke can make the Sun look like a red disc you can almost look at (never stare at it)
• After the 1883 Krakatoa eruption, brilliant red sunsets were seen around the world for months
### More sunset tricks
The air also bends sunlight, so you see the Sun for a couple of minutes after it has actually dropped below the horizon. The bending is stronger near the ground, which squashes the Sun into an oval. In very clear air, the last sliver can flash green for a second, the famous **green flash**, best seen over a sea horizon such as Portugal's west coast.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Long path: reds survive", "Dust and smoke: deeper red", "Refraction: flattened Sun", "Green flash: last second"]}
!! Sunsets are red because the Sun cools in the evening => The Sun doesn't change; its light just crosses far more air
> Sunsets are red because the blue has been scattered away along the long path through the air.

# Seeing colour
Your retina holds about 120 million **rods** and about 6 million **cones**. Rods are very sensitive but can't tell colours apart, which is why everything looks grey in moonlight. Cones work in brighter light and come in three types.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "S cones", "b": "peak near 420 nm, blue-violet"}, {"a": "M cones", "b": "peak near 535 nm, green"}, {"a": "L cones", "b": "peak near 565 nm, yellow-green"}]}
### The brain compares
Each cone type responds to a wide band of wavelengths, overlapping a lot. A single cone can't tell colour; it only signals how much light it absorbs. Your brain compares the signals from the three types. Strong L and M signals with little from S feel like yellow, even though there is no "yellow cone".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Light enters eye", "Three cone types respond", "Brain compares signals", "You see a colour"]}
### Colours that aren't in the rainbow
Magenta stimulates L and S cones but not M. No single wavelength does that, so magenta isn't in the spectrum; your brain invents it. Brown, pink and grey are also mixtures your brain labels, not single wavelengths.
• Most mammals, including dogs, have only two cone types
• Many birds, reptiles and fish have four, including one for ultraviolet
• Humans can tell apart roughly a million different shades
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "number of cone types", "items": [{"l": "Dog", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Human", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Many birds", "v": 4.0}]}
!! The mantis shrimp sees far more colours than us => It has 12 or more receptor types but, in tests, is worse than humans at telling similar colours apart
> Colour isn't in the light itself; it's your brain's comparison of three cone signals.

# Colour blindness
Most red–green colour vision deficiency is inherited. The genes for the M and L cone pigments sit on the X chromosome. Men have only one X, so one faulty copy is enough. Women have two, so they need faulty copies on both, which is much rarer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "red–green deficiency, %", "items": [{"l": "Men", "v": 8.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Women", "v": 0.5}]}
### The main types
• Deuteranomaly: green-sensitive cones shifted towards red; the most common form, about 5% of men
• Protan types: weak or missing red-sensitive cones; reds look darker
• Tritan types: blue–yellow problems, very rare
• Complete colour blindness, seeing only shades of grey, is extremely rare
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Colour vision deficiency", "items": ["Deutan: green cones", "Protan: red cones", "Tritan: blue cones"]}
### Daltonism
The chemist John Dalton described his own colour vision in 1794, after noticing that a flower looked different to him than to others. Colour blindness is still called **daltonismo** in Portuguese. Screening often uses the Ishihara test, plates of coloured dots hiding numbers, first published in 1917.
### Designing for everyone
Good design adds labels, patterns, shapes or position, so meaning never depends on colour alone. Traffic lights use a fixed order. The Portuguese designer Miguel Neiva created **ColorADD**, a code of simple symbols for colours, now used on school materials, transport maps and products in Portugal and abroad.

!? In a typical school class of 30 with 15 boys, chances are at least one boy has a red–green deficiency.
> About one man in twelve sees colours differently, so colour should never be the only clue.

# Infrared and ultraviolet
Visible light is a narrow slice of the **electromagnetic spectrum**. Just beyond each end of the rainbow lie wavelengths our eyes can't detect, but many animals and instruments can.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Ultraviolet: below 400 nm", "Visible: 400–700 nm", "Infrared: above 700 nm"]}
### Discovering them
In 1800 William Herschel placed thermometers in the colours of a spectrum. The temperature rose towards red, and was highest just beyond the red end, where there was no visible light: he had found **infrared**. A year later Johann Ritter found that something beyond violet darkened silver chloride: ultraviolet.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1666", "l": "Newton: the spectrum"}, {"y": "1800", "l": "Herschel: infrared"}, {"y": "1801", "l": "Ritter: ultraviolet"}]}
### Infrared around you
• Your body glows in infrared at about 10 µm; thermal cameras turn this into images
• TV remotes flash infrared at about 940 nm; many phone cameras can see the flashes
• Night-vision cameras light scenes with infrared lamps
### Ultraviolet
UV-A (315–400 nm) ages skin and passes through window glass. UV-B (280–315 nm) causes sunburn and drives skin cancer risk. UV-C is the most energetic but is blocked by the ozone layer. In Portugal the summer UV index often reaches 8–10, rated very high.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "UV-A", "b": "ageing, passes through glass"}, {"a": "UV-B", "b": "sunburn, skin damage"}, {"a": "UV-C", "b": "stopped by ozone"}]}
### Glowing in UV
**Fluorescent** materials absorb UV and re-emit visible light. Tonic water glows blue because of its quinine, white shirts glow because of brighteners in washing powder, and euro banknotes show hidden glowing marks under UV lamps.
!? Bees see ultraviolet, and many flowers have UV "landing strip" patterns that are invisible to us.
> Infrared and ultraviolet are just light our eyes can't see: one we feel as warmth, the other we feel as sunburn.

# Colour temperature
Heat anything enough and it glows. A stove ring glows dull red, a filament yellow-white, and the hottest stars blue-white. The colour of this glow depends only on temperature. **Colour temperature** describes a light source by comparing it with an ideal glowing object, measured in kelvin (K). 0 K is absolute zero, −273 °C.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1000.0, "hi": 10000.0, "unit": "kelvin", "items": [{"l": "Candle", "v": 1900.0}, {"l": "Warm bulb", "v": 2700.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Midday Sun", "v": 5500.0}, {"l": "Blue sky", "v": 9000.0}]}
### The "warm" paradox
Artists call red and orange "warm" and blue "cool". But in physics, bluer light comes from hotter sources. A "cool" daylight bulb at 6,500 K matches a hotter glowing body than a "warm" 2,700 K bulb. LED bulbs don't actually get that hot; they are designed to produce the same mix of colours.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "2,700 K warm white", "items": ["yellowish, cosy", "living rooms, evenings"]}, "r": {"t": "5,000–6,500 K", "items": ["bluish, crisp", "offices, workshops"]}}
### Peak colour and temperature
Hotter objects emit most strongly at shorter wavelengths, a rule called **Wien's law**.
= peak wavelength ≈ 2,900,000 nm·K ÷ temperature
ex: Sun's surface ≈ 5,800 K | 2,900,000 ÷ 5,800 | Peak ≈ 500 nm, blue-green
### In daily life
• Cameras and phones adjust white balance so white paper looks white under any light
• Evening light rich in blue can make it harder for some people to feel sleepy
• Stars show it too: Betelgeuse is reddish at about 3,500 K, Rigel blue-white at about 12,000 K
!? The Sun's light peaks in blue-green, yet it looks white because it contains a balanced mix of all visible colours.
> In physics, hotter means bluer: "warm" light actually comes from cooler sources.

# Screens
Look closely at a screen with a magnifying glass, or a drop of water on a phone, and you'll see tiny red, green and blue elements. From normal viewing distance they're too small to see apart, so your eye blends them and you see one mixed colour.

Most screens control each sub-pixel in 256 steps, from 0 to 255, called 8-bit colour.

### LCD versus OLED
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "LCD", "items": ["white backlight", "filters and shutters"]}, "r": {"t": "OLED", "items": ["each sub-pixel glows", "true black"]}}
An LCD shines a backlight through liquid crystals that act as tiny shutters, and colour filters. Because the backlight never fully switches off, blacks look slightly grey. In an OLED screen each sub-pixel makes its own light, so black pixels are simply off, which also saves power with dark themes.

# What sound is
When a guitar string or loudspeaker cone moves outwards, it pushes air molecules together, making a **compression**. When it moves back, it leaves a region of lower pressure, a **rarefaction**. These regions travel outwards as a wave. The air itself doesn't travel; each molecule just jiggles back and forth and passes the push on.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Object vibrates", "Pushes and pulls air", "Compressions travel out", "Eardrum vibrates"]}
### A longitudinal wave
In sound, air moves back and forth in the same direction as the wave travels, so it's called a **longitudinal** wave. Push a Slinky spring along its length and you'll see pulses of bunched-up coils travel along it in the same way. Waves on water or a shaken rope are transverse: they move up and down across the direction of travel.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Longitudinal", "items": ["sound, Slinky push", "back and forth"]}, "r": {"t": "Transverse", "items": ["rope, water ripples", "up and down"]}}
### Tiny pressure changes
Your ears are astonishingly sensitive. Ordinary conversation changes air pressure by only about 0.02 pascals, against atmospheric pressure of about 101,000 Pa. The quietest sound you can hear is a change of just 0.00002 Pa.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "101,000 Pa", "l": "normal air pressure"}, {"v": "0.02 Pa", "l": "conversation changes it"}, {"v": "0.00002 Pa", "l": "quietest audible sound"}]}
ex: Speed of sound 343 m/s, note of 343 Hz | wavelength = 343 ÷ 343 | = 1 metre between compressions
= wavelength = speed ÷ frequency
!? The quietest sound a young ear can detect moves the eardrum by less than the width of an atom.
> Sound is a travelling pattern of squeezes and stretches in the air, not air flowing towards you.

# Pitch and frequency
**Frequency** is how many vibrations happen each second, measured in hertz (Hz). A high frequency sounds high-pitched; a low frequency sounds low. Doubling the frequency raises the pitch by exactly one octave, which is why notes an octave apart sound so alike.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 20.0, "hi": 20000.0, "log": true, "unit": "frequency, Hz", "items": [{"l": "Lowest piano", "v": 27.5}, {"l": "Concert A", "v": 440.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Top of piano", "v": 4186.0}, {"l": "Mosquito area", "v": 15000.0}]}
### Familiar frequencies
• Orchestras tune to the A above middle C at 440 Hz, an international standard
• Adult male speaking voices mostly vibrate around 85–180 Hz, female voices around 165–255 Hz
• A piano spans about 27.5 Hz to 4,186 Hz, but its overtones go much higher
### Hearing range and age
Babies can hear up to about 20,000 Hz. The hair cells that detect the highest frequencies are the most easily worn out, so the top limit falls with age. Many adults over 50 can't hear above about 12,000–14,000 Hz. This is called **presbycusis**.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "highest frequency heard, kHz", "items": [{"l": "Human", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Dog", "v": 45.0}, {"l": "Bat", "v": 200.0, "hi": true}]}
### The Doppler effect
When an ambulance races towards you, the waves in front are squashed together and the siren sounds higher. As it passes and moves away, they stretch out and the pitch drops.
!? Some shops have used high-pitched "mosquito" alarms at around 17,000 Hz to discourage teenagers from loitering, because most adults can't hear them.
> Pitch is frequency: double the vibrations per second and the note goes up an octave.

# Loudness and decibels
The ear can handle an enormous range of sound intensities: the loudest bearable sound carries about a million million times more energy per second than the faintest you can hear. A linear scale would be useless, so we use **decibels** (dB), a logarithmic scale where 0 dB is the threshold of hearing.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "+10 dB", "items": [{"s": "×10", "m": "sound intensity"}, {"s": "×2", "m": "how loud it feels"}, {"s": "+3 dB", "m": "intensity doubled"}]}
### Everyday levels
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "decibels, dB (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Whisper", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Conversation", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Busy street", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Rock concert", "v": 110.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Jet at 30 m", "v": 140.0}]}
### Working with decibels
Because the scale is logarithmic, decibels don't add up the normal way. Two identical sound sources together are only 3 dB louder than one, not twice the decibels.
ex: Conversation 60 dB to busy street 90 dB | +30 dB = 10 × 10 × 10 | 1,000 × the intensity, about 8 × as loud
### Weighted for human ears
Our ears are most sensitive around 2,000–5,000 Hz and much less to very low or very high sounds. Noise meters usually use an **A-weighting**, written dB(A), that mimics this, so a deep rumble counts for less than a shrill whine of the same energy.
• The "bel" is named after Alexander Graham Bell; a decibel is a tenth of a bel
• Sound above about 120–130 dB becomes painful
!! 80 dB is twice as loud as 40 dB => 80 dB has 10,000 times the intensity and sounds about 16 times as loud
> Every 10 dB means ten times the sound energy, so small numbers on the scale hide huge differences.

# Hearing damage
Deep in the inner ear, each cochlea has about 15,000 **hair cells** topped with tiny bundles of stiff hairs. Loud sound bends them too far, damaging or killing them. In humans, they don't grow back, so the loss is permanent. It often starts at high frequencies, around 4,000 Hz.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Loud sound", "Hair cells overloaded", "Cells damaged or die", "Permanent hearing loss"]}
### Dose: level × time
Damage depends on both loudness and duration. A common rule is that 85 dB is safe for up to 8 hours a day, and every extra 3 dB, a doubling of energy, halves the safe time.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "safe time per day, hours", "items": [{"l": "85 dB", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "91 dB", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "97 dB", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "100 dB", "v": 0.25, "hi": true}]}
ex: 85 dB: 8 hours | 88 dB: 4 h, 91 dB: 2 h, 94 dB: 1 h | 100 dB: only about 15 minutes
### Warning signs
• Ringing or buzzing in the ears afterwards, called **tinnitus**
• Sounds seeming muffled after a concert or nightclub
• Having to raise your voice to talk to someone 1 m away means it's probably over 85 dB
### Protecting your ears
Foam earplugs cut about 20–30 dB. Musicians' earplugs reduce all frequencies evenly, so music still sounds natural. EU rules require employers to provide hearing protection when workplace noise exceeds 80 dB(A). With headphones, keep the volume below about 60% and take breaks; noise-cancelling headphones help you avoid turning it up.
!? The World Health Organization estimates that over a billion young people are at risk of hearing loss from unsafe listening habits.
> Hearing loss from noise is gradual, painless and permanent, so protect your ears before it happens.

# Speed of sound
Sound travels by molecules bumping into their neighbours, so its speed depends on the medium. In stiffer materials, where particles are tightly linked, the push passes on much faster. That's why sound travels faster in water and much faster in steel than in air.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "speed of sound, m/s", "items": [{"l": "Air, 20 °C", "v": 343.0}, {"l": "Water", "v": 1500.0}, {"l": "Steel", "v": 5000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Warmer air, faster sound
In air at 0 °C, sound travels at about 331 m/s. It goes about 0.6 m/s faster for every degree warmer, reaching 343 m/s at 20 °C. High up, where the air is cold, it's slower: about 295 m/s at airliner cruising height.
= speed ≈ 331 + 0.6 × temperature (°C) m/s
### Counting thunder
Light arrives almost instantly, but sound takes about 3 seconds to travel 1 km.
ex: Flash, then thunder after 9 seconds | 9 ÷ 3 | Lightning about 3 km away
### Breaking the sound barrier
An aircraft flying at the speed of sound is at **Mach 1**. Chuck Yeager first flew faster than sound in level flight in 1947. A supersonic aircraft drags a cone of compressed air behind it that sweeps the ground as a sonic boom.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1947", "l": "First supersonic flight"}, {"y": "1976", "l": "Concorde enters service"}, {"y": "2003", "l": "Concorde retires"}]}
### No air, no sound
In space there are almost no molecules to carry vibrations, so explosions are silent. Astronauts talk by radio, which uses electromagnetic waves.
!! Helium makes your vocal cords vibrate faster => Your cords vibrate the same; sound moves faster in helium, shifting the throat's resonances and changing the tone
> Sound needs something to travel through, and the stiffer the medium, the faster it goes.

# Timbre
**Timbre**, pronounced "tam-ber", is the quality that lets you tell a violin from a flute, or a friend's voice from a stranger's, even on the same note at the same loudness. It comes mainly from the mix of **overtones**.
### The harmonic series
A vibrating string doesn't just vibrate as a whole. It also vibrates in halves, thirds, quarters and so on, all at once. These make extra frequencies at whole-number multiples of the main one, called **harmonics**.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "frequency, Hz (note A)", "items": [{"l": "Fundamental", "v": 220.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "2nd harmonic", "v": 440.0}, {"l": "3rd harmonic", "v": 660.0}, {"l": "4th harmonic", "v": 880.0}]}
ex: Fundamental 220 Hz | Harmonics at 2 ×, 3 ×, 4 × | 440, 660, 880 Hz all sounding together
### Every instrument's recipe
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Flute", "b": "few overtones; pure, airy"}, {"a": "Clarinet", "b": "mostly odd harmonics; hollow"}, {"a": "Violin", "b": "many strong overtones; rich"}, {"a": "Guitarra portuguesa", "b": "paired steel strings; shimmer"}]}
The 12 steel strings of the Portuguese guitar, played in pairs, give the bright, ringing sound so typical of fado.
### More than overtones
• The attack, how a note starts, matters a lot: a piano recording played backwards sounds more like an organ
• Vibrato and noise, such as a flute's breathiness, add character
• Your mouth shapes overtones into vowels: "ee" and "ah" on the same note differ only in timbre
In 1822 the mathematician Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating wave can be built by adding simple sine waves. Sound software uses this idea to analyse and recreate timbre.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Timbre comes from", "items": ["Mix of harmonics", "Attack and decay", "Vibrato and noise"]}
!? A tuning fork produces an almost pure tone with hardly any overtones, which is why it sounds plain.
> Two instruments playing the same note differ in their overtone recipe, and that recipe is timbre.

# Acoustics of rooms
In a room, you hear sound directly from the source and then as many reflections from walls, floor and ceiling. Reflections that arrive within about 50 milliseconds blend in and make sound fuller. Later, stronger ones are heard as a separate **echo**. The gradual fading of all the reflections is called **reverberation**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hard surfaces", "items": ["tiles, glass, concrete", "reflect: echoey"]}, "r": {"t": "Soft surfaces", "items": ["curtains, sofas, rugs", "absorb: quieter"]}}
### Reverberation time
Acousticians measure **reverberation time**: how long a sound takes to fade by 60 dB, to one millionth of its intensity. Around 1900, the American physicist Wallace Sabine found it depends on the room's volume and how much its surfaces absorb.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "T = 0.16 × V ÷ A", "items": [{"s": "T", "m": "reverberation time, s"}, {"s": "V", "m": "room volume, m³"}, {"s": "A", "m": "total absorption, m²"}]}
### Matching the room to its use
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "reverberation time, s (typical)", "items": [{"l": "Classroom", "v": 0.6}, {"l": "Concert hall", "v": 2.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Cathedral", "v": 6.0}]}
• Speech needs short reverberation, so words don't blur together
• Orchestral music sounds best with about 2 seconds, which feels rich and enveloping
• Gregorian chant was suited to long, slow cathedral echoes
### Concert halls
Many of the best halls, like Vienna's Musikverein, are long "shoebox" shapes, sending plenty of reflections from the sides. Porto's Casa da Música, opened in 2005, has a shoebox-shaped main hall with corrugated glass walls at each end, shaped to help the sound.
!? In an anechoic chamber, where foam wedges absorb almost all sound, the silence is so deep that visitors can hear their own heartbeat.
> Good acoustics means the right amount of reverberation for the job: short for speech, longer for music.

# Ultrasound
**Ultrasound** is sound above about 20,000 Hz, too high for humans to hear. Its short wavelengths can reveal small details, and it reflects off boundaries between different materials, which makes it ideal for "seeing" with echoes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Send ultrasound pulse", "Bounces off a boundary", "Echo returns", "Timing gives distance"]}
### Medical imaging
A medical scanner uses 2–18 million Hz. A probe made of **piezoelectric** crystals, which vibrate when a voltage is applied, sends pulses into the body and listens for echoes. A computer turns echo times into a picture. It uses no ionising radiation, so it's used routinely in pregnancy; Ian Donald in Glasgow pioneered this in the late 1950s.
ex: Echo returns after 0.1 ms, sound in tissue ≈ 1,540 m/s | 1,540 × 0.0001 = 0.154 m there and back | Organ boundary ≈ 7.7 cm deep
= distance = speed × time ÷ 2
### Other uses
• Car parking sensors at around 40 kHz
• Cleaning baths, where bubbles form and collapse to scrub jewellery and tools
• Sonar on ships, first developed during the First World War to detect submarines
• Breaking up kidney stones and some cataract surgery
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bats", "b": "calls up to about 200 kHz"}, {"a": "Dolphins", "b": "clicks, over 100 kHz"}, {"a": "Medical scan", "b": "2–18 MHz"}, {"a": "Parking sensor", "b": "about 40 kHz"}]}
### Echolocation
Bats emit rapid high-pitched calls and listen to the echoes, building a detailed sound map. They can catch insects in total darkness. Dolphins use clicks in the same way to find fish buried in sand.
!! Bats are blind => All bats can see; many also use echolocation to hunt at night
> Ultrasound lets machines and animals see with echoes, timing how long sound takes to bounce back.

# Infrasound
**Infrasound** is sound below about 20 Hz, too low for humans to hear as a tone, though very strong infrasound can be felt as pressure or vibration. Its long wavelengths, from about 17 metres to many kilometres, lose very little energy in air, so it can travel hundreds or thousands of kilometres.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "High frequencies", "items": ["absorbed quickly", "short range"]}, "r": {"t": "Infrasound", "items": ["little absorption", "travels far"]}}
### Animal messages
• Elephants make deep rumbles, some below 20 Hz, that other elephants can detect several kilometres away, partly through their feet
• Blue and fin whales call at about 10–40 Hz, and their calls can cross ocean basins
• Some birds may use infrasound from ocean waves to navigate
### Listening to the planet
The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty set up a global network of about 60 infrasound stations, one of them in the Azores, to detect secret nuclear explosions. The same stations hear volcanoes, meteors, storms and avalanches.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Sources of infrasound", "items": ["Volcanoes and quakes", "Meteors and storms", "Nuclear tests", "Elephants and whales"]}
### Global pressure waves
In January 2022 the Hunga Tonga undersea volcano exploded. Its pressure wave was recorded by barometers all over the world and circled the planet several times.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1883", "l": "Krakatoa wave circles Earth"}, {"y": "1996", "l": "Test-ban treaty signed"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "Hunga Tonga eruption"}]}
!? The 1883 Krakatoa eruption's pressure wave was detected by barometers worldwide as it circled Earth about seven times.
> The lowest sounds travel the furthest, letting elephants, whales and scientists listen across great distances.

# How we hear
Hearing turns tiny pressure waves into nerve signals in three stages: the outer ear collects sound, the middle ear amplifies it, and the inner ear analyses it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Outer ear funnels sound", "Eardrum vibrates", "Three bones amplify", "Cochlea makes signals"]}
### The middle ear
The eardrum passes vibrations to three tiny bones, the **malleus** (hammer), **incus** (anvil) and **stapes** (stirrup). The stapes, about 3 mm long, is the smallest bone in your body. Sound must pass from air into fluid in the inner ear, which would normally reflect most of it. The bones solve this.
ex: Eardrum area ≈ 55 mm², oval window ≈ 3.2 mm² | Area ratio ≈ 17 × | With lever action, pressure boosted about 20 ×
### The cochlea sorts frequencies
The cochlea is a fluid-filled tube about 35 mm long, coiled like a snail. Running along it is the **basilar membrane**. Near the entrance it is narrow and stiff and vibrates to high frequencies; at the far end it is wide and floppy and responds to low ones. Each spot has its own hair cells, so position tells your brain the pitch.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Cochlea entrance", "items": ["narrow, stiff", "high pitches"]}, "r": {"t": "Cochlea tip", "items": ["wide, floppy", "low pitches"]}}
### Where sound comes from
Your brain compares the two ears. A sound on your left reaches your left ear up to about 0.6 milliseconds earlier and slightly louder. The shape of your outer ear also colours sounds differently from above, below, front or back.
• Georg von Békésy won the 1961 Nobel Prize for showing how the cochlea sorts frequencies
• Cochlear implants bypass damaged hair cells by stimulating the nerve directly with electrodes
!? Your ear canal, about 2.5 cm long, resonates around 3,000 Hz, which helps make you most sensitive to frequencies in human speech.
> Your ear is a mechanical amplifier and frequency analyser, turning air vibrations into a map of pitch for the brain.

# Tiny and mostly empty
An atom is about a tenth of a billionth of a metre across (0.1 nanometres). Its nucleus is roughly 100,000 times smaller again. Yet the nucleus holds more than 99.9% of the atom's mass, so nearly all of the weight sits in a speck at the centre.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "0.1 nm", "l": "width of an atom"}, {"v": "1/100,000", "l": "nucleus vs atom width"}, {"v": "99.9%", "l": "mass in the nucleus"}]}
### How we found out
In 1909 Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working for Ernest Rutherford in Manchester, fired alpha particles at gold foil a few hundred atoms thick. Most went straight through. About 1 in 8,000 bounced back sharply. Rutherford said it was like firing a shell at tissue paper and having it come back. In 1911 he concluded that the positive charge and mass were packed into a tiny core.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Alpha particles fired", "Most pass through foil", "A few bounce back", "Tiny dense nucleus"]}
### Why things still feel solid
If atoms are mostly empty, why doesn't your hand pass through a table? The electrons of neighbouring atoms repel each other electrically, and quantum rules stop electrons crowding into the same state. What you feel as "touch" is that push, not hard balls meeting.
!? If you removed the empty space from every atom in every human on Earth, all eight billion of us would squeeze into something about the size of a sugar cube, though that cube would weigh hundreds of millions of tonnes.
!! Electrons orbit the nucleus like tiny planets => Electrons form fuzzy clouds of probability; the planet picture is a useful early model, not reality
> Matter is almost entirely empty space, held in shape by electric forces between electrons.

# Protons, neutrons, electrons
Three particles build every ordinary atom. Their discovery took 35 years: J. J. Thomson found the electron in 1897, Rutherford identified the proton around 1919, and James Chadwick found the neutron in 1932.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1897", "l": "Electron (Thomson)"}, {"y": "1911", "l": "Nucleus (Rutherford)"}, {"y": "1919", "l": "Proton identified"}, {"y": "1932", "l": "Neutron (Chadwick)"}]}
### Charge and mass
• Proton: charge +1, mass about 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
• Neutron: no charge, about 0.1% heavier than a proton
• Electron: charge −1, about 1/1,836 of a proton's mass
An atom has as many electrons as protons, so the charges cancel and it is neutral overall. Lose or gain an electron and it becomes an **ion**, which is how salt dissolves in water and how nerves send signals.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Proton", "b": "+1 charge, mass 1"}, {"a": "Neutron", "b": "0 charge, mass 1"}, {"a": "Electron", "b": "−1 charge, mass 1/1,836"}]}
### What holds the nucleus together
Protons repel each other, and squeezed this close the push is enormous. The **strong nuclear force** wins at very short range, binding protons and neutrons together. Neutrons add "glue" without adding repulsion, which is why heavy nuclei need more neutrons than protons.
!? Protons and neutrons are not fundamental: each is made of three quarks. A proton is two "up" quarks and one "down"; a neutron is one up and two down.
> An atom's identity comes from its protons, its mass from protons and neutrons, and its chemistry from its electrons.

# Elements
An element is a substance made of atoms that all have the same number of protons, called the **atomic number**. Change the proton count and you change the element. That is why medieval alchemists could never turn lead (82 protons) into gold (79): chemistry only moves electrons around, and the nucleus stays untouched.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "protons (atomic number)", "items": [{"l": "Hydrogen", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Carbon", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Oxygen", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Iron", "v": 26.0}, {"l": "Gold", "v": 79.0, "hi": true}]}
### Natural and made
About 90 elements occur naturally on Earth. The rest have been made in laboratories and reactors, often only a few atoms at a time, and many last less than a second. The four newest names, nihonium, moscovium, tennessine and oganesson, were approved in 2016, completing the table's seventh row at element 118.
### What your body is made of
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of body mass (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Oxygen", "v": 65.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Carbon", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "Hydrogen", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Nitrogen", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Everything else", "v": 4.0}]}
Just six elements (oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium, phosphorus) make up about 99% of you. Most of the oxygen and hydrogen are in water.
!! Modern alchemy is impossible => Physicists have turned other elements into gold in particle accelerators, but it costs vastly more than the gold is worth
!? Element names honour places, people and myths. Polonium is named after Poland, curium after the Curies, and helium after the Greek word for the Sun, where it was spotted in 1868 before it was found on Earth.
> The number of protons is an element's fingerprint: one more or one fewer and you have a different substance.

# Isotopes
Isotopes are versions of an element with different numbers of neutrons. They behave almost identically in chemistry, because chemistry depends on electrons, but their masses differ and some have unstable nuclei. The word was coined in 1913 by Frederick Soddy, from Greek for "same place", since isotopes share one spot in the periodic table.
### Hydrogen's three faces
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hydrogen-1", "b": "1 proton, 0 neutrons"}, {"a": "Deuterium (H-2)", "b": "1 proton, 1 neutron"}, {"a": "Tritium (H-3)", "b": "1 proton, 2 neutrons, decays"}]}
Water made with deuterium is "heavy water", about 11% denser than normal water. An ice cube of heavy water sinks in ordinary water.
### Carbon's isotopes
• Carbon-12: about 98.9% of all carbon, stable
• Carbon-13: about 1.1%, stable
• Carbon-14: about one atom per trillion, radioactive
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Same element", "items": ["same protons", "same chemistry"]}, "r": {"t": "Different isotope", "items": ["different neutrons", "different mass"]}}
### Why isotopes matter
Medicine uses radioactive isotopes to see inside the body and treat cancer. Nuclear power depends on uranium-235, only 0.7% of natural uranium, which must be concentrated or "enriched". Climate scientists read the ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in ice cores to work out temperatures hundreds of thousands of years ago.
!? Most elements have several stable isotopes. Tin has ten, more than any other element.
> Isotopes are the same element with different weights; most are stable, some decay.

# The periodic table
In 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev wrote the 63 known elements on cards and arranged them by atomic mass, grouping those that behaved alike. Where the pattern broke, he left gaps and boldly predicted that new elements would fill them.
### Predictions that came true
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1869", "l": "Mendeleev's table"}, {"y": "1875", "l": "Gallium found"}, {"y": "1879", "l": "Scandium found"}, {"y": "1886", "l": "Germanium found"}, {"y": "1913", "l": "Ordered by protons"}]}
He predicted germanium's density at about 5.5 g/cm³; the measured value turned out to be 5.35. Successes like this convinced chemists the table reflected something real. In 1913 Henry Moseley showed the true order is by atomic number, not mass, which fixed a few awkward swaps.
### Reading the table
• Rows (periods): each new row starts filling a new electron shell
• Columns (groups): elements with the same number of outer electrons
• Left side: reactive metals; right side: non-metals and noble gases
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Group 1", "b": "soft metals, react with water"}, {"a": "Group 17", "b": "halogens: chlorine, iodine"}, {"a": "Group 18", "b": "noble gases, barely react"}]}
The modern table has 18 columns and 7 rows. Sodium and potassium both fizz in water because each has a single outer electron to give away.
!? Mendeleev was not alone: Lothar Meyer in Germany built a similar table at the same time, but Mendeleev's predictions won him the credit.
!! Mendeleev invented the periodic table out of nowhere => Earlier chemists had noticed patterns; Mendeleev's bold predictions made his version stick
> The periodic table works because an element's place reveals its electron pattern, and electrons decide chemistry.

# Electron shells
Electrons can't have just any energy inside an atom. They occupy fixed energy levels, often pictured as shells around the nucleus. Niels Bohr proposed this in 1913. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second up to 8, and the third holds 8 in the simple picture used for light elements.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Outer shell: decides chemistry", "2nd shell: up to 8", "1st shell: up to 2", "Nucleus"]}
### Why atoms bond
Atoms are most stable with a full outer shell, like the noble gases neon and argon. Others get there in two main ways:
• **Ionic bonds**: one atom hands electrons to another. Sodium (2, 8, 1) gives its lone outer electron to chlorine (2, 8, 7), making table salt
• **Covalent bonds**: atoms share pairs of electrons, as in water and methane
• **Metallic bonds**: outer electrons roam freely, which is why metals conduct electricity
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sodium: 1 outer electron", "Gives it to chlorine", "Na+ and Cl− attract", "Salt crystal forms"]}
### Light from jumps
When an electron drops from a higher shell to a lower one, it gives out light of a precise colour. Each element has its own set of colours, like a barcode. Sodium street lamps glow orange for this reason, and fireworks get red from strontium and green from barium.
!? Astronomers identify elements in stars billions of kilometres away by these colour barcodes in starlight.
> Chemistry is the story of atoms trying to complete their outer shell of electrons.

# Molecules
A molecule is two or more atoms held together by shared electrons. Some are tiny, like oxygen gas (O₂). Others are huge: a single human DNA molecule in one chromosome contains billions of atoms and would stretch several centimetres if unwound.
### Shape matters
Water is bent, with an angle of about 104.5° between its two hydrogens. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons towards itself, making one end slightly negative and the other slightly positive. That lopsidedness lets water dissolve salt and sugar, gives it surface tension, and makes it boil at 100 °C rather than about −80 °C as a molecule its size otherwise would.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Water H₂O", "items": ["bent shape", "polar, sticky"]}, "r": {"t": "Carbon dioxide", "items": ["straight line", "non-polar"]}}
### Counting molecules
ex: A glass holds 250 g of water | One mole of water is 18 g | 250 ÷ 18 ≈ 14 moles | 14 × 6 × 10²³ ≈ 8 × 10²⁴ molecules
That is about eight million billion billion molecules in one glass, far more than there are glasses of water in all the oceans.
### Same atoms, different molecules
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "O₂", "b": "oxygen we breathe"}, {"a": "O₃", "b": "ozone, blocks UV high up"}, {"a": "C₂H₆O", "b": "ethanol, or dimethyl ether"}]}
How atoms are arranged can matter as much as which atoms there are. Ethanol (in wine) and dimethyl ether (a gas) have the same atoms in different layouts.
!! Molecules are always made of different elements => O₂ and N₂ are molecules of one element; together they make up about 99% of dry air
> A molecule's properties come from both its atoms and the shape they take.

# States of matter
Whether a substance is solid, liquid or gas depends on a tug-of-war between particle motion and the attractions holding particles together. Heat adds energy and motion; cooling lets attractions win.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Solid: particles vibrate", "Liquid: particles slide", "Gas: particles fly free", "Plasma: atoms ionised"]}
### Changing state
• Melting and freezing: solid ↔ liquid (water at 0 °C)
• Boiling and condensing: liquid ↔ gas (water at 100 °C at sea level)
• Sublimation: solid straight to gas, as with dry ice at about −78 °C
While ice melts, its temperature stays at 0 °C: the heat goes into breaking bonds, not warming. Pressure matters too. At the top of Serra da Estrela, about 2,000 m up, water boils at roughly 93 °C, so pasta cooks more slowly.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": -100.0, "hi": 120.0, "unit": "°C", "items": [{"l": "Dry ice", "v": -78.0}, {"l": "Ice melts", "v": 0.0}, {"l": "Boils, 2,000 m", "v": 93.0}, {"l": "Boils, sea level", "v": 100.0, "hi": true}]}
### Water is odd
Most substances shrink as they freeze. Water expands by about 9%, so ice floats. That's why lakes freeze from the top down and fish survive winter underneath, and why frozen pipes burst.
!? Plasma is the most common state of visible matter in the universe, because stars are made of it. On Earth you see it in lightning, neon signs and auroras.
> States of matter are a balance between how fast particles move and how strongly they attract.

# Seeing atoms
Light can't show atoms because its waves are thousands of times wider than an atom. In 1981 Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM's Zurich lab got round this with the **scanning tunnelling microscope** (STM). It won them the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics.
### How an STM works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ultra-sharp metal tip", "Held 1 nm above surface", "Tiny current tunnels across", "Map current, see atoms"]}
The tip ends in a single atom. Electrons "tunnel" across the gap, a quantum effect. The current changes about tenfold if the gap changes by just a tenth of a nanometre, so the tip can feel each atom's bump as it scans.
### Moving atoms
In 1989 Don Eigler at IBM used an STM to push 35 xenon atoms on a nickel surface into the letters "IBM". The work was done at about −269 °C so the atoms would stay put. In 2013 IBM made a short stop-motion film, "A Boy and His Atom", by moving carbon monoxide molecules frame by frame.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1981", "l": "STM invented"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "Nobel Prize, AFM made"}, {"y": "1989", "l": "IBM in 35 atoms"}, {"y": "2013", "l": "Film made of atoms"}]}
### Other ways to see small
• Atomic force microscopes "feel" surfaces with a tiny cantilever and work on non-conductors
• Electron microscopes use electrons, whose wavelengths are far shorter than light's
• X-ray crystallography reveals atom positions in crystals, including DNA's double helix in 1953
!! An STM takes a photograph of atoms => It builds a map of electric current point by point; the colours are added by computer
> We can't see atoms with light, but we can feel them with a tip one atom sharp.

# Where atoms come from
Almost every atom in you is billions of years old. They were made in three great forges: the Big Bang, the hearts of stars, and violent stellar deaths.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Origins of elements", "items": ["Big Bang: H, He", "Stars: up to iron", "Supernovae: heavier", "Neutron star mergers: gold"]}
### The first minutes
In the first 20 minutes after the Big Bang, about 13.8 billion years ago, the universe was hot enough to fuse protons. It ended up roughly 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, with a trace of lithium. The hydrogen in your body's water dates from then.
### Star forges
Stars fuse hydrogen into helium, then, if massive enough, helium into carbon and oxygen, and onwards up to iron. Fusion stops at iron because making heavier nuclei costs energy instead of releasing it. When massive stars explode as supernovae, they scatter these elements and make some heavier ones.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hydrogen to helium", "Helium to carbon, oxygen", "Up to iron in big stars", "Explosions spread them"]}
### Gold from collisions
Much of the gold and platinum in the universe is thought to come from colliding neutron stars. In 2017 astronomers detected such a merger through gravitational waves and saw the glow of freshly made heavy elements.
!? The Sun and Earth formed 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud already enriched by earlier generations of stars. Your atoms have been through several stars before you.
> You are made of stardust: the carbon in your cells and the iron in your blood were forged in stars.

# Unstable nuclei
A nucleus is a balance between the strong force, which binds protons and neutrons, and electric repulsion between protons. Only certain mixes are stable. Too many neutrons, too many protons, or simply too big, and the nucleus will eventually rearrange itself and release energy.
### Three ways to fix an imbalance
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Too many neutrons", "b": "beta decay: neutron to proton"}, {"a": "Too many protons", "b": "positron emission or capture"}, {"a": "Too heavy", "b": "alpha decay: sheds 2p + 2n"}]}
Each decay can change the element. Uranium-238 passes through 14 steps, including radium and radon, before ending as stable lead-206.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Uranium-238", "Radium-226", "Radon-222", "…more steps", "Lead-206 (stable)"]}
### Random but predictable
You can never say when one particular nucleus will decay. It's like popcorn: you can't tell which kernel pops next, but you can predict roughly how long the bag takes. Temperature, pressure and chemistry make essentially no difference to the rate.
### Discovery
In 1896 Henri Becquerel left uranium salts on a wrapped photographic plate in a drawer and found the plate fogged, with no sunlight involved. The rays came from the uranium itself.
!? Every element heavier than lead is radioactive. Two lighter ones, technetium (43) and promethium (61), have no stable isotopes at all.
> A radioactive nucleus is one with the wrong mix of particles, and decay is its way to a more stable state.

# Three types of radiation
The three classic types were named alpha, beta and gamma, the first letters of the Greek alphabet, by Rutherford (1899) and Paul Villard (1900), before anyone knew what they were. They differ in what they are, how much damage they do and how far they get.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Alpha: stopped by paper or skin", "Beta: stopped by mm of aluminium", "Gamma: needs lead or concrete"]}
### What each one is
• **Alpha**: a helium nucleus, heavy and doubly charged. Travels only a few cm in air
• **Beta**: a fast electron (or positron). Travels about a metre in air
• **Gamma**: high-energy light, no mass or charge. Weakened by matter but never fully stopped
### Outside vs inside
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Alpha outside body", "items": ["dead skin blocks it", "mostly harmless"]}, "r": {"t": "Alpha inside body", "items": ["hits living cells", "very damaging"]}}
Alpha particles dump all their energy in a short distance, so swallowed or inhaled alpha emitters are especially harmful. That is why radon, an alpha emitter breathed into the lungs, is a health concern. Gamma rays pass through you, so they are dangerous from outside but are also useful for imaging and therapy.
### Ionising radiation
All three are "ionising": they knock electrons off atoms, which can break chemical bonds, including in DNA. Neutrons, released in reactors, are a fourth important type that needs water or concrete to stop.
!! Radiation makes things radioactive => Alpha, beta and gamma do not make objects radioactive; contamination means radioactive material itself has landed somewhere
> The more easily a radiation type is stopped, the more damage it does over a short distance.

# Half-life
A half-life is the time it takes for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. It's a statistical property: each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in any given time, regardless of its age. So the amount left keeps halving, never quite reaching zero.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% remaining", "items": [{"l": "Start", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "1 half-life", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "2 half-lives", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "3 half-lives", "v": 12.5}, {"l": "4 half-lives", "v": 6.25, "t": "6"}]}
### Working it out
ex: Iodine-131 half-life is 8 days | After 8 days: 1/2 left | After 24 days: 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8 | After 80 days (10 half-lives): 1/1,024, under 0.1%
Iodine-131 is used to treat thyroid conditions, and was released at Chernobyl. Its short half-life means it was a serious risk for weeks but faded within months.
= amount left = start × (1/2)^(time ÷ half-life)
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "N = N₀ × (½)^(t/T)", "items": [{"s": "N", "m": "amount left"}, {"s": "N₀", "m": "starting amount"}, {"s": "t", "m": "time passed"}, {"s": "T", "m": "half-life"}]}
### Short vs long
A short half-life means intense radiation that disappears quickly. A long half-life means weaker radiation lasting a long time. A rough rule: after 10 half-lives, a source has dropped to about a thousandth.
!! After two half-lives everything has decayed => After two half-lives a quarter remains; the amount halves again each time
> Radioactivity fades by halving: the same fraction disappears in each half-life, however much is left.

# Half-lives vary hugely
Half-lives range across more than 40 powers of ten, from a tiny fraction of a second to far longer than the age of the universe. The value depends on how unstable the nucleus is and which decay route it takes.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Radon-222", "b": "3.8 days"}, {"a": "Caesium-137", "b": "30 years"}, {"a": "Plutonium-239", "b": "24,000 years"}, {"a": "Uranium-238", "b": "4.5 billion years"}]}
### Why the range matters
• **Medicine** wants short half-lives: technetium-99m (6 hours) lights up scans then fades within a day
• **Dating** needs half-lives matched to the age being measured: carbon-14 for thousands of years, uranium for billions
• **Waste** problems come from middle and long half-lives: caesium-137 and strontium-90 (about 30 years) dominate the first centuries
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Polonium-214: 0.00016 seconds", "Iodine-131: 8 days", "Carbon-14: 5,730 years", "Thorium-232: 14 billion years"]}
### Earth's inner heat
Long-lived uranium-238, thorium-232 and potassium-40 have been decaying inside Earth since it formed. Their heat helps keep the mantle churning, which drives plate tectonics and volcanoes, including the Azores.
!? Tellurium-128 has the longest half-life ever measured, about 2 × 10²⁴ years, over 100 trillion times the age of the universe.
> Short half-lives burn bright and fade fast; long ones whisper for ages.

# Carbon dating
Cosmic rays hit nitrogen atoms high in the atmosphere and turn some into carbon-14. This joins carbon dioxide, plants absorb it, and animals eat the plants. So every living thing carries the same tiny proportion of carbon-14 as the air, about one atom per trillion carbon atoms.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cosmic rays make C-14", "Plants absorb CO₂", "Animals eat plants", "Death: intake stops"]}
### The clock starts at death
Once an organism dies, it stops taking in carbon. The carbon-14 decays with a half-life of 5,730 years, while carbon-12 stays put. Measuring the ratio tells you how long ago it died.
ex: Bone has 25% of living C-14 level | 25% = 1/2 × 1/2 | Two half-lives | 2 × 5,730 ≈ 11,500 years old
### Limits and corrections
• After about 50,000 years (nearly 9 half-lives) too little is left to measure
• Only works on once-living material: wood, bone, charcoal, cloth, shell
• Atmospheric C-14 has varied, so dates are calibrated with tree rings and other records
• Nuclear tests in the 1950s–60s almost doubled atmospheric C-14, the "bomb pulse"
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1949", "l": "Libby's first dates"}, {"y": "1960", "l": "Nobel Prize to Libby"}, {"y": "1977", "l": "AMS dating begins"}, {"y": "1988", "l": "Turin Shroud dated"}]}
Modern accelerator mass spectrometry counts carbon-14 atoms directly and needs only milligrams of sample.
!! Carbon dating can date dinosaurs => Dinosaurs died 66 million years ago; their age comes from dating volcanic rock with longer-lived isotopes
> Living things share the air's carbon-14; after death, its steady decay becomes a clock.

# Background radiation
Radiation is everywhere, and always has been. Worldwide, a person receives about 2.4 mSv a year from natural sources on average, though this varies widely with geology and altitude.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mSv per year (world average)", "items": [{"l": "Radon", "v": 1.26, "t": "1.3", "hi": true}, {"l": "Ground and rocks", "v": 0.48, "t": "0.5"}, {"l": "Cosmic rays", "v": 0.39, "t": "0.4"}, {"l": "Food and body", "v": 0.29, "t": "0.3"}]}
### Where it comes from
• **Radon**: a gas from uranium in rocks and soil, about half the natural dose
• **Ground**: uranium, thorium and potassium in rocks and building materials
• **Cosmic rays**: higher at altitude and on flights
• **Your own body**: potassium-40 and carbon-14 in your tissues
### Your radioactive body
An adult body contains about 140 g of potassium, of which a tiny fraction is radioactive potassium-40. Together with carbon-14, around 8,000 atoms decay inside you every second.
### Portugal's granite
Much of northern and central Portugal sits on granite, which contains more uranium than most rocks. Districts such as Guarda, Viseu, Vila Real and Braga have some of the highest indoor radon levels in Europe, so the natural dose there can be several times the world average.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2.4 mSv", "l": "world natural average/yr"}, {"v": "~50%", "l": "of it from radon"}, {"v": "8,000", "l": "decays/s in your body"}]}
!? Places such as Ramsar in Iran and Kerala in India have natural doses many times the world average, and people have lived there for generations.
> Natural radiation is a constant background to life; radon from the ground is the biggest part of it.

# Radon at home
Radon-222 forms when radium in rocks decays. It is a colourless, odourless gas that seeps out of soil and through cracks, pipes and gaps in floors. Outdoors it dilutes harmlessly; indoors it can build up, especially in cellars and ground floors in winter when windows stay shut.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Uranium in granite", "Decays to radium", "Radium gives radon gas", "Seeps into houses", "Breathed into lungs"]}
### Why it matters
Radon itself is breathed in and out, but its decay products are solid alpha emitters that lodge in the lungs. The World Health Organization calls radon the second biggest cause of lung cancer after smoking, and the risk multiplies for smokers.
### Limits and testing
Under EU rules, Portugal set a reference level of 300 becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m³) for homes and workplaces in 2018. The WHO suggests aiming for 100 where possible. Levels swing with weather and season, so a reliable test uses a passive detector left for at least three months, ideally over winter. Detectors are cheap and posted to a lab for analysis.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 1000.0, "unit": "Bq/m³ indoors", "items": [{"l": "Outdoor air", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "WHO target", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "EU/PT limit", "v": 300.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "High N. PT homes", "v": 800.0}]}
### Reducing it
• Ventilate ground floors and cellars; open windows on opposite sides
• Seal cracks in floors and gaps around pipes
• Fit a sump with a small fan under the floor to draw radon out (often cuts levels by 80–90%)
• In new builds, lay a radon-proof membrane
!! Radon is only a problem in old stone houses => Any building on uranium-bearing ground can collect radon, including new concrete ones
> Radon is invisible but easy to measure and usually cheap to reduce, so testing is worth it in granite areas.

# Measuring dose
Two different units matter. The **becquerel** (Bq) counts how many nuclei decay per second in a source. The **sievert** (Sv) estimates the biological effect on a person, weighting for the type of radiation and the organs hit. Doses to the public are usually in millisieverts (mSv), thousandths of a sievert.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.0, "hi": 10000.0, "log": true, "unit": "µSv", "items": [{"l": "Chest X-ray", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Lisbon–NY flight", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Natural yearly", "v": 2400.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "CT abdomen", "v": 8000.0}]}
### Putting doses in context
• Dental X-ray: about 0.005 mSv
• Eating a banana: about 0.0001 mSv
• Public limit from artificial sources (excluding medicine): 1 mSv a year
• Radiation workers in the EU: up to 20 mSv a year
• 1,000 mSv (1 Sv) at once: radiation sickness likely
ex: CT scan 8 mSv | Natural background 2.4 mSv a year | 8 ÷ 2.4 ≈ 3.3 | About three years of background in one scan
### Dose vs risk
Large doses damage cells fast and cause burns and sickness. Small doses are thought to add a small extra cancer risk roughly in proportion to dose. Medical scans are used when the diagnostic benefit clearly outweighs that small risk.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Becquerel (Bq)", "b": "decays per second"}, {"a": "Gray (Gy)", "b": "energy absorbed per kg"}, {"a": "Sievert (Sv)", "b": "biological effect"}]}
!? The unit is named after Rolf Sievert, a Swedish physicist who pioneered radiation protection in the early 20th century.
> Becquerels describe the source; sieverts describe what it does to you.

# Uses of radiation
The same properties that make radiation dangerous make it useful: it penetrates matter, can be detected in tiny amounts and can kill cells.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Uses of radiation", "items": ["Medical imaging", "Cancer therapy", "Sterilising", "Power in space"]}
### Medicine
• **Diagnosis**: technetium-99m is used in tens of millions of scans a year; PET scans use fluorine-18, half-life about 110 minutes
• **Radiotherapy**: about half of all cancer patients receive it, aiming beams to destroy tumour cells
• **Sterilising**: gamma rays from cobalt-60 sterilise syringes, gloves and implants sealed in their packaging
### Industry and home
• Gamma radiography checks welds in pipelines and aircraft parts
• Smoke alarms use a speck of americium-241: its alpha particles let a tiny current flow, and smoke interrupts it
• Food irradiation kills bacteria and delays sprouting without making food radioactive
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Americium emits alpha", "Air ionised, current flows", "Smoke blocks ions", "Current drops: alarm"]}
### Power far from the Sun
Radioisotope generators use heat from plutonium-238 decay to make electricity. They have powered the Voyager probes since 1977, now beyond the Solar System, and the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers on Mars.
!! Irradiated food is radioactive => Food irradiation uses gamma rays or electrons that pass through; the food does not become radioactive
> Radiation is a tool: controlled and shielded, it diagnoses, heals, sterilises and powers.

# Marie Curie
Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867, she moved to Paris in 1891 because women could not attend university in Russian-ruled Poland. She studied physics and maths at the Sorbonne, often living on very little, and married the physicist Pierre Curie in 1895.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1898", "l": "Polonium, radium found"}, {"y": "1903", "l": "Nobel in Physics"}, {"y": "1911", "l": "Nobel in Chemistry"}, {"y": "1934", "l": "Dies of radiation"}]}
### The discoveries
Following Becquerel's uranium rays, she found the pitchblende ore was more active than its uranium could explain. She and Pierre processed tonnes of it in a leaky shed to isolate two new elements: polonium, named after her homeland, and radium, which glows in the dark. She realised radioactivity was a property of atoms themselves, a key step toward nuclear physics.
### Firsts
• First woman to win a Nobel Prize
• First person to win two, and still the only one in two different sciences
• First woman professor at the Sorbonne, in 1906, after Pierre's death in a street accident
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1903 Physics", "b": "shared with Pierre, Becquerel"}, {"a": "1911 Chemistry", "b": "alone: polonium and radium"}]}
### War and legacy
In the First World War she equipped about 20 mobile X-ray vans, the "petites Curies", and trained women to operate them, helping treat over a million soldiers. Her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie won the 1935 Nobel in Chemistry. Long exposure to radiation, unknown then as a danger, led to her death from aplastic anaemia in 1934.
!? Her laboratory notebooks are still radioactive more than a century later and are kept in lead-lined boxes in Paris; readers must sign a waiver.
> Marie Curie showed that radioactivity comes from inside the atom, and opened the nuclear age.

# E = mc²
Einstein published the idea in 1905, as a short follow-up to his paper on special relativity. It says mass is a very concentrated form of energy. The conversion factor is the speed of light squared, and since light travels about 300,000 km per second, that factor is enormous.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "E = m × c²", "items": [{"s": "E", "m": "energy, joules"}, {"s": "m", "m": "mass, kg"}, {"s": "c", "m": "speed of light, 3 × 10⁸ m/s"}]}
### How much energy?
ex: m = 1 gram = 0.001 kg | c² = 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s² | E = 0.001 × 9 × 10¹⁶ = 9 × 10¹³ J | About 25 million kWh, roughly the Hiroshima bomb
Converting a single kilogram fully would give about 25 billion kWh, around half of Portugal's yearly electricity use. In practice, no everyday process converts more than a small fraction of mass.
### What fraction gets converted?
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of mass turned into energy", "items": [{"l": "Burning fuel", "v": 1e-07, "t": "0.00000001"}, {"l": "Fission", "v": 0.09, "t": "about 0.1"}, {"l": "Fusion (D-T)", "v": 0.38, "t": "about 0.4", "hi": true}, {"l": "Matter + antimatter", "v": 100.0}]}
That is why nuclear reactions release millions of times more energy per kilogram than chemical ones such as burning coal or petrol.
!! E = mc² was what made the atomic bomb possible => The bomb depended on discovering fission in 1938; E = mc² just accounts for where the energy comes from
!? The first direct test came in 1932, when John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton split lithium nuclei in Cambridge and found the fragments' energy matched the missing mass.
> A little mass is a huge amount of energy, because c² is a gigantic number.

# Fission
Fission was discovered in Berlin in December 1938 by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, who found barium, a much lighter element, after bombarding uranium with neutrons. Lise Meitner, who had fled Nazi Germany, and her nephew Otto Frisch explained it weeks later: the nucleus had split in two, and they named the process after cell division.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Neutron hits U-235", "Nucleus wobbles, splits", "Two fragments + energy", "2–3 new neutrons"]}
### The chain reaction
Each split releases about 200 million electronvolts, roughly 50 million times the energy from burning one carbon atom. If at least one of the new neutrons splits another nucleus, the reaction keeps itself going.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Reactor", "items": ["1 neutron goes on", "steady power", "3–5% U-235"]}, "r": {"t": "Bomb", "items": ["many neutrons go on", "runaway in µs", "about 90% U-235"]}}
### Why uranium-235?
Natural uranium is 99.3% uranium-238, which rarely splits. Only 0.7% is uranium-235, which splits easily, especially with slow neutrons. Reactors use a **moderator**, usually water, to slow neutrons down. Plutonium-239, made inside reactors from uranium-238, also splits well.
### First chain reaction
On 2 December 1942, Enrico Fermi's team started the first controlled chain reaction in Chicago Pile-1, a stack of graphite and uranium under a sports stand. It produced about half a watt.
!? Nature beat us to it. About 2 billion years ago, uranium deposits at Oklo in Gabon ran as natural fission reactors, on and off, for hundreds of thousands of years.
> Fission splits heavy nuclei; the spare neutrons can split more, making a self-sustaining chain.

# Inside a reactor
Most of the world's roughly 400 power reactors are **pressurised water reactors**. Their core holds tens of thousands of fuel rods filled with uranium oxide pellets, each the size of a fingertip. Water around them slows neutrons and carries heat away.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fission heats fuel", "Hot water, under pressure", "Makes steam in 2nd loop", "Steam spins turbine", "Generator makes power"]}
### Keeping it steady
• **Control rods** of boron or cadmium soak up neutrons; pushing them in slows the reaction
• If cooling water heats up and expands, it slows fewer neutrons, so power falls by itself
• Emergency systems drop all rods in about two seconds, a "scram"
### Heat after shutdown
Even after the chain reaction stops, the radioactive fission products keep releasing heat, about 6–7% of full power at first, falling over days. That is why cooling must never fail, as Fukushima showed.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Turbine and generator", "Steam generator", "Reactor vessel and core", "Thick concrete containment"]}
### Efficiency
A typical reactor turns about a third of its heat into electricity; the rest leaves through cooling towers or into a river or sea. A 1,000 MW electric plant runs at around 3,000 MW of heat.
!! The clouds above nuclear cooling towers are pollution => They are water vapour, the same as from any steam power station
!? Spain's Almaraz plant, on the Tagus about 100 km from the Portuguese border, has supplied Iberian electricity since the early 1980s.
> A reactor is a kettle with a nuclear heat source: fission boils water, steam turns a turbine.

# Energy density
Nuclear fuel packs extraordinary energy into a small mass, because nuclear forces are millions of times stronger than the chemical bonds broken in burning.
ex: 1 kg of U-235 fully split ≈ 8 × 10¹³ J | 1 kg of coal ≈ 3 × 10⁷ J | Ratio ≈ 2.7 million | 1 kg U-235 ≈ 2,700 tonnes of coal
Real reactor fuel is only 3–5% uranium-235 and is not fully used, so a kilogram of fuel replaces roughly 100–150 tonnes of coal. Still, a 1,000 MW plant needs only about 25 tonnes of fresh fuel a year, a few lorry loads, where a coal plant burns millions of tonnes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "1 GW coal plant", "items": ["~3 million t coal/yr", "~6 million t CO₂"]}, "r": {"t": "1 GW nuclear plant", "items": ["~25 t fuel/yr", "almost no CO₂"]}}
### Climate and land
Over their whole life cycle, including mining and construction, nuclear plants emit about 12 g of CO₂ per kWh, similar to wind and far below gas or coal.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "g CO₂ per kWh, life cycle (IPCC)", "items": [{"l": "Coal", "v": 820.0}, {"l": "Gas", "v": 490.0}, {"l": "Solar (large)", "v": 48.0}, {"l": "Wind", "v": 11.0}, {"l": "Nuclear", "v": 12.0, "hi": true}]}
### The catch
High energy density doesn't automatically mean cheap. Recent European projects, such as Flamanville 3 in France and Olkiluoto 3 in Finland, ran over a decade late and cost several times their budgets.
!? A person's entire lifetime of electricity from nuclear power would produce spent fuel about the size of a drinks can.
> Nuclear fuel is a million times denser in energy than fossil fuel, which is both its strength and why its waste is so compact.

# Nuclear waste
Spent fuel is about 95% uranium, 1% plutonium and 4% fission products. Those fission products make it intensely radioactive and hot when it leaves the reactor, so it must be cooled and shielded.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Spent fuel leaves core", "5–10 years in pools", "Dry steel-concrete casks", "Deep geological burial"]}
### How long?
Radioactivity falls fast at first: after 40 years it is about a thousandth of the level at removal. But long-lived elements such as plutonium mean it takes very roughly 100,000 years to drop to the level of the uranium ore it came from. Reprocessing, as France does, recovers uranium and plutonium and cuts the volume of high-level waste.
### Onkalo
Finland is building the world's first permanent repository at Olkiluoto. Fuel goes into copper canisters, packed in bentonite clay, placed about 430 m deep in 1.9-billion-year-old granite. The idea is several barriers, so no single failure matters. A full trial run began in 2024; Sweden began building a similar site at Forsmark in 2025.

### Volume

> Nuclear waste is small in volume but long-lived, so the plan is to isolate it deep in stable rock.

# Accidents
The International Nuclear Event Scale runs from 1 to 7. Only two accidents have reached level 7.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1979", "l": "Three Mile Island, US"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "Chernobyl, Ukraine"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Fukushima, Japan"}]}
### Chernobyl, 26 April 1986
During a badly run safety test at night, reactor 4 surged out of control. The Soviet RBMK design had a dangerous flaw: as water boiled, power rose instead of fell. A steam explosion blew off the reactor's massive steel lid and the graphite burned for days, with no strong containment building. About 30 workers and firefighters died within months. Thousands of thyroid cancers followed in children who drank contaminated milk, most treated successfully. Estimates of long-term deaths are disputed. A 30 km exclusion zone remains.
### Fukushima, 11 March 2011
A magnitude-9 earthquake shut the reactors down safely, but a tsunami about 14 m high flooded the backup generators. Without cooling, three cores melted and hydrogen explosions wrecked buildings. Around 150,000 people were evacuated. The tsunami itself killed about 18,000 people; one later worker death has been attributed to radiation, while the stress of evacuation contributed to many deaths among the elderly.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Chernobyl", "items": ["flawed design", "no containment", "test gone wrong"]}, "r": {"t": "Fukushima", "items": ["natural disaster", "cooling lost", "sea wall too low"]}}
### Lessons
After 2011, EU reactors went through stress tests, adding backup power and flood defences. Germany chose to phase out nuclear power, closing its last plants in 2023.
!? Wildlife has flourished in the Chernobyl exclusion zone without people; wolves, lynx and Przewalski's horses roam there.
> The worst nuclear accidents came from design flaws and losing cooling, and each one rewrote safety rules worldwide.

# Portugal and nuclear
Portugal is one of several EU countries never to have generated nuclear power. Yet nuclear history runs through its geology, its science and its politics.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1913", "l": "Radium mining begins"}, {"y": "1961", "l": "Research reactor opens"}, {"y": "1976", "l": "Ferrel plan dropped"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Uranium mining ends"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Reactor shut down"}]}
### Ferrel
In the 1970s the government chose Ferrel, near Peniche, for a nuclear plant. In March 1976 local farmers and fishermen occupied the site and protested, and the plan was shelved. Later energy plans mentioned nuclear but never went ahead, and Portugal chose hydro, wind and later solar instead.
### Uranium country
The granite of the Beiras is rich in uranium. Mining began for radium early in the 20th century at Urgeiriça, near Nelas, and later supplied uranium. Around 60 old mines remain, and many have been cleaned up by the state company EDM to deal with tailings and radon.
### Science
Portugal ran a small research reactor at Sacavém/Bobadela, near Lisbon, from 1961 to 2016 for materials research and isotopes. Portuguese physicists also take part in CERN and in European fusion research.

!! Portugal uses no nuclear electricity => Portugal trades power with Spain, which gets about a fifth of its electricity from nuclear
> Portugal never built a nuclear plant, but it mined uranium for decades and remains part of a nuclear-powered Iberian grid.

# Fusion
Fusion is the opposite of fission: light nuclei join to make a heavier one. The product weighs slightly less than the parts, and the missing mass becomes energy. Per kilogram, the deuterium–tritium reaction releases about four times as much energy as fission.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Deuterium + tritium", "Overcome repulsion", "Helium-4 + neutron", "17.6 MeV released"]}
### Why so hot?
Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other strongly. To make them touch, they must hit at huge speed. The Sun manages at 15 million °C thanks to crushing pressure, gravity and quantum tunnelling. On Earth we can't match that pressure, so we use temperatures of 100–150 million °C, about ten times the Sun's core.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "million °C", "items": [{"l": "Sun's surface", "v": 0.0055, "t": "0.006"}, {"l": "Sun's core", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "Fusion reactor", "v": 150.0, "hi": true}]}
### Fuel
• Deuterium is in all water: about 1 hydrogen atom in 6,400 in seawater
• Tritium is rare and radioactive, so reactors must breed it from lithium
• The waste is helium, and fusion produces no long-lived high-level waste, though the reactor walls become radioactive
### The hard part
Fusion is not a chain reaction. If anything goes wrong, the plasma cools and stops in seconds. The challenge is the opposite of fission: keeping it going at all.
!? The deuterium in about 45 litres of water, fused with tritium bred from the lithium in one laptop battery, could supply about 200,000 kWh: roughly 30 years of one EU citizen's electricity.
> Fusion releases energy by joining light nuclei, but only at temperatures hotter than the Sun's core.

# Fusion reactors
There are two main approaches. **Magnetic confinement** holds a hot plasma for a long time in a magnetic cage. **Inertial confinement** crushes a tiny fuel pellet with lasers for a few billionths of a second.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tokamak (ITER)", "items": ["doughnut-shaped", "magnets hold plasma", "runs for minutes"]}, "r": {"t": "Lasers (NIF)", "items": ["peppercorn pellet", "192 laser beams", "lasts nanoseconds"]}}
### ITER
ITER, in Cadarache in southern France, is a collaboration of the EU, China, India, Japan, Korea, Russia and the US. Its goal is to produce 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of heating, a gain of ten. It has faced long delays: the current plan targets first research operations in 2034 and full deuterium–tritium fuel in 2039. It will not make electricity; it is an experiment.
### NIF's milestone
On 5 December 2022, the US National Ignition Facility fired 2.05 MJ of laser light at a target and got 3.15 MJ of fusion energy out. Later shots did better. But the lasers drew around 300 MJ from the grid, so the whole facility is far from a net gain.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "megajoules, NIF Dec 2022", "items": [{"l": "Grid to lasers", "v": 300.0, "t": "~300"}, {"l": "Laser to target", "v": 2.05}, {"l": "Fusion out", "v": 3.15, "hi": true}]}
### Portugal's part
Lisbon's Instituto Superior Técnico runs a fusion research institute, operated a small tokamak (ISTTOK) and contributes to ITER and the European EUROfusion programme. Private companies worldwide are also racing to build compact machines.
!! Fusion is always 30 years away and never progresses => Fusion performance has improved by many orders of magnitude since the 1970s; the step to cheap, practical power is still unproven
> Fusion has proved it can release more energy than goes into the fuel; turning that into practical power is the next challenge.

# Small modular reactors
Small modular reactors (SMRs) produce up to about 300 MW of electricity, compared with 1,000–1,600 MW for large plants. "Modular" means building standard parts in a factory and assembling them on site, like aircraft, instead of building each plant as a unique giant project.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Large reactor", "items": ["1,000+ MW", "built on site", "10+ years"]}, "r": {"t": "SMR", "items": ["up to ~300 MW", "factory-built parts", "faster, in theory"]}}
### The promise
• Lower up-front cost, so smaller utilities and countries can afford one
• Passive safety: many designs cool themselves by natural circulation, without pumps or power
• Can replace old coal plants and reuse their grid connections
• Can supply heat for industry or hydrogen as well as electricity
### Where things stand
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Russia, 2020", "b": "floating plant, 2 × 35 MW"}, {"a": "China, 2023", "b": "gas-cooled HTR-PM running"}, {"a": "Canada", "b": "BWRX-300 under construction"}, {"a": "UK", "b": "Rolls-Royce SMR chosen"}]}
### The doubts
Cheaper per plant is not the same as cheaper per kWh: large reactors benefit from scale. A flagship US project by NuScale was cancelled in 2023 when costs rose. Mass production only pays off once many units are ordered, and the first few are always the most expensive.
!? Nuclear-powered submarines and aircraft carriers have run on small reactors since the USS Nautilus in 1954, so the idea of compact reactors is not new.

== ph-relativity

# Light speed is the same for everyone
Throw a ball forward from a moving train and its speed adds to the train's. Common sense says light should behave the same way. It doesn't. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley tried to detect Earth's motion through a supposed "ether" by timing light in different directions, and found no difference at all.
### Einstein's two rules (1905)
• The laws of physics are the same for everyone moving at a steady speed
• Light in a vacuum always travels at the same speed, about 300,000 km/s, whoever measures it
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ball from train", "items": ["train 100 km/h", "ball 50 km/h", "seen: 150 km/h"]}, "r": {"t": "Light from train", "items": ["train 100 km/h", "light: c", "seen: still c"]}}
### Something has to give
Speed is distance divided by time. If light's speed can't change, then distance and time must differ between observers. Moving rulers shrink along their direction of motion and moving clocks run slow, by exactly the amounts needed to keep light's speed fixed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Light speed fixed", "Speed = distance ÷ time", "So time must stretch", "And lengths must shrink"]}
### A fixed number
Since 1983 the speed of light has been defined as exactly 299,792,458 m/s, and the metre is defined from it: the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second.
!? Einstein was 26 and working as a patent clerk in Bern when he published special relativity, along with three other landmark papers in 1905, his "miracle year".
> Light's speed is the one thing everyone agrees on; space and time adjust to keep it that way.

# Time dilation
The slowing of moving clocks is set by the **Lorentz factor**, γ (gamma). It is almost exactly 1 at everyday speeds and climbs steeply close to light speed.
= γ = 1 ÷ √(1 − v²/c²)
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "time slowdown factor γ", "items": [{"l": "Jet, 900 km/h", "v": 1.0, "t": "1.0000000000003"}, {"l": "50% of c", "v": 1.15}, {"l": "87% of c", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "99% of c", "v": 7.1, "hi": true}]}
ex: v = 0.87c | v²/c² = 0.757 | √(1 − 0.757) = 0.49 | γ ≈ 2: one hour on board = two hours outside
### Proof from the sky
Cosmic rays striking the upper atmosphere, about 15 km up, create particles called **muons**. Muons live only about 2 millionths of a second, long enough to travel about 600 m. Yet many reach the ground. Travelling at over 99% of light speed, their clocks run slow enough to make the trip.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Muons made 15 km up", "Should decay in ~600 m", "Moving clock runs slow", "Many reach the ground"]}
### Nobody feels it
Each observer sees their own clock ticking normally. Only when comparing with someone moving relative to them do they see the difference.
!? Astronauts who spend a year on the International Space Station come back a few thousandths of a second younger than if they had stayed on Earth.
> Time is not universal: the faster you move relative to someone, the slower your clock runs in their eyes.

# The twin paradox
Imagine twins, Ana and Rita. Ana boards a ship to a star 10 light-years away, travelling at 87% of the speed of light, then comes straight back. Rita stays in Viana do Castelo.
ex: Distance there and back: 20 light-years | Rita's time: 20 ÷ 0.87 ≈ 23 years | Ana's clock runs at half rate | Ana ages about 11.5 years
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rita (on Earth)", "items": ["waits ~23 years", "no acceleration"]}, "r": {"t": "Ana (traveller)", "items": ["ages ~11.5 years", "turns round, accelerates"]}}
### Why it's not really a paradox
From Ana's ship, Earth moves away, so shouldn't Rita's clock run slow? The answer is that the situation isn't symmetrical. Ana has to accelerate, turn round and decelerate, switching between moving frames. Rita stays in one. When the full calculation includes the turnaround, both agree Ana is younger.
### Tested for real
In 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew caesium atomic clocks round the world on airliners, eastward and westward, and compared them with clocks left in Washington.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "nanoseconds gained vs ground clock", "items": [{"l": "Westward flight", "v": 273.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Eastward flight", "v": 0.0, "t": "lost 59"}]}
The results matched relativity's predictions, combining speed effects and gravity effects, within the experimental error. Particle accelerators confirm the effect every day with unstable particles that live many times longer when moving fast.
!! The traveller just feels younger => Her body really has aged less: fewer heartbeats, fewer cell divisions, less time
> Travel fast enough and you return to a future where more time has passed for everyone else.

# Nothing faster than light
Pushing an object closer to light speed takes ever more energy for ever smaller gains in speed. Its energy grows by the Lorentz factor, which shoots towards infinity as speed approaches c.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy ÷ rest energy", "items": [{"l": "90% of c", "v": 2.3}, {"l": "99% of c", "v": 7.1}, {"l": "99.9% of c", "v": 22.4, "t": "22", "hi": true}]}
### Fastest things we've made
• Protons in CERN's Large Hadron Collider reach 99.999999% of light speed, yet never c
• The Parker Solar Probe, the fastest spacecraft, reached about 190 km/s in 2024, under 0.1% of c
• Voyager 1, launched 1977, takes about 23 hours for its radio signals to reach us
### Why it matters
If information could travel faster than light, some observers would see an effect before its cause. Relativity's speed limit protects cause and effect. Light itself goes at c only because photons have no mass.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "With mass", "b": "always slower than light"}, {"a": "Massless (photons)", "b": "always exactly at c"}, {"a": "Space expanding", "b": "not motion, no limit"}]}
### Faster than light in water
Light slows to about 75% of c in water. Particles from a reactor core can outrun it there, producing an eerie blue glow called **Cherenkov radiation**, an optical version of a sonic boom.
!! Nothing at all can move faster than light => Nothing with mass can reach c in a vacuum, but light can be beaten in water, and distant galaxies recede faster than c because space itself expands
> Reaching light speed would need infinite energy, so it is a limit, not a target.

# General relativity
In 1907 Einstein had what he called his "happiest thought": a person falling freely doesn't feel their own weight. Gravity and acceleration feel the same. Eight years of hard maths later, in November 1915, he published general relativity.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Newton (1687)", "items": ["gravity is a force", "acts instantly"]}, "r": {"t": "Einstein (1915)", "items": ["gravity is curvature", "travels at light speed"]}}
### The idea
Mass and energy curve space-time, the four-dimensional fabric of space and time together. Objects move along the straightest possible paths through this curved fabric. As the physicist John Wheeler summed it up: matter tells space-time how to curve; space-time tells matter how to move.
### Its first triumph
For decades Mercury's orbit had puzzled astronomers. Its closest point to the Sun shifts by an extra 43 arcseconds per century that Newton's theory couldn't explain. Einstein's equations gave exactly 43. He said the result gave him heart palpitations.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1915", "l": "Theory and Mercury"}, {"y": "1919", "l": "Starlight bending"}, {"y": "1959", "l": "Gravity slows time"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "Gravitational waves"}]}
### Gravitational waves
Accelerating masses send ripples through space-time. On 14 September 2015, the LIGO detectors in the US caught waves from two black holes merging 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal stretched their 4 km arms by less than a thousandth of a proton's width. The discovery won the 2017 Nobel Prize.
!! The trampoline picture shows how gravity works => It's a helpful analogy for space curving, but it uses gravity to explain gravity, and real curvature also involves time
> Gravity isn't a pull across space; it's the shape of space-time guiding how things move.

# Gravity slows time
General relativity predicts that clocks deeper in a gravitational field tick more slowly. Light climbing out of gravity loses energy and shifts towards red, which is the same effect seen another way.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Mountain top: clock faster", "Sea level: slightly slower", "Earth's core: slower still"]}
### Measuring it
In 1959 Robert Pound and Glen Rebka at Harvard sent gamma rays 22.5 m up a tower and measured a tiny change in their frequency, matching the prediction. In 2010 optical atomic clocks at the US standards lab detected the difference from raising one clock just 33 cm.
ex: Height difference: 1,000 m | Rate change ≈ g × h ÷ c² ≈ 1 part in 10¹³ | About 9 nanoseconds a day | Over 80 years: about 0.3 milliseconds
### Practical uses
Clocks are now so precise that they act as altimeters: comparing two clocks can reveal a centimetre of height difference. Scientists plan to use networks of clocks to measure Earth's shape and sea level and to detect underground mass, such as magma.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1959", "l": "Tower test, 22.5 m"}, {"y": "1976", "l": "Rocket clock test"}, {"y": "2010", "l": "Clock raised 33 cm"}]}
!? Because of gravity, Earth's core is estimated to be about two and a half years younger than its surface, after 4.5 billion years.
!! Time slows because gravity affects the clock's mechanism => All processes slow equally, from atoms to heartbeats; it's time itself
> The stronger the gravity, the slower time flows, and modern clocks can measure it over the height of a table.

# GPS needs relativity
GPS, and Europe's Galileo system, work by timing. Your phone receives signals from at least four satellites, each saying when it was sent. Since radio waves travel at light speed, each microsecond of delay means about 300 m. So the clocks must agree to within billionths of a second.
### Two effects, opposite directions
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "microseconds per day vs ground", "items": [{"l": "Speed", "v": 7.0, "t": "−7"}, {"l": "Height (gravity)", "v": 45.0, "t": "+45"}, {"l": "Net", "v": 38.0, "t": "+38", "hi": true}]}
GPS satellites orbit about 20,200 km up at about 14,000 km/h. Their speed slows their clocks by about 7 microseconds a day. Being higher in Earth's gravity makes them tick faster by about 45. The net result: +38 microseconds a day.
ex: Error per day: 38 µs | Light travels 0.3 km per µs | 38 × 0.3 ≈ 11 km | Positions drift about 10 km a day
### The fix
Engineers set the satellite clocks to run slightly slow before launch, at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so they tick at the right rate in orbit.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Satellites send time", "Phone measures delays", "Delay × c = distance", "4+ distances: position"]}
!? In 2014 two Galileo satellites were launched into the wrong, elliptical orbits. Scientists turned the mistake into an experiment, using their changing altitude to confirm the gravity effect on time more precisely than ever before.
> Your phone's map works only because engineers account for both of Einstein's theories every day.

# Bending light
If space-time is curved, light passing a heavy object should bend. Einstein predicted that starlight grazing the Sun would be deflected by 1.75 arcseconds, twice the value from Newton's theory. The only chance to see stars close to the Sun is during a total solar eclipse.
### The 1919 expeditions
On 29 May 1919, British teams photographed the eclipse from two places: Arthur Eddington on Príncipe island in the Gulf of Guinea, then a Portuguese colony, and another team at Sobral in Brazil. Clouds almost ruined Eddington's view, but the measurements favoured Einstein. In November the results were announced in London and newspapers made Einstein a global celebrity overnight.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Newton predicts", "items": ["0.87 arcseconds", "half the bend"]}, "r": {"t": "Einstein predicts", "items": ["1.75 arcseconds", "confirmed 1919"]}}
### Gravitational lenses
Whole galaxies and clusters bend light from objects far behind them. This produces arcs, multiple images, and even complete circles called Einstein rings. Astronomers use these natural lenses as telescopes to see galaxies too faint to observe otherwise, and to map invisible dark matter.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Distant galaxy shines", "Light passes cluster", "Path bends round mass", "We see arcs and rings"]}
!? There's a plaque at the Roça Sundy plantation on Príncipe marking where Eddington set up his telescope; a centenary celebration was held there in 2019.
> Mass bends light's path, and the 1919 eclipse, partly seen from Príncipe, turned this into a world headline.

# Black holes
In 1916, months after Einstein's theory appeared, Karl Schwarzschild found a solution describing a point mass. It contained a radius at which escape speed equals light speed. Squeeze any mass inside that radius and it becomes a black hole.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Earth squeezed", "b": "about 2 cm across"}, {"a": "Sun squeezed", "b": "about 6 km across"}, {"a": "M87* (real)", "b": "bigger than our Solar System"}]}
### How they form
Most stellar black holes form when a star more than about 20 times the Sun's mass runs out of fuel and its core collapses. Supermassive black holes, millions to billions of solar masses, sit at the centres of most galaxies; how they grew so big so early is an open question.
### Seeing the unseeable
The Event Horizon Telescope linked radio dishes across the planet into a virtual telescope the size of Earth. In April 2019 it published an image of the black hole in galaxy M87: a dark shadow inside a ring of glowing gas, 6.5 billion solar masses, 55 million light-years away. In 2022 it imaged Sagittarius A*, the 4-million-solar-mass black hole at our galaxy's centre.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1916", "l": "Schwarzschild solution"}, {"y": "1971", "l": "Cygnus X-1 identified"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "Mergers heard (LIGO)"}, {"y": "2019", "l": "First image, M87*"}]}
### Near the edge
Near a small black hole, the pull on your feet would be far stronger than on your head, stretching you thin, called "spaghettification". Stephen Hawking showed in 1974 that quantum effects should make black holes slowly leak radiation and evaporate.
!! Black holes suck in everything around them => From a distance, a black hole pulls like any object of the same mass; if the Sun became one, Earth's orbit wouldn't change
> A black hole is mass squeezed so tightly that its event horizon hides everything inside from the rest of the universe.

# Energy and mass
E = mc² means every form of energy has mass, and mass is a form of energy. Most of the time the effect is far too small to notice. In nuclear reactions it becomes obvious.
### The missing mass in fusion
ex: 4 hydrogen atoms: 4.0313 mass units | 1 helium-4 atom: 4.0026 mass units | Difference: 0.0287, about 0.7% | That 0.7% becomes the Sun's energy
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Separate protons, neutrons", "Bind into a nucleus", "Nucleus weighs less", "Difference released"]}
### Binding energy
The energy holding a nucleus together shows up as missing mass. Iron and nickel nuclei are the most tightly bound, which is why light nuclei release energy by fusing towards iron and heavy nuclei by splitting towards it.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Light nuclei", "items": ["fuse together", "towards iron"]}, "r": {"t": "Heavy nuclei", "items": ["split apart", "towards iron"]}}
### Everyday mass changes
• A hot cup of coffee weighs slightly more than when it's cold
• A wound-up spring weighs slightly more than an unwound one
• Burning petrol: the exhaust weighs about one part in ten billion less than fuel plus oxygen
All true, but far too small for any scale to detect.
!? Most of your body's mass doesn't come from the mass of quarks at all. Over 98% of a proton's mass is the energy of the quarks and the gluon fields binding them.
> Mass is stored energy: in nuclear reactions the products weigh less, and the difference is the energy released.

== ph-quantum

# Energy comes in packets
In 1900 Max Planck was trying to explain the colours of light given off by hot objects, such as a glowing iron bar. The classical theory predicted absurd results: infinite energy at ultraviolet wavelengths. Planck fixed the maths by assuming energy could only be emitted in chunks, which he called quanta. He thought it was a trick; it turned out to be how nature works.
= E = h × f
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "E = h × f", "items": [{"s": "E", "m": "energy of one photon"}, {"s": "h", "m": "Planck's constant"}, {"s": "f", "m": "frequency of the light"}]}
### Colour sets the packet size
Higher frequency means more energetic photons. That is why ultraviolet light can damage skin cells and cause sunburn while a much brighter red lamp cannot: each UV photon carries enough energy to break chemical bonds in DNA.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy per photon (electronvolts)", "items": [{"l": "Radio (FM)", "v": 4e-07, "t": "0.0000004"}, {"l": "Red light", "v": 1.8}, {"l": "Blue light", "v": 2.8}, {"l": "UV-B", "v": 4.1, "hi": true}]}
ex: Red light: f ≈ 4.3 × 10¹⁴ Hz | h = 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s | E = h × f ≈ 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ J | About 1.8 electronvolts per photon
### Why we don't notice
Each photon is tiny. A small 5 milliwatt laser pointer sends out roughly 15 million billion photons every second, so the light looks perfectly smooth.
!? Photographers' darkrooms used red safelights because red photons don't carry enough energy to trigger the chemistry in photographic paper.
!! Brighter light means more energetic photons => Brightness is the number of photons; colour (frequency) sets the energy of each one
> Light is delivered in packets, and its colour decides how much energy each packet carries.

# The photoelectric effect
Heinrich Hertz noticed in 1887 that ultraviolet light helped sparks jump between metal electrodes. Later experiments showed the light was knocking electrons out of the metal, and the details made no sense if light were just a wave.
### The puzzle
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Wave theory said", "items": ["brighter = faster electrons", "any colour, given time"]}, "r": {"t": "Experiments showed", "items": ["colour sets electron speed", "below a cut-off: nothing"]}}
### Einstein's answer (1905)
Light arrives as photons. One photon gives all its energy to one electron. If that energy is less than the metal's "work function", the energy needed to escape, no electron leaves however many photons arrive. Brighter light means more photons, so more electrons, but not faster ones.
= electron energy = photon energy − work function
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Photon hits metal", "Gives energy to 1 electron", "Pays escape cost", "Leftover = speed"]}
### Recognition
Robert Millikan spent ten years testing Einstein's equation, half expecting to disprove it, and instead confirmed it in 1916. Einstein's Nobel Prize, for 1921, was awarded for this work, not for relativity, which was still considered controversial.
### Everywhere today
• Solar panels turn photons into electric current (a close cousin, the photovoltaic effect)
• Camera sensors in phones count photons pixel by pixel
• Night-vision devices and photomultipliers amplify single photons
!? Caesium releases electrons with visible light because its work function is only about 2 eV; zinc needs ultraviolet, over 4 eV.
> One photon frees one electron, but only if that single photon has enough energy.

# Wave–particle duality
In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and saw stripes of light and dark on a screen. That is **interference**, waves adding where crests meet and cancelling where crest meets trough. It seemed to prove light was a wave, until Einstein's photons.
### Matter waves
In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that particles also behave as waves, with a wavelength that shrinks as momentum grows. In 1927 electrons fired at a nickel crystal were found to diffract exactly like waves.
= wavelength = h ÷ momentum
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1801", "l": "Young's two slits"}, {"y": "1905", "l": "Light as photons"}, {"y": "1924", "l": "de Broglie's waves"}, {"y": "1927", "l": "Electrons diffract"}, {"y": "1989", "l": "1 electron at a time"}]}
### One at a time
In 1989 Akira Tonomura's team at Hitachi sent electrons through a double-slit device so slowly that only one was in the apparatus at a time. Each arrived as a single dot. But over thousands of arrivals, the dots built up interference stripes. Each electron behaved as a wave passing through both slits, then landed as a particle.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fire single electrons", "Each lands as a dot", "Dots build stripes", "Wave went through both"]}
### Look and it changes
If you add a detector to see which slit each electron uses, the stripes vanish and you get two plain bands. Gaining "which-path" information destroys the interference.
!? Interference has been shown with molecules of up to about 2,000 atoms. Electron microscopes rely on electrons' tiny wavelength to see details far smaller than light allows.
> Everything small travels like a wave and arrives like a particle.

# Uncertainty
In 1927 Werner Heisenberg, aged 25, showed that a particle's position and momentum can't both have exact values at the same time. This isn't about clumsy instruments; it follows from particles being waves. A wave spread over a small region contains a wide mix of wavelengths, so its momentum is blurry, and vice versa.
= Δx × Δp ≥ h ÷ 4π
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Δx × Δp ≥ h/4π", "items": [{"s": "Δx", "m": "spread in position"}, {"s": "Δp", "m": "spread in momentum"}, {"s": "h", "m": "Planck's constant"}]}
### Why atoms don't collapse
ex: Electron confined to an atom, Δx ≈ 0.1 nm | Δp ≥ about 5 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s | Divide by electron mass | Speed spread ≈ 600 km/s
Squeeze an electron closer to the nucleus and its momentum spread, and so its energy, shoots up. This is why electrons don't spiral into the nucleus and why atoms have the size they do.
### Why footballs are unaffected
Planck's constant is so tiny that for anything you can see, the uncertainty is far below anything measurable.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Electron in atom", "items": ["huge speed spread", "blur dominates"]}, "r": {"t": "Football", "items": ["spread ~10⁻³¹ m/s", "effect invisible"]}}
!? Because of uncertainty, particles can never be completely still. That "zero-point" jiggling is why helium stays liquid even at absolute zero unless squeezed.
!! Uncertainty means measuring disturbs the particle => Measurement can disturb things, but the principle is deeper: a particle doesn't have both exact values to begin with
> The more sharply a particle's position is defined, the more spread out its momentum must be.

# Superposition
In quantum theory a system's state can be a blend of possibilities. An electron's spin, a kind of built-in magnetism, can be "up", "down", or a superposition of both. The state assigns each outcome a weight; squaring it gives the probability. This rule was set out by Max Born in 1926.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Prepare superposition", "Up 70%, down 30%", "Measure spin", "Get one: up or down"]}
### What measuring does
Measure, and you always get one definite result. Repeat the experiment with identically prepared electrons and the results follow the probabilities. Before measurement, the electron isn't secretly up or down; experiments rule out that kind of hidden answer in many situations.
### Schrödinger's cat
In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger imagined a cat in a box whose fate depends on a single decaying atom. If the atom is in superposition, is the cat both alive and dead? He meant it as a criticism, to show how strange the theory sounds when scaled up.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tiny, isolated", "items": ["superposition lasts", "interference visible"]}, "r": {"t": "Big, warm", "items": ["environment 'measures'", "looks classical"]}}
### Why we don't see it
Large objects constantly interact with air molecules and light, which effectively measures them. This process, **decoherence**, destroys superpositions almost instantly. Keeping superpositions alive is the main challenge in building quantum computers.
!! The cat really is half alive => Schrödinger used the cat to mock a naive reading; a cat is constantly decohered by its surroundings
> A quantum system holds several possibilities at once until an interaction forces one outcome.

# Entanglement
When two particles interact in certain ways, they can share a single quantum state. Measure one and you instantly know what the matching measurement on the other will show, even if it's on the other side of the planet.
### Einstein's objection
In 1935 Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen argued this meant quantum theory was incomplete: the particles must carry hidden instructions from the start. In 1964 John Bell found a way to test that idea. If hidden instructions were real, certain correlations could never exceed a limit.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1935", "l": "EPR paper"}, {"y": "1964", "l": "Bell's test proposed"}, {"y": "1982", "l": "Aspect's experiments"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "Nobel Prize awarded"}]}
### The verdict
Experiments by John Clauser, Alain Aspect and Anton Zeilinger broke Bell's limit again and again, in line with quantum theory. They shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. Nature is not "locally" pre-programmed in the way Einstein hoped.
### No faster-than-light messages
Each side's results, on their own, look completely random. Only when the two lists are compared, using an ordinary signal, does the correlation appear. So entanglement can't send information faster than light.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Quantum cryptography", "b": "tampering reveals itself"}, {"a": "Quantum computers", "b": "qubits work together"}, {"a": "Quantum sensors", "b": "beyond normal precision"}]}
!? In 2017 China's Micius satellite sent entangled photons to ground stations about 1,200 km apart. The EU's EuroQCI project, which includes Portugal, is building quantum-secured communication links.
> Entangled particles share one state: their results match across any distance, yet no message can ride on them.

# Tunnelling
Roll a ball at a hill without enough speed and it rolls back. A quantum particle is a wave, and part of the wave leaks into the barrier and out the other side. So there's a chance the particle simply appears beyond it, even without enough energy to climb over.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Wave meets barrier", "Fades inside it", "Small part emerges", "Particle may get through"]}
### Thin barriers only
The chance falls off steeply with the barrier's width and height. For an electron, a barrier a few atoms thick is passable; for anything you can see, the odds are effectively zero.
### Where it matters
• **The Sun**: protons in the core don't have enough energy to touch. Tunnelling lets a few fuse, enough to power the Sun for 10 billion years
• **Alpha decay**: George Gamow explained in 1928 how alpha particles escape nuclei by tunnelling, which accounts for the huge range of half-lives
• **Flash memory**: in USB sticks and phones, electrons tunnel through a thin insulating layer to store bits
• **Microscopes**: the scanning tunnelling microscope images individual atoms
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Quantum tunnelling", "items": ["Fusion in the Sun", "Alpha decay", "Flash memory", "Tunnelling microscope"]}
### A limit for chips
As transistors shrink to a few nanometres, electrons tunnel through barriers meant to stop them, leaking current and wasting power. Chipmakers redesign transistors partly to fight this.
!? Without tunnelling, the Sun's core would need to be roughly a thousand times hotter for fusion to proceed at its actual rate. Life on Earth depends on a quantum leak.
> Particles can cross barriers they lack the energy to climb, and that leak powers the stars.

# Quantum in your pocket
Quantum physics can sound remote, but it's built into most of the technology you use. Understanding how electrons behave in solids led to the electronics revolution.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Transistor (1947)", "b": "electron bands in silicon"}, {"a": "Laser (1960)", "b": "stimulated emission"}, {"a": "Blue LED (1990s)", "b": "precise band gaps"}, {"a": "MRI", "b": "spin of hydrogen nuclei"}]}
### The transistor
Silicon conducts in a controlled way because of how quantum rules arrange electron energies into "bands". The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, uses this to switch current on and off. A modern phone chip holds around 15–20 billion of them, each a few tens of atoms across in key dimensions.
### Light and medicine
• **Lasers** read barcodes, carry internet data along fibres and perform eye surgery
• **LEDs** emit a colour set by their band gap; the blue LED, which made white LED lighting possible, won the 2014 Nobel Prize
• **MRI scanners** flip the spins of hydrogen nuclei in your body and listen to the radio signals they give back
### Timekeeping
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Caesium atoms", "Exact energy jump", "9,192,631,770 Hz", "Defines one second"]}
Since 1967 the second has been defined by the frequency of a quantum jump in caesium atoms. Atomic clocks keep GPS and Galileo working, and timestamp phone networks and financial trades.
!? Solar panels, digital cameras, smoke detectors, fibre-optic internet and the hard drive in a data centre all depend on quantum effects.
> Quantum physics isn't exotic: it's the hidden engine of almost every electronic device.

# Quantum computers
A normal bit is 0 or 1. A **qubit** can be in a superposition of both, and many qubits can be entangled. A quantum computer manipulates these combined states so that wrong answers cancel out through interference and right ones are reinforced.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "amplitudes tracked (powers of 2)", "items": [{"l": "10 qubits", "v": 10.0, "t": "~1,000"}, {"l": "50 qubits", "v": 50.0, "t": "~10¹⁵"}, {"l": "300 qubits", "v": 300.0, "t": "~10⁹⁰", "hi": true}]}
Describing 300 qubits exactly would take more numbers than there are atoms in the observable universe (about 10⁸⁰).
### What they'd be good at
• Simulating molecules and materials, for drugs, batteries and fertilisers
• Breaking widely used public-key encryption, using Shor's algorithm (1994)
• Some optimisation and search problems, though the speed-ups are often modest
### Why it's hard
Qubits are fragile: heat, vibration and stray signals cause errors. Superconducting qubits are cooled to about −273 °C, colder than outer space. Error correction combines many physical qubits into one reliable "logical" qubit. In 2024 Google's Willow chip showed errors falling as more qubits were added, a key milestone.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Normal computer", "items": ["bits: 0 or 1", "great at most tasks"]}, "r": {"t": "Quantum computer", "items": ["qubits: superposed", "only some problems"]}}
### Preparing now
Because encrypted data stolen today could be decrypted later, governments are moving to "post-quantum" encryption. The US standards body published the first such standards in 2024, and the EU wants critical systems switched by the early 2030s.
!! Quantum computers try every answer at once => They can't simply read out all answers; algorithms must use interference cleverly, so they help only for specific problems
> Quantum computers won't replace laptops, but for a few important problems they could do what no normal machine can.

# Founders of quantum theory
Quantum mechanics was built in a burst, mostly by young physicists in Europe. Heisenberg was 23 when he made his breakthrough, and Paul Dirac 26 when he wrote his equation. Older colleagues nicknamed it "Knabenphysik", boys' physics.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1900", "l": "Planck: quanta"}, {"y": "1905", "l": "Einstein: photons"}, {"y": "1913", "l": "Bohr: atom model"}, {"y": "1925", "l": "Heisenberg: matrices"}, {"y": "1926", "l": "Schrödinger: waves"}]}
### Other key builders
• **Louis de Broglie** (1924): matter waves
• **Wolfgang Pauli** (1925): no two electrons in the same state, which explains the periodic table
• **Max Born** (1926): the wave tells you probabilities
• **Paul Dirac** (1928): merged quantum theory with special relativity and predicted antimatter, found in 1932
### Arguments that shaped it
At the 1927 Solvay Conference in Brussels, Einstein and Bohr began a famous, friendly debate. Einstein, uneasy with chance at the heart of physics, said God does not play dice; Bohr defended the new theory. Of the 29 people in the conference photograph, 17 were or later became Nobel laureates.
### How precise?
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~25 years", "l": "to build the theory"}, {"v": "1 in 10¹²", "l": "QED's electron magnet match"}, {"v": "17 of 29", "l": "Solvay 1927 Nobel winners"}]}
Quantum electrodynamics predicts the electron's magnetism and it matches experiment to about one part in a trillion, the most precise agreement in science.
!! Quantum theory is just a speculative idea => It is the best-tested theory in physics and underpins chemistry, electronics and lasers
> A generation of physicists rewrote the rules of nature in 25 years, and every test since has backed them.

# The Sun
The Sun holds about 99.8% of all the mass in the Solar System, roughly 333,000 times Earth's. Everything else, from Jupiter to the smallest grain of dust, is a crumb by comparison.
### Layers of the Sun
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Corona: over 1 million °C", "Photosphere: about 5,500 °C", "Convection zone: boiling gas", "Radiative zone", "Core: 15 million °C"]}
Energy made in the core takes a very long time to stagger out through the dense radiative zone, then rises by convection. The visible surface, the photosphere, looks granular, like boiling rice, with cells about the size of Portugal.
### The surprising corona
Strangely, the thin outer atmosphere, the corona, is hundreds of times hotter than the surface. Magnetic fields are thought to carry energy up and release it, and missions like NASA's Parker Solar Probe and ESA's Solar Orbiter are investigating exactly how.
### An active star
• Sunspots are cooler, darker regions where magnetic fields poke through
• Their number rises and falls over an 11-year cycle; the latest peak came around 2024–25
• Flares and coronal mass ejections fling charged particles into space
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Solar storm erupts", "Particles reach Earth", "Hit magnetic field", "Auroras, grid risks"]}
!? In May 2024 the strongest geomagnetic storm in two decades produced auroras visible as far south as Portugal and Spain, a rare sight at these latitudes.
!! The Sun is on fire => There is no burning; it shines by nuclear fusion, which needs no oxygen
> The Sun dominates the Solar System by mass and energy, and its magnetic moods reach all the way to Earth.

# Eight planets
The planets formed from a disc of gas and dust around the young Sun. Close in, it was too hot for ice, so only rock and metal could clump together, giving small rocky planets. Beyond the "frost line", between Mars and Jupiter, ice was plentiful, cores grew big enough to grab huge amounts of gas, and giants formed.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rocky (inner)", "items": ["Mercury, Venus", "Earth, Mars", "small, dense"]}, "r": {"t": "Giants (outer)", "items": ["Jupiter, Saturn: gas", "Uranus, Neptune: ice", "huge, many moons"]}}
### Sizes compared
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "diameter, Earth = 1", "items": [{"l": "Mercury", "v": 0.38}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Neptune", "v": 3.9}, {"l": "Saturn", "v": 9.4}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 11.2, "hi": true}]}
### What happened to Pluto?
In 2005 astronomers found Eris, beyond Pluto and about as large. Either the Solar System had many more planets, or the definition needed tightening. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union in Prague agreed a planet must:
• orbit the Sun
• be massive enough to be pulled into a round shape
• have cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other bodies
Pluto fails the third test, since it shares its zone with many Kuiper Belt objects. It became a **dwarf planet**, alongside Ceres, Eris, Haumea and Makemake.
!? In 2015 NASA's New Horizons flew past Pluto and found mountains of water ice, nitrogen glaciers and a heart-shaped plain, a far livelier world than expected.
> Rocky worlds formed near the hot Sun, giants formed out where ice could survive, and Pluto belongs to a crowd of icy dwarfs.

# Venus: the hothouse
Venus is Earth's near twin in size, about 95% of its diameter, and formed from similar material. Yet its surface is the most hostile in the Solar System for a landing probe.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth", "items": ["about 15 °C average", "1 bar air pressure", "N₂ and O₂ air"]}, "r": {"t": "Venus", "items": ["about 465 °C", "about 92 bar", "96% CO₂"]}}
### A runaway greenhouse
Venus probably once had more water. Being closer to the Sun, it warmed, water evaporated, and water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas, trapped more heat. Eventually the oceans boiled away, and with no liquid water to lock carbon into rocks, CO₂ built up into a crushing atmosphere. Today its thick clouds of sulphuric acid reflect most sunlight, yet the trapped heat keeps the surface hot enough to melt lead.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Warmer than Earth", "Water evaporates", "More greenhouse heat", "Oceans lost, CO₂ builds"]}
### Strange rotation
Venus spins backwards compared with most planets, and very slowly: one rotation takes 243 Earth days, longer than its 225-day year.
### Visiting Venus
Soviet Venera probes landed in the 1970s and 80s. The longest survived about two hours before heat and pressure destroyed it. New missions are planned for the 2030s, including ESA's EnVision.
!? The pressure on Venus's surface equals being about 900 m deep in Earth's ocean.
!! Mercury is hottest because it's closest to the Sun => Venus is hotter, day and night, because its thick CO₂ atmosphere traps heat
> Venus shows what a runaway greenhouse effect can do to an Earth-sized planet.

# Mars
Mars is about half Earth's width and has 38% of its gravity, so you would weigh about a third as much there. Its red colour comes from iron oxide, rust, in its dust. Its air is about 1% as dense as Earth's, and mostly CO₂.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "−60 °C", "l": "average temperature"}, {"v": "22 km", "l": "height of Olympus Mons"}, {"v": "24 h 37 min", "l": "length of a Mars day"}]}
### Giant landscapes
• **Olympus Mons** is about 22 km high, two and a half times Everest, and around 600 km wide, about the length of mainland Portugal
• **Valles Marineris** is a canyon system some 4,000 km long, which would stretch across Europe
• Polar caps of water ice and frozen CO₂ grow and shrink with the seasons
### A wetter past
Dried riverbeds, deltas and minerals that form only in water show that Mars had lakes and rivers over 3 billion years ago. It lost much of its atmosphere, likely because its magnetic field died and the solar wind stripped gas away, and its surface water froze or escaped.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1976", "l": "Viking landers"}, {"y": "1997", "l": "First rover"}, {"y": "2012", "l": "Curiosity lands"}, {"y": "2021", "l": "Perseverance lands"}]}
### Searching for life
Perseverance landed in Jezero crater in 2021, an ancient lake with a river delta. It has been collecting rock samples in sealed tubes. In 2025 NASA reported one rock's chemical patterns as possible signs of past microbial life, but such features can also form without life, and confirming it would need the samples back on Earth.
!? Perseverance carried a small helicopter, Ingenuity, which made 72 flights, the first powered flights on another world.
> Mars was once warm and wet enough for rivers; whether life ever began there is one of science's biggest questions.

# Jupiter and Saturn
Jupiter is more than twice as massive as all the other planets put together. About 1,300 Earths would fit inside it. It spins in under 10 hours, the fastest of any planet, which flattens it visibly at the poles.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Jupiter", "items": ["11 Earths wide", "day under 10 hours", "storm: Great Red Spot"]}, "r": {"t": "Saturn", "items": ["9.4 Earths wide", "density 0.69 g/cm³", "rings of ice"]}}
### The Great Red Spot
This storm has raged for at least 190 years, and possibly since the 1600s. Its winds blow at hundreds of km/h. It has been shrinking: once wide enough for three Earths, it's now a little larger than one.
### Saturn's rings
The rings span about 280,000 km but are typically only about 10 m thick, thinner, relative to their width, than a sheet of paper. They are mostly chunks of water ice, from dust grains to house-sized boulders. Their age is debated; some evidence suggests they could be far younger than Saturn.

# Moons
Mercury and Venus have no moons; Earth has one; Mars has two tiny ones. The giants have dozens each: Saturn has over 270 known and Jupiter close to 100, most of them small captured rocks a few kilometres wide.
### How our Moon formed
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mars-sized Theia hits", "Debris thrown into orbit", "Debris clumps together", "The Moon forms"]}
Evidence for this giant impact: Moon rocks brought back by Apollo have almost the same oxygen isotope mix as Earth's mantle, the Moon has only a small iron core, and its surface was once a global ocean of magma.
### The Moon is leaving
Lasers bounced off mirrors left by Apollo astronauts show the Moon drifting away by about 3.8 cm a year. Tides slow Earth's spin, and the Moon gains that energy. Days are lengthening by about 2 milliseconds per century.
### Worlds in their own right
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ganymede (Jupiter)", "b": "largest moon, beats Mercury"}, {"a": "Io (Jupiter)", "b": "hundreds of volcanoes"}, {"a": "Europa (Jupiter)", "b": "ocean under the ice"}, {"a": "Titan (Saturn)", "b": "thick air, methane lakes"}]}

!? The Moon is about 400 times smaller than the Sun but also about 400 times closer, which is why it can just cover the Sun in a total eclipse, like the one crossing northern Spain in August 2026.
> Our Moon was probably born in a giant collision, and moons elsewhere include some of the Solar System's most promising places for life.

# Asteroids and comets
Asteroids and comets are leftover building blocks from 4.6 billion years ago. Asteroids are rocky or metallic and mostly orbit between Mars and Jupiter, where Jupiter's gravity stopped them forming a planet. Comets are icy and come from far out.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Asteroids", "items": ["rock and metal", "asteroid belt", "no tail"]}, "r": {"t": "Comets", "items": ["ice and dust", "Kuiper Belt, Oort Cloud", "tails near the Sun"]}}
### The belt is empty
Films show asteroids packed close together, but the main belt is mostly empty. Its total mass is only about 3% of the Moon's, and a third of that is the dwarf planet Ceres. Spacecraft pass through without dodging.
### Comet tails
As a comet nears the Sun, ice turns straight to gas, releasing dust. It forms two tails, both pointing away from the Sun: a curved dust tail and a straight blue tail of charged gas blown by the solar wind.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Icy comet far away", "Nears Sun, ice vaporises", "Coma of gas and dust", "Tails pushed away"]}
### Planetary defence
On 26 September 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately crashed into the small asteroid Dimorphos and shortened its orbit around its partner by about 32 minutes: the first test of nudging an asteroid. ESA's Hera mission is following up.

!? Meteor showers, like the Perseids peaking around 12 August, happen when Earth passes through dust left by comets. Each "shooting star" is a grain often smaller than a grain of sand.

# Seasons on other planets
Seasons come from a planet's tilt, not its distance from the Sun. When your hemisphere tilts towards the Sun, sunlight arrives more directly and days are longer. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the northern winter.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "axial tilt, degrees", "items": [{"l": "Jupiter", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 23.4}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Saturn", "v": 27.0}, {"l": "Uranus", "v": 98.0, "hi": true}]}
### Uranus on its side
Uranus is tilted 98°, probably knocked over by an ancient collision. During its 84-year orbit, each pole points almost straight at the Sun for part of the year, then away.
ex: Uranus year: 84 Earth years | Each pole: half the orbit in light | 84 ÷ 2 = 42 years | Then 42 years of darkness
### Elsewhere
• **Mars** has a similar tilt to Earth, so similar seasons, but each lasts about twice as long; its elongated orbit makes southern summers hotter
• **Saturn** and **Neptune** have seasons lasting 7 and about 40 years
• **Jupiter**, barely tilted, has almost no seasons
• **Mercury**, with almost no tilt, has craters at its poles never touched by sunlight, holding ice despite being closest to the Sun

> Tilt, not distance, makes seasons, and a planet tipped on its side gets decades-long days and nights.

# Age
The Solar System is about 4.57 billion years old. The most precise date comes from tiny white grains in meteorites called calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions, the first solids to condense from the cloud around the newborn Sun.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gas and dust cloud", "Collapses, spins", "Sun forms in centre", "Disc builds planets"]}
### How do we date it?
Radioactive uranium decays into lead at known rates. Measuring the ratio of uranium to lead isotopes in meteorite grains gives their age. In 1956 Clair Patterson used lead in a meteorite to calculate Earth's age at about 4.55 billion years, a figure that has barely changed since.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "4.57 bn", "l": "First solids form"}, {"y": "4.5 bn", "l": "Earth and Moon"}, {"y": "4.4 bn", "l": "Oldest Earth crystals"}, {"y": "3.8 bn", "l": "Signs of early life"}]}
### Clues from the beginning
• Meteorites are unchanged leftovers, unlike Earth's constantly recycled crust
• The oldest Earth crystals, zircons from western Australia, are about 4.4 billion years old
• Traces of short-lived isotopes in meteorites suggest a nearby exploding star may have helped trigger the cloud's collapse
### Watching it happen elsewhere
Radio telescopes such as ALMA in Chile have imaged young stars surrounded by dusty discs with gaps, where planets are being born right now, as our own system once was.
!? While measuring lead, Patterson discovered how much lead pollution from petrol was everywhere, and his campaign helped get leaded petrol banned; the EU phased it out by 2000.
> Meteorites are time capsules from the Solar System's birth, and their radioactive clocks give its age: about 4.57 billion years.

== ph-stars

# What a star is
Stars are born inside cold clouds of gas and dust called nebulae. A denser clump starts to collapse under its own gravity, heating up as it shrinks. When the core reaches about 10 million °C, hydrogen nuclei slam together hard enough to fuse, and a star switches on.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cold gas cloud", "Clump collapses", "Core heats up", "Fusion ignites: a star"]}
### A balancing act
For most of its life a star is stable because two forces cancel. Gravity pulls every layer inwards; the pressure of hot gas, fed by fusion, pushes outwards. If fusion speeds up, the star swells and cools slightly, and fusion slows again. Astronomers call this **hydrostatic equilibrium**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gravity", "items": ["pulls inwards", "wants to collapse"]}, "r": {"t": "Gas pressure", "items": ["pushes outwards", "fed by fusion"]}}
### How small, how big
• The lightest stars have about 8% of the Sun's mass, roughly 80 Jupiters. Below that, the core never gets hot enough: you get a failed star, a **brown dwarf**.
• The heaviest known stars have over 100 times the Sun's mass.
• Most stars are small, dim **red dwarfs**, about three in four. Not one is visible to the naked eye.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.05, "hi": 200.0, "log": true, "unit": "mass (Suns)", "items": [{"l": "Red dwarf", "v": 0.1}, {"l": "Sun", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Rigel", "v": 21.0}, {"l": "Heaviest", "v": 150.0}]}
!! Stars are giant fires => Fire is chemistry that needs oxygen; stars shine by fusing atomic nuclei, millions of times more energy per kilo
!? Many stars are not alone: a large share of Sun-like stars have a companion star, orbiting together as a pair.
> A star is a long tug-of-war between gravity squeezing in and fusion pushing out.

# Light-years
Despite the name, a light-year measures distance, not time. Light crosses about 300,000 km every second, the fastest anything can travel, and a year contains about 31.6 million seconds.
ex: 300,000 km/s × 31.6 million s | ≈ 9,500,000,000,000 km | 1 light-year ≈ 9.5 trillion km
### A ladder of light-times
The same idea works for shorter hops, and it gives a feel for the emptiness of space.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Moon", "b": "1.3 light-seconds"}, {"a": "Sun", "b": "8.3 light-minutes"}, {"a": "Neptune", "b": "about 4 light-hours"}, {"a": "Proxima Centauri", "b": "4.2 light-years"}]}
### Our nearest neighbour
Proxima Centauri is a small red dwarf, too faint to see without a telescope. It belongs to a triple system with Alpha Centauri A and B, which together look like one bright star from the southern sky. That gap of 4.2 light-years is about 40 trillion km.
ex: Voyager 1 travels at about 17 km/s | 40 trillion km ÷ 17 km/s ≈ 2.4 trillion s | About 75,000 years to reach Proxima
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "9.5 trillion", "l": "km in one light-year"}, {"v": "75,000", "l": "years for Voyager 1"}]}
Professional astronomers often use the **parsec** instead, about 3.26 light-years, which comes from how far a star seems to shift as Earth orbits the Sun.
!! A light-year is a long time => It is a distance: how far light goes in a year
> A light-year is a distance so large that our fastest spacecraft would need tens of thousands of years to cross one to the nearest star.

# Colours of stars
Heat anything enough and it glows. An electric hob ring glows dull red; a filament bulb is hotter and glows yellow-white. Stars follow the same physics: the hotter the surface, the more the light shifts towards blue. This is **Wien's law**: peak wavelength shrinks as temperature rises.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "surface temperature, °C (about)", "items": [{"l": "Betelgeuse", "v": 3300.0}, {"l": "Sun", "v": 5500.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Sirius", "v": 9700.0}, {"l": "Rigel", "v": 11800.0}]}
### The star alphabet
Astronomers sort stars into classes by their spectra, from hottest to coolest: O, B, A, F, G, K, M. Generations of students have learnt it as "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me". The Sun is a G star.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["O, B: blue, over 10,000 °C", "A, F: white", "G: yellow-white (Sun)", "K, M: orange to red, cool"]}
### See it yourself
On a winter night, find Orion. Betelgeuse at the top left shines orange-red; Rigel at the bottom right is blue-white. The difference is easy to see without a telescope, and it is a difference of thousands of degrees.
!! Red means hot => On taps red means hot, but among stars red is the coolest and blue the hottest
!? There are no green stars. A star hot enough to peak in green also gives out plenty of red and blue, and the mix looks white to us. The Sun's light actually peaks in the green.
> A star's colour is a thermometer you can read from trillions of kilometres away.

# Life of a Sun-like star
The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago and is roughly halfway through its hydrogen-burning life. It isn't static, though: it grows about 10% brighter every billion years as helium builds up in its core.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "−4.6 bn", "l": "Sun is born"}, {"y": "Now", "l": "Middle age"}, {"y": "+1 bn", "l": "Earth's oceans boil off"}, {"y": "+5 bn", "l": "Red giant"}, {"y": "+7 bn", "l": "White dwarf"}]}
### Running out of fuel
When the core hydrogen is used up, the core shrinks and heats while the outer layers swell enormously. The Sun will become a **red giant** perhaps 200 times its current width, swallowing Mercury and Venus. Whether Earth is engulfed is still uncertain. Later the core fuses helium into carbon and oxygen.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hydrogen runs out", "Swells to red giant", "Outer layers drift off", "White dwarf remains"]}
### The quiet end
The outer layers are puffed away into a glowing shell called a **planetary nebula**. The name is a historical mistake: through 18th-century telescopes they looked like round planets. What is left is a white dwarf, a carbon-oxygen core about the size of Earth.
ex: A white dwarf has about half the Sun's mass | Squeezed into an Earth-sized ball | A teaspoon would weigh several tonnes
!? With no fusion, a white dwarf just slowly cools. It takes so long to fade to a cold "black dwarf" that the universe isn't yet old enough for any to exist.
> The Sun will die gently, not with a bang: a swollen red giant, then a fading white ember the size of Earth.

# Massive stars explode
Big stars live fast. Their huge weight makes the core hotter and denser, so fusion runs far faster. A star of 20 solar masses burns through its fuel in about 10 million years, a thousandth of the Sun's lifespan.
### An onion of elements
As each fuel runs out, the core fuses heavier elements: helium, then carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon. The star ends up layered like an onion. The process stops at **iron**, because fusing iron absorbs energy instead of releasing it.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Hydrogen (outer layer)", "Helium", "Carbon, neon, oxygen", "Silicon", "Iron core"]}
### Collapse and rebound
Once the iron core passes about 1.4 solar masses, nothing can hold it up. It collapses in under a second, the infalling layers bounce off the ultra-dense centre, and a flood of neutrinos helps blast the star apart. For a few weeks, a single supernova can outshine its whole galaxy.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1054", "l": "Crab supernova seen"}, {"y": "1572", "l": "Tycho's star"}, {"y": "1604", "l": "Kepler's star"}, {"y": "1987", "l": "SN 1987A, neutrinos"}]}
The 1054 explosion was recorded by Chinese astronomers and was visible in daylight for weeks. Its remains are the Crab Nebula. In 1987, detectors in Japan and the USA caught about two dozen neutrinos from a supernova in a neighbouring galaxy, hours before its light was seen.
!! Betelgeuse is about to explode => It will go supernova, but "soon" for astronomers means sometime in the next 100,000 years or so
!? Much of the iron in your blood and the oxygen you breathe was forged in stars and scattered by these explosions.
> Massive stars build the heavy elements, then explode and seed space with the raw material for planets and people.

# Neutron stars
When a massive star's core collapses, electrons are forced into protons, turning them into neutrons. The result is a ball of almost pure neutrons, as dense as an atomic nucleus.
ex: Density ≈ 200 million million kg per m³ | A teaspoon is about 5 millilitres | ≈ 1,000 billion kg = 1 billion tonnes
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sun", "items": ["1.4 million km wide", "rotates once a month"]}, "r": {"t": "Neutron star", "items": ["about 20 km wide", "up to 700 spins/s"]}}
### Cosmic lighthouses
A collapsing core spins up like a skater pulling in her arms. Its magnetic field shoots beams from the magnetic poles; if a beam sweeps past Earth, we see regular pulses. That is a **pulsar**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Core collapses", "Spins up fast", "Beams from magnetic poles", "Pulses sweep past Earth"]}
### Discovery
In 1967 Jocelyn Bell, a PhD student at Cambridge, noticed a signal repeating every 1.3 seconds. It was so regular the team half-jokingly labelled it "LGM-1", for Little Green Men. The 1974 Nobel Prize went to her supervisor, Antony Hewish, and not to her, a decision still widely criticised.
• Surface gravity is about 100 billion times Earth's
• Magnetars have the strongest magnetic fields known
• The fastest pulsar known spins 716 times a second
!? In 2017 detectors saw gravitational waves from two neutron stars colliding. The blast made heavy elements such as gold and platinum, several Earth masses' worth.
> A neutron star is the crushed core of a dead star: a city-sized atomic nucleus spinning like a top.

# The Milky Way
From inside, our galaxy is a faint band of light across the sky. The ancient Greeks called it a river of milk, which gives us "galaxy" (from *gala*, milk) and the Portuguese **Via Láctea**. Galileo's telescope showed in 1610 that it is made of countless stars.
### Its shape
Seen from outside, it would look like a flat disc with a central bar and spiral arms, only about 1,000 light-years thick. The Sun sits about 26,000 light-years from the centre, in a minor arm called the Orion Arm.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "100,000", "l": "light-years across"}, {"v": "26,000", "l": "light-years to the centre"}, {"v": "230 km/s", "l": "the Sun's orbital speed"}]}
### The heart
At the centre lies **Sagittarius A***, a black hole of about 4 million solar masses. Its existence was proved by tracking stars orbiting it, work that won the 2020 Nobel Prize, and it was imaged in 2022.
ex: Sun's orbit ≈ 2 × π × 26,000 light-years | ≈ 160,000 light-years round | At 230 km/s ≈ 230 million years per lap
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Galactic centre", "b": "black hole, 4 million Suns"}, {"a": "Bar and bulge", "b": "old stars, packed close"}, {"a": "Disc and arms", "b": "gas, dust, new stars"}, {"a": "Halo", "b": "old clusters, dark matter"}]}
!? The last time the Sun was at this point in its orbit, the first dinosaurs were only just appearing.
!? Portugal's Alqueva region became the world's first certified "Starlight Tourism Destination" in 2011, one of the best places in Europe to see the Milky Way.
> We live in the suburbs of a spinning spiral of hundreds of billions of stars, and we are carried around it at 230 km per second.

# Galaxies everywhere
A century ago, many astronomers thought the Milky Way was the whole universe and the fuzzy "spiral nebulae" were clouds inside it. In 1923–24 Edwin Hubble found a pulsating star in Andromeda whose brightness revealed its distance: far beyond our galaxy. Andromeda was a galaxy in its own right.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1920", "l": "The Great Debate"}, {"y": "1924", "l": "Andromeda is far away"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "Hubble Deep Field"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "Webb's first images"}]}
### Shapes of galaxies
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Galaxy types", "items": ["Spiral: disc and arms", "Elliptical: smooth ball", "Irregular: no clear shape"]}
### Counting them
In 1995 the Hubble Space Telescope stared for ten days at a patch of sky as small as a grain of sand at arm's length. It found about 3,000 galaxies. Scaled up across the whole sky, that suggests hundreds of billions; models of faint, unseen galaxies push estimates higher.
### The coming merger
Andromeda is approaching at about 110 km/s. A head-on merger has long been predicted in 4–5 billion years, though a 2025 study using newer data put the odds of a merger in the next 10 billion years at only about one in two. Even if they merge, individual stars would almost never collide: the gaps between them are vast.
!? The two Magellanic Clouds, small galaxies visible from the southern hemisphere, are named after the Portuguese navigator Fernão de Magalhães, whose crew described them on the first voyage around the world.
> The Milky Way is just one galaxy among hundreds of billions, each with its own hundreds of billions of stars.

# Looking back in time
Light is fast but not instant. Every time you look at something, you see it as it was when the light left it. Nearby, the delay is negligible. Across space, it becomes enormous.
ex: Sun to Earth ≈ 150 million km | ÷ 300,000 km/s | ≈ 500 s ≈ 8.3 minutes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Moon", "b": "as it was 1.3 s ago"}, {"a": "Sun", "b": "as it was 8 min ago"}, {"a": "Betelgeuse", "b": "as it was ~550 years ago"}, {"a": "Andromeda", "b": "2.5 million years ago"}]}
### A time machine for free
Astronomers use this delay. To study young galaxies, they look very far away, because that light set out when the universe was young. The farthest light of all, the cosmic microwave background, shows the universe about 380,000 years after the Big Bang.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Galaxy emits light", "Light crosses space", "Space and time pass", "We see the old image"]}
### Nothing is "now"
Even on Earth there is a delay: you see a friend across the room as they were about ten billionths of a second ago. In space there is no single shared "now" you can observe; there is only the latest news to arrive.
!! Many stars we see are already dead => Almost every naked-eye star is within a few thousand light-years, so nearly all of them still shine
!? When Andromeda's light now reaching your eye set off, our ancestors were early members of the human family in Africa, long before *Homo sapiens*.
> The farther you look, the further back in time you see.

# Exoplanets
For centuries, planets around other stars were pure speculation. The first confirmed ones, in 1992, orbit a pulsar. Then in 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz in Geneva found 51 Pegasi b around a Sun-like star: a gas giant orbiting so close its "year" lasts just over four days. They shared the 2019 Nobel Prize.
### How we find them
Planets are tiny and faint next to their stars, so most are detected indirectly.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Finding exoplanets", "items": ["Transit: starlight dips", "Wobble: star moves", "Direct photo", "Microlensing"]}
### The transit method
When a planet crosses its star, it blocks a fraction of the light equal to the ratio of their areas. NASA's Kepler (2009–2018) and TESS (from 2018) found thousands this way.
ex: Jupiter is about 1/10 the Sun's width | Area ratio (1/10)² = 1/100 | Starlight dims by about 1%
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "dip in starlight, %", "items": [{"l": "Jupiter-size", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Earth-size", "v": 0.008}]}
### Where things stand
The count passed 6,000 confirmed planets in 2025. Proxima b orbits our nearest star; TRAPPIST-1 has seven Earth-sized worlds. The James Webb telescope now reads some planets' atmospheres, and ESA's PLATO mission is being prepared to hunt Earth-like planets around Sun-like stars.
!? Portuguese astronomers in Porto and Lisbon helped build ESPRESSO, an instrument on ESO's Very Large Telescope in Chile that detects stellar wobbles of a few tens of centimetres per second.
> Planets are the rule, not the exception: most stars in the sky probably have at least one.

== ph-cosmos

# An expanding universe
From about 1912, Vesto Slipher measured the light of spiral nebulae and found most of it shifted towards red: they were moving away. In 1927 Georges Lemaître, a Belgian priest and physicist, showed expansion followed from Einstein's equations. In 1929 Edwin Hubble added distances and saw the pattern clearly.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = H₀ × d", "items": [{"s": "v", "m": "speed away from us"}, {"s": "H₀", "m": "about 70 km/s per Mpc"}, {"s": "d", "m": "distance, megaparsecs"}]}
ex: Galaxy at 100 megaparsecs (326 million light-years) | v = 70 × 100 | Moving away at about 7,000 km/s
### Redshift: stretched light
As light travels through expanding space, its waves are stretched to longer, redder wavelengths. The amount of stretching tells astronomers how much the universe has grown since the light set out.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Galaxy emits blue light", "Space stretches", "Waves get longer", "Arrives redder"]}
### No centre
On a balloon covered in dots, every dot sees all the others moving away, with farther ones receding faster. No dot is the centre. The same is true of galaxies.
• Things held together by gravity or electric forces don't expand: not you, not Earth, not the Milky Way
• Nearby Andromeda is actually approaching us, because local gravity wins
!! We are at the centre of the expansion => Every galaxy sees the same pattern; there is no centre
!? Since 2018 the rule is officially called the **Hubble–Lemaître law**, recognising Lemaître's earlier work.
> Galaxies aren't flying through space away from us; space between them is growing.

# The Big Bang
If galaxies are rushing apart, they were once closer together. Run the film backwards and everything converges on a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. Lemaître called it the "primeval atom". The name "Big Bang" was coined by Fred Hoyle in a 1949 BBC radio talk; he favoured a rival "steady state" theory.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "0", "l": "Hot, dense start"}, {"y": "3 min", "l": "Hydrogen, helium nuclei"}, {"y": "380 kyr", "l": "First atoms, light freed"}, {"y": "~200 Myr", "l": "First stars"}, {"y": "13.8 bn", "l": "Today"}]}
### Not an explosion
An explosion happens at a point and throws debris into surrounding space. The Big Bang happened everywhere at once: all of space was hot and dense, and all of it has been expanding since.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Explosion", "items": ["one centre", "debris flies into space"]}, "r": {"t": "Big Bang", "items": ["happened everywhere", "space itself stretches"]}}
### Why we believe it
• Galaxies are receding, as expansion predicts
• The cosmic microwave background, leftover heat from the early universe
• The amounts of hydrogen, helium and lithium match predictions
• Distant galaxies look younger and different from nearby ones
### What it doesn't say
The theory describes how the universe evolved from a very early moment. It does not explain what, if anything, came "before", or why there was a universe at all.
!! The Big Bang was a bomb going off in empty space => There was no outside; space itself was expanding everywhere
> The Big Bang is not the explosion of a thing in space, but the expansion of space itself from a hot, dense past.

# The cosmic microwave background
For its first 380,000 years the universe was a glowing fog of charged particles. Light couldn't travel far before bouncing off free electrons. When things cooled to about 3,000 °C, electrons joined nuclei to make neutral atoms, and the fog cleared. That light has been travelling ever since.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hot fog of particles", "Cools to ~3,000 °C", "Atoms form", "Light travels freely"]}
### Stretched to microwaves
Since then the universe has grown about 1,100 times larger, and the light's wavelength with it. What was orange-yellow glow is now faint microwaves.
ex: Released at about 3,000 K | Stretched about 1,100 times | 3,000 ÷ 1,100 ≈ 2.7 K, i.e. about −270 °C
### Found by accident
In 1965 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs in the USA were troubled by a hiss in their radio antenna that came from every direction. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings. Nearby physicists had just realised what it must be. The pair won the 1978 Nobel Prize.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1948", "l": "Predicted"}, {"y": "1965", "l": "Detected by accident"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "COBE: tiny ripples"}, {"y": "2013", "l": "ESA Planck map"}]}
### Ripples that became galaxies
The glow is uniform to about one part in 100,000. Those tiny ripples mark slightly denser regions which gravity later pulled into galaxies and clusters. ESA's Planck satellite mapped them in exquisite detail.
!? On an old analogue TV tuned between channels, about 1% of the "snow" was this leftover glow from the Big Bang.
> The cosmic microwave background is the oldest light in the universe: a baby photo taken 380,000 years after the Big Bang.

# Dark matter
In 1933 Fritz Zwicky noticed galaxies in the Coma cluster moving so fast the cluster should fly apart. He proposed unseen "dark matter". In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars orbit in spiral galaxies, and the evidence became hard to ignore.
### The rotation puzzle
In the Solar System, outer planets orbit more slowly, because most mass is in the Sun. Galaxies should behave the same way. They don't: stars far out orbit as fast as those further in.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Expected", "items": ["outer stars slower", "mass where light is"]}, "r": {"t": "Observed", "items": ["speeds stay flat", "extra unseen mass"]}}
### More evidence
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Evidence for dark matter", "items": ["Galaxy rotation", "Cluster motions", "Bending of light", "Ripples in the CMB"]}
In the Bullet Cluster, two clusters collided. The hot gas, most of the ordinary matter, slowed and piled up in the middle, but the gravity, mapped by how it bends background light, passed straight through. Something with mass but no light went its own way.
### What could it be?
Leading ideas are new particles, such as WIMPs or axions. Sensitive detectors deep underground have searched for decades and found nothing yet. A minority of physicists think gravity itself may need modifying instead. ESA's Euclid telescope, launched in 2023, is mapping dark matter across a third of the sky.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of all matter, %", "items": [{"l": "Dark matter", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Ordinary matter", "v": 15.0}]}
!! Dark matter is just dark gas and dust => Ordinary gas absorbs or glows in some kind of light; dark matter doesn't interact with light at all
> There seems to be about five times more invisible matter than ordinary matter, and we only know it's there by its gravity.

# Dark energy
In the 1990s two teams set out to measure how fast the expansion was slowing. They used a type of supernova that always peaks at about the same brightness, so how bright it looks reveals its distance. In 1998 they found the distant ones were too faint: farther away than expected. The expansion is speeding up.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Find distant supernovae", "Measure their brightness", "Fainter than expected", "Expansion accelerating"]}
Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize for the discovery.
### What it might be
The simplest explanation is energy belonging to empty space itself, Einstein's **cosmological constant**. As space grows, there is more of it, so its push grows too. Gravity dominated until roughly 5 billion years ago; since then dark energy has taken over.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of universe's energy, %", "items": [{"l": "Dark energy", "v": 68.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Dark matter", "v": 27.0}, {"l": "Ordinary matter", "v": 5.0}]}
### Thin but everywhere
Its density is tiny: equivalent to the mass of a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre. But it fills all of space, so it adds up to most of the cosmos.
### Still changing?
Results from the DESI survey in 2024–25 hinted that dark energy may be weakening over time. If confirmed, the cosmological constant would not be constant at all. The evidence is not yet conclusive.
!! Everything we see makes up the universe => Atoms, stars, planets and people are only about 5% of its energy content
> Most of the universe is a mysterious energy of empty space that is pushing galaxies apart ever faster.

# How big is it?
The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so you might think we can see 13.8 billion light-years in every direction. But while the light was travelling, space kept expanding, carrying its source farther away.
ex: Oldest light set off when its source was ~42 million ly away | Space then grew about 1,100 times | That source is now about 46 billion light-years away
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "46 bn ly", "l": "radius of what we can see"}, {"v": "93 bn ly", "l": "diameter"}, {"v": "~10^80", "l": "atoms inside"}]}
### Faster than light?
Nothing can move through space faster than light. But space itself can expand faster, and over large distances it does. Galaxies beyond about 14 billion light-years are already receding faster than light, and we still see light they sent long ago.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Nothing can recede faster than light", "r": "Space can grow faster than light"}]}
### Beyond the edge
The "edge" is only the limit of our view, like the horizon at sea. Measurements show space is very nearly flat, so the whole universe is at least much larger than the part we see, and may be infinite.
• Because of dark energy, light emitted today by galaxies over about 16 billion light-years away will never reach us
• Every observer has their own observable universe, centred on them
!? Over trillions of years, all galaxies outside our local group will slip beyond view. Astronomers then would see only one merged galaxy in an empty sky.
> The observable universe is a bubble 93 billion light-years across, and the whole universe is at least far larger.

# Telescopes as time machines
The James Webb Space Telescope lifted off on 25 December 2021 on a European Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, in French Guiana. It is a joint mission of NASA, ESA and the Canadian Space Agency. It orbits about 1.5 million km from Earth, hidden behind a sunshield the size of a tennis court.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "main mirror diameter, m", "items": [{"l": "Hubble (1990)", "v": 2.4}, {"l": "Webb (2021)", "v": 6.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "ESO ELT (2030s)", "v": 39.0}]}
### Why infrared?
The first galaxies gave out ultraviolet and visible light. Over 13 billion years, expansion has stretched it deep into the infrared. Webb's mirror is coated in gold, which reflects infrared well, and it is kept below about −230 °C so its own warmth doesn't swamp the faint signals.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Young galaxy shines UV", "Space stretches it", "Arrives as infrared", "Cold Webb detects it"]}
### What it found
Webb has seen galaxies from about 300 million years after the Big Bang, and they are brighter and more mature than many models expected. That puzzle is reshaping ideas about how fast the first stars and galaxies formed.
• Infrared also passes through dust, revealing stars being born inside clouds
• Webb studies the atmospheres of planets around other stars
!? Portugal is a member of ESO, the European Southern Observatory, which is building the Extremely Large Telescope in Chile's Atacama Desert.
> To see the first galaxies you must look far away, and to catch their stretched light you need a cold infrared telescope.

# Elements from the early universe
For about the first 20 minutes, the whole universe was a nuclear reactor. Protons and neutrons fused into helium and a few other light nuclei. Then expansion cooled everything too much for fusion to continue. This is called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**.
ex: About 1 neutron for every 7 protons | 16 particles: 2 neutrons + 14 protons | 2n + 2p make one helium (mass 4) | Helium ≈ 4 ÷ 16 = 25% by mass
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of ordinary matter by mass, %", "items": [{"l": "Hydrogen", "v": 74.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Helium", "v": 24.0}, {"l": "Everything else", "v": 2.0}]}
### A strong test
The theory predicts the amounts of helium, deuterium (heavy hydrogen) and lithium from first principles. Helium and deuterium match observations well. Lithium doesn't quite: old stars have about three times less than predicted, the unsolved "lithium problem".
### Where the rest came from
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Big Bang", "b": "hydrogen, helium, lithium"}, {"a": "Ordinary stars", "b": "carbon, nitrogen, oxygen"}, {"a": "Supernovae", "b": "silicon, iron and more"}, {"a": "Neutron star mergers", "b": "gold, platinum"}]}
A classic 1948 paper by Alpher and Gamow added Hans Bethe as co-author largely as a pun, so the authors read "Alpher, Bethe, Gamow", like alpha, beta, gamma.
!? The hydrogen atoms in the water you drink were made in the first minutes of the universe, 13.8 billion years ago.
> The Big Bang supplied the hydrogen and helium; nearly every other element in you was cooked later inside stars.

# Open questions
Cosmology can describe the universe with remarkable precision, yet some of its biggest ingredients remain unidentified. The open problems are not gaps at the edges: they sit at the centre of the picture.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["What is dark matter?", "What is dark energy?", "Why more matter than antimatter?", "How does gravity become quantum?", "Why do expansion rates disagree?"]}
### The missing antimatter
The Big Bang should have made matter and antimatter in equal amounts, and they annihilate on contact. Yet some matter survived. Physics has found tiny asymmetries, but far too small to explain it.
ex: For every billion antiparticles | there were about a billion and one particles | Everything we see is that "one" left over
### Quantum gravity
Quantum mechanics rules atoms; general relativity rules gravity and space. Both work superbly, but they clash inside black holes and at the Big Bang itself. String theory and loop quantum gravity are candidate fixes, untested so far.
### The Hubble tension
Measured from the cosmic microwave background, the expansion rate is about 67 km/s per megaparsec; from nearby stars and supernovae, about 73. The gap has survived years of checks. It could be an error, or a sign of new physics.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "expansion rate, km/s per Mpc", "items": [{"l": "Early universe", "v": 67.0}, {"l": "Local measures", "v": 73.0, "hi": true}]}
!? Portugal has been a member of CERN in Geneva since 1985. Experiments there make and trap antimatter to test whether it behaves exactly like matter.
> About 95% of the universe is made of things we can name but cannot yet explain.

== ph-weather

# The Sun drives the weather
Sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere at about 1,360 watts per square metre. Near the equator it strikes almost head-on; near the poles it arrives at a low angle, spread over a much larger area, and passes through more air. Over a year, the tropics receive more than twice as much solar energy per square metre as the poles.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "yearly average sunshine at top of air, W/m² (about)", "items": [{"l": "Equator", "v": 420.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Portugal (40°N)", "v": 340.0}, {"l": "North Pole", "v": 170.0}]}
### Land versus sea
Water needs about four to five times more energy than dry soil to warm by the same amount, and sunlight mixes deep into it. So land heats and cools fast while the sea changes slowly. That's why Bragança has hot summers and cold winters, while coastal Viana do Castelo stays milder all year.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Land", "items": ["heats fast", "cools fast"]}, "r": {"t": "Sea", "items": ["heats slowly", "stores heat for months"]}}
### Heat on the move
Warm air rises at the equator, flows towards the poles high up, sinks around 30° latitude and returns along the surface. This loop, the **Hadley cell**, creates the tropical rains and the desert belt, and feeds the Azores High that shapes Portugal's summers. Ocean currents such as the Gulf Stream do the rest, keeping western Europe far milder than eastern Canada at the same latitude.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Equator heats, air rises", "Flows poleward aloft", "Sinks near 30°", "Returns as trade winds"]}
!! Summer is when Earth is closest to the Sun => Earth is closest in early January; seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis
> Weather is the atmosphere and oceans endlessly trying, and failing, to spread the Sun's uneven heat evenly.

# Wind
Air pressure is the weight of the air above you, about 1,013 hectopascals at sea level. Where air is warmer or rising, pressure falls; where it is cooler or sinking, pressure rises. Air pushes from high to low, and the steeper the difference, the stronger the wind. On weather maps, closely packed isobars mean a windy day.
### The Coriolis twist
Earth spins underneath moving air. Seen from the ground, air heading north or south seems to swerve: to the right in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern. Air rushing towards a low never reaches it directly; it swirls around it instead.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["High pressure pushes", "Air flows to low", "Earth's spin deflects it", "Air circles the low"]}
• Northern hemisphere: anticlockwise around lows, clockwise around highs
• Southern hemisphere: the reverse
• Near the equator the effect vanishes, which is why hurricanes never cross it
### A handy rule
Stand with your back to the wind in the northern hemisphere and the low pressure is on your left. This is **Buys Ballot's law**, from 1857.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 12.0, "unit": "Beaufort force", "items": [{"l": "Calm", "v": 0.0}, {"l": "Breeze", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Gale", "v": 8.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Hurricane", "v": 12.0}]}
!! The Coriolis effect makes sinks drain the other way round => It is far too weak over a basin; the shape of the sink and how the water was poured decide
!? The Beaufort scale was devised in 1805 by a British naval officer, describing wind by its effect on a ship's sails; it was later adapted to effects on land and sea.
> Wind is air sliding from high to low pressure, bent into great spirals by the turning Earth.

# Clouds
As air rises, the pressure around it drops, so it expands. Expanding takes energy, and the air cools: about 10 °C per kilometre while dry. Once it cools to its **dew point**, vapour condenses onto tiny specks of dust, sea salt or smoke called condensation nuclei. Without them, clouds would struggle to form at all.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Air rises", "Expands and cools", "Reaches dew point", "Vapour condenses: cloud"]}
### Finding the cloud base
ex: Ground temperature 25 °C, dew point 13 °C | Gap = 12 °C | 12 × 125 m ≈ 1,500 m | Flat cloud bases at about 1,500 m
### Reading the sky
In 1802–03, Luke Howard, an English pharmacist, gave clouds Latin names that are still used: cumulus (heap), stratus (layer), cirrus (curl of hair) and nimbus (rain). Today there are ten main types, grouped by height.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["High: cirrus, ice wisps", "Middle: altocumulus, altostratus", "Low: stratus, stratocumulus", "Rising: cumulus, cumulonimbus"]}
• Cirrus thickening from the west often means a front within a day
• Puffy cumulus on a summer afternoon shows the ground heating the air
• Grey stratus means a shallow layer of stable, moist air
!? A modest cumulus cloud about 1 km across holds roughly 500 tonnes of water, but the droplets are so small they float on gentle updrafts.
> A cloud is the visible sign that air has risen and cooled to its dew point.

# Rain
Cloud droplets are about 0.02 mm across, so light that they fall at around a centimetre per second and are held up by the gentlest updraft. To make rain, about a million of them must combine.
ex: Raindrop ≈ 2 mm, droplet ≈ 0.02 mm | 100 times wider | Volume 100 × 100 × 100 | ≈ 1 million droplets per raindrop
### Two routes to rain
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "How raindrops form", "items": ["Warm clouds: collide, merge", "Cold clouds: ice grows, melts"]}
In warm clouds, bigger droplets fall faster and sweep up smaller ones. In clouds colder than 0 °C, water often stays liquid, "supercooled", alongside ice crystals. Vapour moves from the droplets to the ice, which grows into snowflakes. These fall and usually melt into rain on the way down. Most rain in Europe begins this way, as snow.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "falling speed, m/s (about)", "items": [{"l": "Cloud droplet", "v": 0.01}, {"l": "Drizzle drop", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Big raindrop", "v": 9.0, "hi": true}]}
### The rainy northwest
Moist Atlantic air forced up over the mountains of the Minho and Peneda-Gerês dumps heavy rain: over 3,000 mm a year in the wettest places, among the highest in Europe. Lisbon gets around 700 mm, and parts of the Alentejo less than 600.
!! Raindrops are teardrop-shaped => Small drops are spheres; bigger ones flatten like burger buns, and drops over about 5 mm break apart
> Rain is a million cloud droplets joined into one, usually after starting life as ice.

# Humidity and dew
Faster-moving water molecules escape into the air more easily, so warm air can hold far more vapour before it saturates. The maximum roughly doubles with every 10 °C rise. That is why a summer storm can drop so much water.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "max water vapour, g per m³ of air", "items": [{"l": "0 °C", "v": 4.8}, {"l": "10 °C", "v": 9.4}, {"l": "20 °C", "v": 17.3}, {"l": "30 °C", "v": 30.4, "hi": true}]}
### Relative humidity
= relative humidity = vapour present ÷ maximum possible × 100%
ex: Evening at 20 °C, air holds 8.7 g/m³ | 8.7 ÷ 17.3 ≈ 50% | Overnight it cools to about 9 °C | Now saturated: dew forms
### Why clear nights are wet
Ground loses heat by radiating it to the sky. Clouds act like a blanket and send some back; on clear, calm nights the ground and the air touching it cool quickly. Grass and car roofs, which lose heat especially fast, reach the dew point first.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Cloudy night", "items": ["clouds return heat", "little dew"]}, "r": {"t": "Clear, calm night", "items": ["heat escapes upward", "dew, fog or frost"]}}
### Fog
Fog is a cloud at ground level. Cool, damp air settles into valleys overnight, which is why the Douro and Lima valleys often wake up under fog that burns off by mid-morning.
!! 100% humidity means it is raining => It means the air is saturated; you get fog or dew, but rain needs droplets to grow and fall
!? Muggy days feel hotter because sweat evaporates slowly when the air is already near saturation, so your body loses less heat.
> Cool air down far enough and it must give up its water as dew, fog or frost.

# Fronts
An air mass is a huge body of air with similar temperature and humidity: cold and dry from the Arctic, warm and moist from the subtropics. Where two meet, they don't mix easily. The lighter warm air is pushed up over the denser cold air, cools, and builds clouds and rain.
### Cold and warm fronts
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Cold front", "items": ["steep, fast-moving", "short, heavy showers"]}, "r": {"t": "Warm front", "items": ["gentle slope", "long, steady rain"]}}
### A depression passes
Atlantic low-pressure systems usually carry a warm front followed by a cold one. You can often watch the sequence in the sky over a day or two.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["High cirrus appears", "Cloud thickens, lowers", "Steady rain (warm front)", "Showers, then clearer"]}
### Why the Minho gets so much
In winter the jet stream and its storm track sit further south, steering depressions towards Iberia. The northwest coast is first in line, and its hills squeeze extra rain from every front. Sometimes a long, narrow plume of tropical moisture, an **atmospheric river**, feeds a front and causes floods.
!? Norwegian meteorologists in Bergen named fronts around 1919, borrowing the word from the battle lines of the First World War.
> A front is a battle line between air masses, and rain falls where the warm air is forced to climb.

# Thunderstorms
A thunderstorm needs three things: moisture, unstable air that keeps rising once it starts, and a trigger such as a hot afternoon, a hill or a cold front. As vapour condenses it releases heat, making the rising air even more buoyant. Updrafts can exceed 100 km/h and drive a cumulonimbus cloud up to the top of the lower atmosphere, 12 km or more.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Warm, moist air rises", "Condensing adds heat", "Tower grows to 12 km", "Ice collisions split charge"]}
### Making lightning
Inside the cloud, small ice crystals and soft hail pellets collide. The light crystals tend to become positively charged and are carried to the top; the heavier pellets gather negative charge lower down. When the voltage reaches hundreds of millions of volts, a spark leaps across.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Top: positive (ice crystals)", "Middle: negative (soft hail)", "Base: small positive patch", "Ground: induced charge"]}
### Thunder and distance
A bolt heats the air around it to about 30,000 °C, five times hotter than the Sun's surface, in a few millionths of a second. The air expands explosively: that is thunder.
ex: Flash, then count seconds to thunder | Sound travels about 1 km in 3 s | 9 s ≈ 3 km away
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~45", "l": "lightning flashes per second, worldwide"}, {"v": "30,000 °C", "l": "temperature of a bolt"}]}
!! Lightning never strikes the same place twice => Tall objects are hit again and again; the Empire State Building gets struck about 20 times a year
> A thunderstorm is a giant engine that turns rising humid air into falling rain and an enormous electrical charge.

# Sea breezes
On a sunny morning the land warms quickly while the sea barely changes. Warm air over the land rises, pressure near the ground drops, and cooler, denser sea air slides in to replace it. By early afternoon the breeze can reach 20 km/h or more and push tens of kilometres inland.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Sun heats the land", "Warm air rises", "Low pressure over land", "Cool sea air flows in"]}
### Turning around at night
After sunset the land cools faster than the sea. The pattern reverses and a weaker **land breeze** blows offshore, often strongest just before dawn.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Day: sea breeze", "items": ["land warmer than sea", "wind blows onshore"]}, "r": {"t": "Night: land breeze", "items": ["sea warmer than land", "wind blows offshore"]}}
### The Nortada
In summer, the Azores High sits to the west while heat builds a low-pressure area over inland Iberia. Between them, a strong north wind blows down Portugal's west coast, boosted each afternoon by the sea-breeze effect. It's why Viana do Castelo's Cabedelo beach is a favourite for windsurfers and kitesurfers.
• The Nortada pushes surface water away from the coast, and cold deep water wells up to replace it
• That **upwelling** keeps Portugal's Atlantic beaches chilly, often 16–19 °C even in August
• It also feeds rich fisheries, including the sardine
!? Cool upwelled water often chills the air above it into coastal fog on summer mornings, while it's hot and sunny just a few kilometres inland.
> A sea breeze is a small-scale weather system: land and sea heat differently, pressure follows, and the wind does the rest.

# Forecasting
In 1922 Lewis Fry Richardson tried to forecast the weather by solving the physics equations by hand. A six-hour forecast took him weeks and came out badly wrong. In 1950 the ENIAC computer produced the first successful numerical forecasts. Since then, computing power and observations have grown enormously.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Millions of observations", "Best estimate of now", "Physics steps forward", "Forecast maps"]}
### How it works today
Satellites, weather balloons, aircraft, ships, buoys and ground stations measure the atmosphere. Supercomputers split the globe into a 3D grid, with cells about 9 km wide in the best global models, and calculate how wind, temperature, pressure and moisture change, minute by simulated minute.
### Chaos and ensembles
In the 1960s Edward Lorenz found that tiny differences in starting conditions grow until forecasts diverge: the **butterfly effect**. So forecasters run the model dozens of times, each with slightly different starts. If the runs agree, confidence is high; if they spread out, the forecast is uncertain.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "days ahead, similar accuracy", "items": [{"l": "1980s", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Today", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}]}
!! "40% chance of rain" means rain over 40% of the area => It means a 40% chance of measurable rain at any given point
!? Since 2023, AI models trained on decades of past weather have matched traditional models for many forecasts. The European Centre (ECMWF), of which Portugal is a member, began running one operationally in 2025.
> Weather can be predicted because it obeys physics, but chaos limits detailed forecasts to roughly two weeks.

# IPMA warnings
IPMA, the Instituto Português do Mar e da Atmosfera, is Portugal's national weather, sea and seismic service. It was created in 2012 by merging the old Meteorological Institute with the fisheries and sea research institute.

### What gets a warning

### Why by district?
The same weather affects places differently. A temperature that is normal in Évora in July may be dangerous in Viana do Castelo, and 50 mm of rain means more on dry ground than in the Minho. So thresholds are set for each district and region, including the Azores and Madeira.

== ph-greats

# Archimedes (c. 287–212 BCE)
Archimedes lived in Syracuse, a Greek city in Sicily, and is often counted among the greatest mathematicians in history. He combined pure geometry with practical engineering in a way few have matched.
### Buoyancy
An object in a fluid is pushed upwards by a force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. This is why steel ships float: their hulls displace a lot of water.
= upthrust = weight of fluid displaced
The famous "Eureka!" story, of him leaping from the bath to test a king's gold crown, was written down by the Roman Vitruvius about 200 years later and is probably embellished.
### The lever
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F₁ × d₁ = F₂ × d₂", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "force on each side"}, {"s": "d", "m": "distance from pivot"}]}
ex: 600 N rock 0.5 m from pivot | Push 3 m from the pivot | F = 600 × 0.5 ÷ 3 = 100 N
### Pinning down π
He drew polygons with 96 sides inside and outside a circle and calculated their perimeters. That trapped π between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7, about 3.1408 and 3.1429.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Archimedes screw", "b": "lifts water uphill"}, {"a": "Buoyancy", "b": "why ships float"}, {"a": "Lever law", "b": "small force, big load"}, {"a": "Area and volume", "b": "sphere vs cylinder"}]}
### Death and rediscovery
He was killed by a Roman soldier when Syracuse fell in 212 BCE. In 1906 a scholar found a lost work of his hidden under a medieval prayer book, written over scraped parchment, now read with X-ray and ultraviolet imaging.
!! Archimedes set Roman ships on fire with mirrors => Ancient writers mention it centuries later; modern tests suggest it would barely work
> Archimedes showed that mathematics could explain and harness the physical world.

# Galileo Galilei (1564–1642)
Born in Pisa, Galileo insisted on testing ideas with careful experiments and measurements, rather than relying on ancient authority. Many call him the father of modern science.
### Motion
Falling objects move too fast to time by eye, so Galileo rolled balls down gentle slopes. He found the distance grows with the square of the time: twice the time, four times the distance. He also realised that, without air resistance, heavy and light objects fall equally fast.
ex: After 1 second: 1 unit of distance | After 2 seconds: 4 units | After 3 seconds: 9 units
### The telescope
In 1609 he heard of a Dutch spyglass and built his own, eventually magnifying about 20 times.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1609", "l": "Builds a telescope"}, {"y": "1610", "l": "Moons of Jupiter"}, {"y": "1610", "l": "Phases of Venus"}, {"y": "1633", "l": "Tried by Inquisition"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "Church admits error"}]}
### Why Venus mattered
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth-centred model", "items": ["Venus always a crescent", "no full phase"]}, "r": {"t": "Sun-centred model", "items": ["full set of phases", "matches Galileo"]}}
Jupiter's moons, now called Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, showed that not everything orbits Earth.
### Trial
His 1632 *Dialogue* argued for a Sun-centred system. In 1633 the Inquisition forced him to recant, and he spent his last years under house arrest near Florence.
!! Galileo dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa => The story comes from a later biographer; he may never have done it
!? In 1971 Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the airless Moon. They hit the ground together, as Galileo predicted.
> Galileo taught science to ask nature directly, through experiments and observation.

# Isaac Newton (1643–1727)
When plague closed Cambridge University in 1665–66, the young Newton went home to his family farm in Lincolnshire. In about 18 months he laid the foundations of calculus, his theory of colour and his ideas on gravity.
### The laws of motion
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["1: No force, no change in motion", "2: Force = mass × acceleration", "3: Every push has an equal push back"]}
### Universal gravitation
The same force that pulls an apple down keeps the Moon in orbit. Newton realised that gravity weakens with the square of distance and acts between every pair of objects.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = G × m₁ × m₂ ÷ r²", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "gravitational pull"}, {"s": "m₁, m₂", "m": "the two masses"}, {"s": "r", "m": "distance between them"}, {"s": "G", "m": "gravitational constant"}]}
### Principia (1687)
His great book, paid for by the astronomer Edmond Halley, explained the planets' orbits, tides and comets with one set of laws. It was the first time the heavens and Earth were shown to obey the same physics.
### Light
With prisms he split sunlight into a rainbow and then recombined it into white. He also built the first practical reflecting telescope, the design most large telescopes use today.
• He spent his later years running the Royal Mint, chasing counterfeiters
• He wrote more about alchemy and theology than physics
• He fought bitter disputes with Hooke and with Leibniz over calculus
!! An apple fell on Newton's head => Newton himself said that seeing an apple fall set him thinking; nothing hit his head
> Newton showed that the same simple laws govern a falling apple and the orbiting Moon.

# Michael Faraday (1791–1867)
The son of a London blacksmith, Faraday left school at 13 and became a bookbinder's apprentice, reading the science books he bound. In 1812 he attended lectures by the chemist Humphry Davy, sent Davy his neat notes, and was hired as his assistant the following year.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1813", "l": "Assistant to Davy"}, {"y": "1821", "l": "First electric motor"}, {"y": "1825", "l": "Christmas Lectures"}, {"y": "1831", "l": "Induction, dynamo"}]}
### Induction
In 1831 he found that a changing magnetic field makes electricity flow in a wire. Move a magnet near a coil and a current appears; stop moving it and the current stops. Every generator, from a bicycle dynamo to the turbines of Portuguese dams and wind farms, works this way.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Magnet moves near coil", "Magnetic field changes", "Current flows in wire", "Electricity generated"]}
### Fields
Faraday had little mathematics, so he thought in pictures. He imagined invisible **lines of force** filling the space around magnets and charges. Others scoffed, but Maxwell later turned these field lines into equations, and fields became central to all modern physics.
### Words and ideas he gave us
• Electrode, anode, cathode and ion, coined with the help of William Whewell
• The **Faraday cage**, which shields its inside from electric fields
• Laws of electrolysis, the basis of electroplating
!? The Royal Institution Christmas Lectures for young people, which Faraday started in 1825, are still held and broadcast every year.
> Faraday discovered how to turn motion into electricity, and with it laid the foundations of the electrical age.

# James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879)
Maxwell, born in Edinburgh, took Faraday's pictures of fields and turned them into precise mathematics. His equations, published in the 1860s and later tidied by Oliver Heaviside into four, describe all electric and magnetic phenomena.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Charges create electric fields", "No lone magnetic poles", "Changing magnetism makes electricity", "Currents make magnetism"]}
### Light is an electromagnetic wave
The equations predicted waves of electric and magnetic fields that keep each other going. When Maxwell calculated their speed, it came out at about 300,000 km/s, the measured speed of light. Light, he concluded, is an electromagnetic wave.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Maxwell's equations", "Predict waves", "Hertz makes them (1887)", "Radio, TV, Wi-Fi"]}
### The whole spectrum
Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all the same kind of wave, differing only in wavelength. Your phone's mobile signal and the light you read by obey the same equations.
### Other achievements
• Made the first colour photograph, of a tartan ribbon, in 1861
• Proved that Saturn's rings must be made of countless small particles
• Helped explain gas temperature as the motion of molecules
!? Many physicists rank Maxwell alongside Newton and Einstein. Richard Feynman thought that, seen from far in the future, his equations would be judged the most significant event of the 19th century.
> Maxwell showed that electricity, magnetism and light are one phenomenon, and predicted the waves that now carry our communications.

# Marie Curie (1867–1934)
Maria Skłodowska grew up in Warsaw, then part of the Russian Empire, where women could not attend university. She moved to Paris in 1891, studied physics and maths at the Sorbonne, and married the physicist Pierre Curie.
### A new kind of energy
In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that uranium gave off mysterious rays. Curie showed the rays came from the atoms themselves, not from any chemical reaction, and coined the word **radioactivity**. That hinted that atoms were not the unchangeable building blocks people had assumed.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1898", "l": "Polonium, radium"}, {"y": "1903", "l": "Nobel Prize, physics"}, {"y": "1911", "l": "Nobel Prize, chemistry"}, {"y": "1914–18", "l": "Mobile X-ray units"}]}
### Tonnes for a speck
Isolating radium meant processing tonnes of uranium ore by hand in a leaky shed.
ex: Several tonnes of pitchblende ore | Years of dissolving and crystallising | About 0.1 g of radium chloride
She named polonium after her occupied homeland, Poland.
### War work
In the First World War she built mobile X-ray cars, nicknamed "petites Curies", and trained women to run them, helping surgeons find bullets and fractures in over a million wounded soldiers.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "2", "l": "Nobel Prizes"}, {"v": "2", "l": "different sciences"}, {"v": "5", "l": "Nobels in the family"}]}
!? Her notebooks are still radioactive and are kept in lead-lined boxes. Her daughter Irène also won a Nobel Prize in chemistry, in 1935.
> Marie Curie discovered that atoms themselves release energy, opening the door to nuclear physics and radiation medicine.

# Albert Einstein (1879–1955)
In 1905 Einstein was 26 and working as a technical assistant at the patent office in Bern, Switzerland. In his spare time he published papers that changed physics.

### General relativity (1915)
Einstein then described gravity not as a pull but as the curving of space and time by mass. A key prediction was that the Sun's gravity would bend starlight by a measurable amount.
### A Portuguese connection
In May 1919 a British expedition led by Arthur Eddington photographed a solar eclipse from the island of Príncipe, then a Portuguese colony, while a second team observed from Sobral in Brazil. The stars near the Sun appeared shifted, as Einstein predicted, and he became world famous overnight.

### Life after fame
Being Jewish and a pacifist, he left Germany when the Nazis took power in 1933 and settled in Princeton, USA. His Nobel Prize was for the photoelectric effect, not relativity.
!! Einstein failed maths at school => He excelled at maths and had mastered calculus by about 15
!? Your phone's GPS works only because satellite clocks are corrected for relativity: without it, positions would drift by about 10 km a day.
> Einstein reshaped our ideas of space, time, light and energy, largely by thinking carefully about simple questions.

# Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg
In 1913 the Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed that electrons can only occupy certain orbits, each with a fixed energy. When an electron jumps down a level, the atom gives out a photon of exactly that energy difference. This explained the sharp colour lines in hydrogen's light.

### The new quantum mechanics
Bohr's model was a stepping stone. In 1925 Werner Heisenberg, aged 23, recovering from hay fever on the island of Heligoland, worked out matrix mechanics: a new mathematics for atoms that used only measurable quantities. A year later Erwin Schrödinger produced an equivalent wave version. In 1927 Heisenberg found the uncertainty principle.
### Arguing with Einstein

Their debates, especially at the Solvay conferences in Brussels in 1927 and 1930, sharpened quantum theory. Experiments since the 1980s, recognised by the 2022 Nobel Prize, have sided with Bohr on the key points.
### Copenhagen
Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, opened in 1921, became the meeting place for a generation of young physicists. Both men won Nobel Prizes, in 1922 and 1932. Their friendship was strained during the Second World War, when Heisenberg led Germany's nuclear research and Bohr had to flee occupied Denmark in 1943.
!? Element 107, bohrium, is named after Bohr. Heisenberg's wartime visit to Bohr in 1941 is the subject of the play *Copenhagen*.
> Bohr and Heisenberg helped build the strange but hugely successful rules that govern atoms.

# Richard Feynman (1918–1988)
Born in New York, Feynman worked as a young man on the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he amused himself by cracking colleagues' safes. He went on to become one of the most original physicists of the 20th century.
### Quantum electrodynamics
QED is the theory of how light and matter interact. Feynman invented simple sketches, **Feynman diagrams**, that turned impossibly long calculations into pictures. He shared the 1965 Nobel Prize with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, who reached the theory by other routes.

### The great teacher
His *Feynman Lectures on Physics*, from courses at Caltech in 1961–63, are still read worldwide and are free online. He believed that if you couldn't explain something simply, you didn't really understand it.
### Challenger
On 28 January 1986 the Space Shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew. On the investigating commission, Feynman made the cause plain on live television.

He squeezed a piece of O-ring rubber in a clamp, dipped it in iced water, and showed it didn't spring back. Engineers had warned about the cold the night before the launch.
!? In a 1959 talk, "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom", he imagined writing whole encyclopaedias on a pinhead, an early vision of nanotechnology.
> Feynman combined deep theory with a gift for making physics vivid and honest.

# Science is a team effort
Newton wrote that he had seen further by "standing on the shoulders of giants". Every famous discovery rests on earlier ideas, careful measurements, new instruments and many people whose names are rarely remembered.
### The Higgs boson
In 1964 several physicists independently predicted a field that gives particles their mass. Finding its particle took nearly 50 years and the largest machine ever built.

A Nobel Prize can go to at most three people, so most contributors go unrecognised. Some were overlooked for other reasons.

### How science checks itself
Results are published, scrutinised by other experts and repeated by independent teams. A claim becomes accepted only when it survives that testing.
!! Science advances through lone geniuses => Even the greatest relied on teachers, colleagues, instrument makers and rivals
> Modern physics is built by communities, each discovery resting on the work of many hands.
