# Shape and size
Mainland Portugal is a tall rectangle on the western edge of Iberia, from Melgaço in the north to Cabo de São Vicente in the south-west. Its Atlantic coast runs for over 900 km, and its land border with Spain, about 1,200 km, is one of the oldest unchanged frontiers in Europe.

### How big is that?
Portugal is about a sixth of Spain's size, a little larger than Hungary and a little smaller than Austria. Driving from Valença to Faro takes about 6 hours by motorway. Of the total area, the mainland accounts for about 89,000 km², the Azores about 2,300 km² and Madeira about 800 km².

### The edge of Europe
**Cabo da Roca**, near Sintra, is the westernmost point of mainland Europe, at about 9.5° W. The Azores island of Flores reaches even further west, about 31° W, making Portugal one of Europe's westernmost countries.

# Mainland and islands
The Azores and Madeira are **autonomous regions**: each has its own elected government and regional parliament, which handle matters like health, education, transport and tourism. Defence, foreign policy and justice stay with Lisbon. Autonomy came with the 1976 Constitution, after the 1974 revolution.

### Nine Azorean islands
• **Eastern group**: São Miguel (the largest, with Ponta Delgada) and Santa Maria
• **Central group**: Terceira, Graciosa, São Jorge, Pico and Faial
• **Western group**: Flores and Corvo
The regional government sits in Ponta Delgada, the parliament in Horta and some departments in Angra do Heroísmo, a balance between the old rival islands.
### Madeira's archipelago
Madeira island holds almost all the people. Porto Santo, 40 km away, has a long golden beach. The Desertas and the Selvagens are uninhabited nature reserves; the Selvagens lie closer to the Canary Islands than to Madeira.

# North and south
The Tejo is a rough natural dividing line. North of it, the land is higher, wetter and rockier, mostly granite and schist. South of it, it's flatter, lower and drier, with rolling plains, cork oaks and wide wheat fields.

### Land and history
The pattern runs deep. In the north, land was divided again and again among heirs, creating tiny plots called **minifúndios** and a dense scatter of villages and parishes. In the Alentejo, land reconquered from the Moors was granted in huge estates, the **latifúndios**, worked by landless labourers. After 1974 these estates were the scene of land occupations and agrarian reform.

### Food follows the land
The north eats caldo verde, cabbage, chestnuts and vinho verde; the south bread soups (açordas), olive oil, pork from acorn-fed black pigs, and robust Alentejo reds. The Algarve's flatter coastal strip grows citrus and almonds and draws most of the tourists.
!? Over half of mainland Portugal's area lies south of the Tejo, but only about a third of its people live there.
> North of the Tejo is green, hilly and crowded; south is flat, dry and spacious.

# Coast and interior
Portugal's population is pulled towards the sea. The coastal strip from Viana do Castelo to Setúbal, with the Lisbon and Porto metropolitan areas, holds the great majority of the people, the industry and the jobs. Inland districts like Bragança, Guarda, Castelo Branco and Portalegre have far fewer people than a century ago.

### Why the interior emptied
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Farming jobs disappear", "Young people leave", "Schools, services close", "Villages age and empty"]}
From the 1960s, people left the interior for Lisbon, Porto and abroad. Today many inland parishes have more deaths than births and more than a third of residents aged 65 or over.
### Fire and abandoned land
When fields are abandoned, scrub and fast-growing trees like eucalyptus and pine take over, and fewer people are around to clear them. This makes summer wildfires larger. The fires of June and October 2017 killed over 100 people and pushed fire prevention and rural policy to the top of the national agenda.

> Portugal leans towards the sea: busy coast, emptying interior.

# Regions of the mainland
Portugal has several overlapping maps. The 18 mainland **districts** date from 1835 and are still used for many purposes, such as car-free civil protection and football associations. The **NUTS II regions** are an EU statistical system, used to share out EU funds. And the old **provinces** live on in everyday speech and identity.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Norte", "Centro", "Grande Lisboa + Península de Setúbal", "Alentejo (incl. Oeste e Vale do Tejo)", "Algarve"]}
### The old provinces
People still say they're from the Minho, Trás-os-Montes, the Beira, the Ribatejo or the Alentejo. These traditional provinces were formally defined in 1936 and abolished as administrative units in 1976, but they still describe landscapes, accents, food and folklore better than any official map.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ways to divide Portugal", "items": ["18 districts (1835)", "308 municipalities", "NUTS II regions (EU)", "Old provinces (identity)"]}
### Municipalities and parishes
The units that run daily life are the **308 municípios** (councils) and their **freguesias** (parishes), around 3,000 of them. Portugal has no elected regional governments on the mainland; a 1998 referendum on creating them was rejected.
!? In 2024 the NUTS II map was redrawn: Lisbon split into Grande Lisboa and the Península de Setúbal, and Oeste and the Tejo valley moved into the Centro and Alentejo regions.
> Districts, NUTS regions and provinces all coexist; the old provinces still define identity.

# The Minho
The Minho, Portugal's north-western corner, sits between the Minho and Douro rivers and faces straight into Atlantic storms. Its mild, wet climate makes it the greenest region in the country, a landscape of maize fields, vines on pergolas, granite walls and hillsides of oak and pine.

### Vinho verde
"Green wine" refers to youth, not colour. It's light, fresh and often slightly sparkling, made from grapes like Alvarinho (especially around Monção and Melgaço) and Loureiro. Vines were traditionally trained high on trees and pergolas so that crops like maize and beans could grow below.
### Culture and tradition
The Minho is known for **espigueiros** (granite granaries on stilts), the gold filigree and traditional costumes of Viana, and the Romaria d'Agonia in August. Ponte de Lima, with its medieval bridge, claims to be Portugal's oldest town, chartered in 1125.

!? Many espigueiros in Soajo and Lindoso, near Peneda-Gerês, are still used today and some are over 200 years old.
> The Minho is Portugal's green, rainy, granite north-west, the land of vinho verde.

# Peneda-Gerês
The **Parque Nacional da Peneda-Gerês** covers about 700 km² in the far north, along the border with Galicia, where it joins Spain's Baixa Limia–Serra do Xurés park. It includes the Peneda, Soajo, Amarela and Gerês mountains, reaching 1,548 m.

### Wild residents
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Garrano horses, native breed", "Iberian wolves", "Roe deer and golden eagles", "Gerês lily, found nowhere else", "Ancient oak and holly forests"]}
The **Garrano** is a small, sturdy native horse that roams semi-wild in the hills. The park is a stronghold for the Iberian wolf, protected in Portugal since 1988, and for rare plants like the Gerês lily.
### People in the park
Unlike many national parks, this one includes inhabited villages. Places like Soajo, Lindoso and Pitões das Júnias keep traditions such as communal grazing and the **brandas and inverneiras** system, where families moved between summer pastures up high and winter homes in the valleys. A Roman road, the Geira, crosses the park with many milestones still standing.
!? The ruined village of Vilarinho da Furna was flooded by a dam in 1972; in dry summers its stone walls reappear from the reservoir.
> Portugal's only national park protects wild granite mountains and the villages that shaped them.

# Rivers and dams
Portugal has over 250 large dams. They store water from wet winters for dry summers, generate electricity and supply irrigation and drinking water. Some also help control floods. Most big hydroelectric dams are on the Douro, Cávado, Tejo and Zêzere.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Winter rain fills reservoir", "Water stored behind dam", "Released through turbines", "Electricity and irrigation"]}
### Alqueva
The **Alqueva** dam on the Guadiana, closed in 2002, created a reservoir of about 250 km², one of the largest artificial lakes in Western Europe, with a shoreline over 1,000 km long. Its water irrigates over 120,000 hectares of the Alentejo, transforming dry farmland into olive groves, almond orchards and vineyards.

### Costs and benefits
Dams flood valleys, villages and farmland, block fish migration and trap sediment that would otherwise feed beaches. The old village of Aldeia da Luz was rebuilt elsewhere for Alqueva. Hydropower is still a big part of Portugal's renewable electricity, varying from about 10% in dry years to 30% in wet ones, and some dams pump water back uphill to store wind and solar energy.

> Dams let Portugal save winter rain for summer, at a cost to rivers and the coast.

# The Azores
The Azores lie about 1,400 km west of Lisbon, roughly a third of the way to North America. They grew from volcanoes along the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**, at the meeting point of three tectonic plates, and they're still geologically active: earthquakes are common and some islands have hot springs and fumaroles.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "São Miguel", "b": "Sete Cidades, Furnas, tea"}, {"a": "Pico", "b": "Portugal's highest peak, vines"}, {"a": "Terceira", "b": "UNESCO town of Angra"}, {"a": "Faial", "b": "Horta marina, Capelinhos"}]}
### Mild, wet and green
The climate is oceanic: mild all year (around 14–24 °C), humid and changeable, often with "four seasons in one day". The high pressure that forms in the region, the **Azores High**, shapes weather across Europe, bringing settled summers to Portugal.
### Volcanic living
• In Furnas, the **cozido das Furnas** stew is cooked for hours in pots buried in hot volcanic ground.
• Pico's vineyards grow inside black lava walls, a UNESCO World Heritage landscape since 2004.
• Gorreana on São Miguel is Europe's oldest tea plantation still working, since 1883.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Whale and dolphin watching", "Crater lakes", "Hot springs in Furnas", "Lava-walled vineyards"]}
!? Former whaling lookouts (vigias) on Pico and Faial now guide whale-watching boats; about a third of the world's whale and dolphin species have been seen in Azorean waters.
> The Azores are green volcanoes in mid-Atlantic, sitting where three plates meet.

# Madeira
Madeira lies about 1,000 km south-west of Lisbon and about 600 km from Africa's coast. It's the top of a huge volcanic shield rising from the ocean floor, so steep that much of the island seems to stand on end. Its peaks reach 1,862 m at Pico Ruivo.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1419", "l": "Zarco reaches Porto Santo"}, {"y": "1450s", "l": "Sugar boom"}, {"y": "1700s", "l": "Wine trade with Britain"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "Laurissilva on UNESCO list"}]}
### The laurel forest
The **Laurissilva** is a forest of laurel-type trees that once covered much of southern Europe millions of years ago. Madeira holds the largest surviving area, about 15,000 hectares, listed by UNESCO in 1999. It catches moisture from clouds and feeds the island's springs.
### Levadas
Most rain falls on the wet north side, while farms and towns cluster on the sunnier south. From the 1500s, Madeirans cut **levadas**, narrow channels, into cliffs to carry water around the mountains. There are over 2,000 km of them, and the maintenance paths beside them are now famous walking trails.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clouds hit north slopes", "Forest catches water", "Levadas carry it south", "Farms and towns use it"]}
### Madeira wine
Madeira wine is fortified and then heated. Merchants found that casks carried through the tropics on ships improved, so producers began warming the wine on purpose. The result can last for well over a century.
!? Madeira's island records include Cristiano Ronaldo, born in Funchal in 1985, whose name is now on the island's airport.
> Madeira is a steep volcanic garden, watered by ancient forest and hand-cut channels.

# The County of Portucale
The name **Portucale** joined two places on the Douro: Portus (a port on the north bank, today's Porto) and Cale (on the south bank, near Gaia). In 868 a nobleman, Vímara Peres, took the area back from Muslim control and it became a county, a territory run by a count for the king of León.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Portus + Cale on Douro", "868: county created", "1096: Henry and Teresa", "1128: Afonso takes over", "1143: a kingdom"]}
### Henry and Teresa
Around 1096 King Alfonso VI of León and Castile gave the county to **Henry of Burgundy**, a French knight who had come to fight the Muslims. Henry married Teresa, Alfonso's daughter (born outside marriage). The county stretched roughly from the Minho to around Coimbra.

### Teresa rules alone
Henry died in 1112. Teresa ruled the county and called herself "queen". She grew close to a powerful Galician noble, Fernão Peres de Trava, which worried Portuguese barons who feared being swallowed by Galicia.

> Portugal began as a frontier county on the Douro, granted by one king to a foreign knight.

# São Mamede, 1128
On 24 June 1128, near Guimarães, the young **Afonso Henriques**, then about 17–20 years old, beat the forces of his mother Teresa and her ally Fernão Peres de Trava. Teresa left for Galicia, where she died two years later.

### Why it mattered
It was a modest battle, but it put power in the hands of a ruler backed by the local nobility. From then on Afonso pushed for independence from León and expansion south against the Muslims.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1112", "l": "Henry dies"}, {"y": "1128", "l": "São Mamede"}, {"y": "1139", "l": "Ourique, uses 'king'"}, {"y": "1143", "l": "Zamora"}]}
### The cradle
Guimarães was the county's main seat. Its castle and the Palace of the Dukes of Braganza sit on the "sacred hill", and a section of the old town wall bears the words "Aqui nasceu Portugal", "Portugal was born here".
• Afonso's exact birth year and place are uncertain; Guimarães, Viseu and Coimbra all claim him
• Tradition says he was baptised in the small church of São Miguel by the castle
!? A later legend says Afonso even chained up his mother; it's a chroniclers' story with little evidence.
> At São Mamede a son beat his mother, and a county started on the road to being a kingdom.

# Recognition: 1143 and 1179
Medieval kingdoms needed recognition. After victories such as **Ourique** (1139), Afonso began calling himself king. In 1143, at Zamora, his cousin Alfonso VII of León, who called himself "emperor of Spain", accepted Afonso's royal title, with a papal envoy present.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1139", "l": "Ourique victory"}, {"y": "1143", "l": "Treaty of Zamora"}, {"y": "1169", "l": "Defeat at Badajoz"}, {"y": "1179", "l": "Pope confirms king"}]}
### Going over León's head
Afonso then declared himself a vassal of the Pope and promised an annual tribute of gold. This was clever: owing loyalty directly to Rome meant not owing it to León. The Popes delayed for decades, but in 1179 Alexander III issued the bull **Manifestis Probatum**, calling Afonso king and his land a kingdom.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Pope: confirms 1179", "Afonso: vassal of Rome", "León: recognises 1143", "Barons: back Afonso"]}
### Why the Pope agreed
Afonso had proved useful: he had taken Lisbon and pushed far south against Muslim rulers, which the Church saw as a crusade. Money and military success made the case.
!? The Treaty of Zamora is traditionally dated 5 October 1143, the same day of the year the Portuguese Republic was proclaimed in 1910.
> Portugal became a kingdom in two steps: a neighbour's nod in 1143 and the Pope's seal in 1179.

# Moving south
Expansion was both conquest and settlement. Kings granted land and charters (forais) to attract settlers, and military orders such as the Templars, Hospitallers and Order of Santiago held frontier castles.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1147", "l": "Santarém and Lisbon"}, {"y": "1165", "l": "Évora taken"}, {"y": "1249", "l": "Faro, Algarve"}, {"y": "1297", "l": "Alcañices border"}]}
### Lisbon, 1147
Santarém fell to a surprise night attack in March 1147. That summer, a fleet of crusaders from England, Flanders and Germany, sailing to the Holy Land, stopped in Porto and agreed to help. After a siege of about 17 weeks, Lisbon surrendered in October. The crusaders were paid in plunder, and some stayed.
### Setbacks
In 1169 Afonso was wounded and captured at Badajoz, and the Almohads fought back hard in the 1190s. It took four more kings to reach the coast of the Algarve: Afonso III took Faro in 1249.

### Fixed borders
In 1297 King Dinis and Castile signed the Treaty of Alcañices. Portugal gained the Riba-Côa area, and the land border has barely moved since, apart from the disputed town of Olivença, taken by Spain in 1801.
!? Portugal's land border with Spain is about 1,200 km long and one of the oldest stable borders in Europe.
> In about 150 years Portugal reached the south coast, and then kept the same shape for more than seven centuries.

# King Dinis
Dom Dinis came to the throne in 1279 at 17. With the reconquest finished, he could focus on building the country. He was nicknamed **o Lavrador**, "the Farmer", and also "the Poet King".
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Dinis's legacy", "items": ["Pine forest of Leiria", "University, 1290", "Portuguese in documents", "Order of Christ, 1319"]}
### Order of Christ
When the Pope dissolved the Templars in 1312, many kings seized their wealth. Dinis instead created a new order, the **Order of Christ**, in 1319, and handed it the Templars' castles and lands. A century later its money would help pay for Henry the Navigator's voyages, and its red cross flew on the sails.
### Land and sea
He set up fairs, protected farmers, rebuilt dozens of castles along the border and hired a Genoese admiral, Manuel Pessanha, in 1317 to organise a navy. The pine forest near Leiria held back moving sand dunes and later supplied timber for ships.

### A poet king
Dinis wrote more than 130 songs (cantigas) in Galician-Portuguese, many of them love songs in a woman's voice. His wife, Isabel of Aragon, became a saint, the Rainha Santa of Coimbra.
!? About 86% of the Pinhal de Leiria burned in the fires of October 2017; replanting is still under way.
> Dinis turned a conquered territory into a working kingdom with its own language, schools and forests.

# The 1383–85 crisis
King Fernando died in October 1383. His only heir was his daughter **Beatriz**, aged about 10 and married to Juan I of Castile. If she inherited, Portugal could become part of Castile. Fernando's widow, Leonor Teles, ruled as regent, influenced by a Galician noble, Count Andeiro.

### Who backed whom
In December 1383 João, Master of the military Order of Avis and Fernando's half-brother born outside marriage, killed Andeiro in the palace. Lisbon's people rose in support. Much of the high nobility, however, sided with Castile.

### Holding on
Castile besieged Lisbon by land and sea in 1384, but plague broke out in its camp and the army withdrew. Meanwhile the young commander Nuno Álvares Pereira won at Atoleiros in the Alentejo. In April 1385 the Cortes in Coimbra chose João as king, after the lawyer João das Regras argued that the throne was vacant.
!? Most of what we know comes from Fernão Lopes, a royal chronicler writing about 50 years later, who gave unusual attention to ordinary townspeople.
> The crisis turned a succession quarrel into the first moment of Portuguese national identity.

# Aljubarrota, 1385
Juan I of Castile invaded with a large army, including heavy French cavalry. João I and Nuno Álvares chose to wait for them on ground they had picked, near Aljubarrota, between Leiria and Alcobaça.

### Defensive tactics
The Portuguese stood on foot on a small plateau, with streams protecting the flanks. In front they dug ditches and hidden pits, and English archers shot from the sides. These were the same ideas the English used against the French at Crécy and Poitiers.

The Castilian army arrived tired, late in the afternoon of 14 August, and attacked a narrow front. Charging knights fell into the ditches and were cut down. Juan I fled to Santarém and then by sea to Seville.

!? Numbers for both armies are estimates from chroniclers; historians agree only that Portugal was heavily outnumbered.
> At Aljubarrota good ground and discipline beat sheer numbers, and Portugal stayed independent.

# Batalha monastery
João I had vowed to build a monastery to the Virgin if he won. Work began in 1386 at a site a few kilometres from the battlefield, and the town that grew around it took the name **Batalha**, simply "battle".

### Highlights

The Chapter House roof is a star vault about 19 m across with no central support. Legend says the architect, Afonso Domingues, now blind, sat under it for three days to prove it would not fall. It now holds the tomb of Portugal's Unknown Soldiers from the First World War, with a guard of honour.
### Unfinished forever
King Duarte began a second burial chapel, but later kings moved their money and attention to the Jerónimos in Lisbon. So the Unfinished Chapels still stand open to the sky, with huge carved Manueline doorways.

# The oldest alliance
Portugal and England first made an alliance in 1373. After Aljubarrota, where English archers had fought, they signed the **Treaty of Windsor** on 9 May 1386, promising "perpetual friendship" and mutual help.

### A royal marriage
In 1387 João I married **Philippa of Lancaster**, daughter of John of Gaunt, in Porto Cathedral. She brought English courtiers and customs. Their children, later called the "Ínclita Geração" (illustrious generation), included King Duarte, Prince Pedro and Prince Henry.
### Using it
The alliance had rough patches, like Britain's 1890 ultimatum over African territory, which caused outrage in Portugal. But it has been invoked many times.

# The Avis dynasty
The House of Avis ruled from 1385 to 1580. With peace with Castile signed in 1411, Portugal's kings, short of land to conquer at home, looked overseas for gold, trade and crusading glory.

# Why Portugal turned to the sea
Portugal had perhaps a million people in the 1400s, poor soils in much of the country and a long land border with a much bigger Castile. Expansion by land was blocked. But it had a long Atlantic coast, skilled fishermen, and Lisbon, a busy port where Genoese and other merchants brought money and know-how.
### The motives
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why sail south?", "items": ["Gold from West Africa", "Spices from Asia", "Christian allies", "Crusade and glory"]}
West African gold reached Europe through Muslim traders across the Sahara. Reaching it by sea would cut out the middlemen. Europeans also hoped to find "Prester John", a legendary Christian king in Africa or Asia, as an ally against Islam.
### Step by step
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1415", "l": "Ceuta captured"}, {"y": "1419", "l": "Madeira reached"}, {"y": "1427", "l": "Azores sighted"}, {"y": "1434", "l": "Cape Bojador passed"}, {"y": "1488", "l": "Cape of Good Hope"}]}
The capture of Ceuta, a North African port, gave Portugal's nobles a taste for overseas ventures. Islands like Madeira soon paid for themselves with wheat and then sugar, funding further voyages.
• Peace with Castile from 1411 freed resources for adventures abroad
• Royal support meant voyages could continue for decades
!? Cape Bojador, on today's Western Sahara coast, was feared as a point of no return. Gil Eanes finally sailed past it in 1434 and found the sea beyond was perfectly ordinary.

# The caravel
The caravel was a small, light ship, typically 20–25 metres long with a crew of about 20. Its **lateen** sails, triangular and set along the length of the ship, came from Mediterranean and Arab sailing traditions.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Square sail", "items": ["great with wind behind", "poor into the wind", "good for cargo ships"]}, "r": {"t": "Lateen sail", "items": ["sails closer to wind", "needs skilled crew", "ideal for exploring"]}}
### The return problem
Sailing south along Africa was easy: the trade winds blew that way. Coming back meant beating against them for weeks. The solution was the **volta do mar** ("turn of the sea"): sail far out into the open Atlantic, curve north to the latitude of the Azores, and ride the westerly winds home.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sail south along Africa", "Head out into Atlantic", "Swing north to Azores", "Westerlies blow you home"]}
### Shallow and nimble
A shallow hull let caravels creep along unknown coasts, enter river mouths and avoid sandbanks. They were less useful for heavy cargo, so later fleets mixed caravels with bigger ships.
• Columbus' Niña and Pinta were caravels
• The "caravela redonda" added square sails for speed downwind
• Da Gama's fleet to India used larger naus plus one caravel, the Bérrio
!! Caravels carried the spice trade => They were exploration ships; the cargo was carried by big naus (carracks) able to hold hundreds of tonnes
> The caravel's genius was not size but agility: it could go into the wind and into the unknown.

# Finding latitude from the stars
Latitude is how far north or south of the equator you are. Because Earth is round, the sky looks different at different latitudes. At the North Pole, the Pole Star is overhead (90°); at the equator it sits on the horizon (0°). In between, its height above the horizon matches your latitude.
### Using the Sun instead
Below the equator, the Pole Star disappears. The Sun is always there, but its noon height changes with the seasons. Portuguese astronomers produced tables of the Sun's **declination**: how far north or south of the equator it is on each day.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "lat = 90° − h + d", "items": [{"s": "lat", "m": "your latitude"}, {"s": "h", "m": "Sun's height at noon"}, {"s": "d", "m": "Sun's declination (table)"}]}
ex: 21 June, Sun at noon is 74.7° high | Table says declination = +23.4° | 90 − 74.7 + 23.4 = 38.7° N, the latitude of Lisbon
### The instruments
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Quadrant", "b": "plumb line marks the angle"}, {"a": "Mariner's astrolabe", "b": "heavy brass ring, sights the Sun"}, {"a": "Cross-staff", "b": "slides to match star height"}]}
Tables printed in the 1490s, including Abraham Zacuto's almanac, published in Leiria in 1496, made the method reliable.
### What they couldn't do
Longitude, your east–west position, needed accurate time at sea. That problem wasn't solved until marine chronometers in the late 1700s. Until then, navigators sailed to the right latitude and then ran east or west along it.
!? In the 1400s the Pole Star was about 3.5° away from the true pole, so pilots learned a rule, the "regimento do Norte", to correct for its position.
> A simple angle, measured with care and corrected with tables, told sailors where they were on an empty ocean.

# The great voyages
Each voyage built on the one before. Pilots, maps and wind knowledge were carefully gathered in Lisbon and often kept secret from rivals.

### Dias and da Gama
Bartolomeu Dias was blown south by a storm and only realised he had passed Africa's tip when he turned north and found the coast on his left. ### Cabral and Magellan
Cabral's fleet of 13 ships landed at Porto Seguro in April 1500, then continued to India. Fernão de Magalhães, after a quarrel with King Manuel I, offered his services to Spain. He found the strait at South America's tip that bears his name, crossed the Pacific, and was killed in the Philippines in 1521. Juan Sebastián Elcano brought the last ship home.

# The spice trade
In medieval Europe, spices were luxuries and status symbols. Pepper came from India's Malabar coast, cinnamon from Sri Lanka, and cloves and nutmeg only from a few tiny islands in today's Indonesia.

# Legacy of the discoveries
The voyages started an exchange of plants, animals, people and germs between continents that had been separate for millennia, often called the **Columbian Exchange**.

# Alfama and the Moorish city
For about 400 years, from the early 700s to 1147, Lisbon was a Muslim city, part of al-Andalus. Its walled town sat on the castle hill and ran down to the river, roughly where Alfama is now. The name probably comes from al-hamma, "hot springs" or "baths", after warm springs that once rose there.
### Layers of history

Alfama is often said to have come through the earthquake better because it stands on firmer ground than the soft river silts of the Baixa. # From Portus Cale to Porto
The hill where Porto Cathedral stands, the Morro da Sé, has been lived on since the Iron Age. Under Roman rule, the road from Lisbon to Braga crossed the Douro here, and a port grew on the river. Later the Suevi and Visigoths held the area, and by the 6th century the town had a bishop.
### A city of merchants and bishops
In 1120 Countess Teresa gave the town to its bishop, and for centuries bishops and citizens argued over who ran it. Porto's wealth came from trade, fishing and shipbuilding, not from a royal court. By tradition, nobles were not even allowed to stay in the city for more than three days.

# The Ribeira
For centuries the Ribeira was Porto's working heart. Boats unloaded at the quay, merchants traded under the arcades of Praça da Ribeira, and families lived above their shops in houses only a few metres wide but four or five storeys tall. Tiles and paint protected the façades from the damp. By the 1970s much of the quarter was run-down and overcrowded, and its restoration since the 1980s helped win UNESCO status.

# Porto's bridges
Before the 19th century the Douro at Porto was crossed by boat and, from 1806, by a bridge of boats. In 1809, during the French invasion, it gave way under crowds fleeing the troops, and many hundreds of people drowned.
### The age of iron
When the railway from Lisbon reached the Douro, engineers had to span a deep gorge. Eiffel's company won with the Maria Pia Bridge (1877), a single wrought-iron arch of 160 m, then the longest of its kind in the world. Its engineer, Théophile Seyrig, later left Eiffel and designed the Dom Luís I Bridge (1886), whose arch spans 172 m.

# Port wine
Yeast turns grape sugar into alcohol. If winemakers add a clear grape spirit (aguardente) of about 77% alcohol when only part of the sugar has fermented, the yeast stops working and the rest of the sugar stays in the wine. The result is sweet, strong and keeps well.
ex: About 4 parts fermenting grape juice | + 1 part grape spirit at 77% | Sweet wine of about 19–22% alcohol
### Why the British loved it
Wars with France in the late 1600s cut England off from French wine. The Methuen Treaty of 1703 lowered duties on Portuguese wine, and British merchants set up firms in Porto and Gaia. Names such as Taylor's, Croft, Graham's and Sandeman still show that history.

### Styles
Ruby ports age briefly and keep a deep red colour and fresh fruit. Tawny ports age for years in small wooden casks, slowly turning amber and nutty. Vintage port comes from a single outstanding year, ages mostly in the bottle and can last for decades. Today the Douro and Port Wine Institute controls quality and labels.

# The Douro valley
The wine valley stretches for about 100 km towards the Spanish border. Mountains to the west, especially the Serra do Marão, block much of the Atlantic rain, so the upper valley has hot, dry summers, often above 40 °C, and cold winters. Vines thrive in the poor, stony schist, which splits into layers that roots can follow deep down to find water.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Atlantic rain", "Blocked by Serra do Marão", "Hot, dry valley", "Vines root in schist"]}
### Built by hand
The slopes are so steep that almost everything was done by hand. Workers broke the schist and built dry-stone walls to make narrow terraces (socalcos). Later terraces were cut by machine into wider earth banks (patamares), and some newer vineyards run straight up and down the slope.

### Crisis and recovery
From the 1860s phylloxera, a tiny insect from America that attacks vine roots, killed most of the vines. Many terraces were abandoned and can still be seen as overgrown "mortórios". Vines were replanted on American rootstocks, which resist the pest.

# Rabelo boats
Before the railway, the Douro was the only practical road out of the wine valley. After the wine had rested through the winter, barrels called pipes, of about 550 litres each, were loaded onto rabelos at riverside quays and carried downstream to the lodges in Gaia.
### A dangerous river
The Douro was then fast and full of rapids and rocks. A crew of a handful of men steered with the long oar from a raised platform at the stern, using the current to go down. Coming back, they sailed with the sea breeze and were towed from the bank. Many boats and lives were lost.

The worst obstacle was the Cachão da Valeira gorge, which blocked boats completely until rocks were blasted away in the 1780s and 1790s, opening the upper Douro.

# Granite and green
Much of northern Portugal sits on granite and schist formed around 300 million years ago, when ancient continents collided to build the supercontinent Pangaea. Molten rock cooled slowly deep underground into granite, and erosion has since brought it to the surface as rounded hills and huge boulders.

### A granite culture
Because granite was everywhere, northern builders used it for almost everything: house walls, steps, window frames, wayside crosses, wine presses, granaries and posts for vines. In Porto, granite door and window frames contrast with façades covered in coloured azulejos.
### Why so green
Wet winds from the Atlantic rise over the coastal mountains and drop their rain. The Peneda-Gerês mountains receive around 3,000 mm in a year, among the highest totals in Europe, while parts of the Alentejo get less than a fifth of that.

# Born from the ocean floor
At the Mid-Atlantic Ridge the sea floor is pulling apart by roughly 2 cm a year, and molten rock wells up to fill the gap. Near the Azores this happens across an unusually broad zone, the Azores plateau, probably because a plume of hot mantle also rises beneath it. The extra melt built a cluster of volcanoes tall enough to break the surface.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plates pull apart", "Hot mantle rises, melts", "Lava piles up on seabed", "Volcano breaks surface"]}
### Hotspot islands
Madeira formed differently. It sits over a hotspot, a long-lived column of hot mantle, while the African plate creeps slowly over it. Each volcano is carried away from the source and goes quiet, and a new one grows above the hotspot. That is why Porto Santo, to the north-east, is much older and more worn down than Madeira.

### Youngest and oldest
• **Santa Maria**, the oldest Azorean island, is the only one with marine fossils and pale sandy beaches
• **Pico**, the youngest, is a near-perfect cone reaching 2,351 m, the highest point in Portugal
• Porto Santo's long golden beach is made of crushed shells and old reef, not volcanic rock
Because the islands are so young, their soils are thin on the newest lava and rich on older ash, and their coasts are mostly steep cliffs.

# Volcanoes still awake
Since the Portuguese arrived, the Azores have seen many eruptions, on land and offshore, and hundreds of felt earthquakes. Madeira, by contrast, has had no eruption in historical times.
### Capelinhos
In September 1957 a volcano began erupting from the sea just off the western tip of Faial. For about 13 months it threw out ash, steam and lava, building a new peninsula of about 2.4 km² and burying nearby villages and the lighthouse in ash. No one died, but farms and homes were ruined.

# Empty islands
Madeira was explored by João Gonçalves Zarco and Tristão Vaz Teixeira around 1419–20, sent by Prince Henry. Its name means "wood", after the dense forest. Settlers arrived from about 1425, and the islands were split into captaincies, each run by a captain on behalf of the crown.

# Sugar, oranges and tea
### Madeira's white gold
Sugar cane thrived on Madeira's warm south side. By around 1500 Madeira was the leading sugar producer of the Atlantic world, worked largely by enslaved people from Africa and the Canaries, and its mills and water channels became a model later copied in São Tomé and Brazil. Brazil's larger, cheaper sugar soon undercut it, and Madeira turned to wine.

### The Azores' crops
Early settlers grew wheat for Portugal's forts in North Africa and woad (pastel), a plant whose blue dye sold well in Flanders. Angra, on Terceira, became a vital stop for fleets returning from India and Brazil. Growers then tried new crops. Pineapples were raised in glasshouses, and tea was planted with the help of experts brought from Macau in 1878.

# An Atlantic climate
The sea changes temperature slowly, so islands far from land rarely get very hot or very cold. In Funchal, winter afternoons are often around 19–20 °C and summer days rarely pass 30 °C. Frost happens only on the highest peaks, where snow falls now and then in winter.

### The Azores High
The Azores lie near a zone of high pressure over the subtropical Atlantic, the Azores High. In summer it usually grows and shelters the islands, giving long settled spells. In winter it often shifts south, and Atlantic depressions sweep through with rain and gales. The Azores therefore get much less sunshine than Madeira, and hydrangeas thrive in the damp.

# Laurel forest survivors
Laurisilva is a humid evergreen forest found in Macaronesia, the island groups of the Azores, Madeira and the Canaries. Each archipelago has its own endemic species, because plants and animals that arrived from the mainland evolved in isolation for millions of years.

### The Azores' lost forest

### The priolo

# From whaling to whale-watching
Whaling came to the Azores with American ships from New England, which stopped in the islands for water, food and crew. Many young Azoreans signed on, and some settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts. In the 1800s islanders started their own shore-based whaling.

### How it worked

# From Latin to Portuguese
The Romans landed in Iberia in 218 BC, during their war with Carthage, and needed about two centuries to conquer the west. The Lusitanians, led by Viriathus, resisted fiercely until he was murdered in 139 BC. Soldiers, traders and settlers brought not the polished Latin of Cicero but the everyday spoken Latin, often called Vulgar Latin.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "218 BC", "l": "Romans land in Iberia"}, {"y": "139 BC", "l": "Viriathus killed"}, {"y": "409", "l": "Suebi enter Iberia"}, {"y": "711", "l": "Moors arrive"}]}
### Regular sound changes
Portuguese is easy to recognise by a few changes that happened again and again:
• An l between vowels disappeared: dolorem became dor, salire became sair
• An n between vowels vanished, often leaving a nasal vowel: luna became lua, manum became mão
• Pl-, cl- and fl- at the start of a word turned into ch-: plenum became cheio, flamma became chama
• The group -ct- became -it-: noctem became noite, lactem became leite

### Germanic traces
After Rome fell, the Germanic Suebi and then the Visigoths ruled the north-west. They soon adopted Latin but left a few dozen words, many of them military, such as guerra (war), guardar (to guard) and roubar (to rob), plus the colour branco (white).

# Galician-Portuguese
The language took shape between the 9th and 12th centuries in the old Kingdom of Galicia, which reached south to the Douro. Formal documents were still written in Latin, but scribes let local words slip in, and by around 1200 whole texts appeared in the spoken language. Besides Afonso II's will, an early example is the Notícia de Torto, a list of grievances from the same years.

• Galicia was ruled from Castile, so Castilian became the language of power and writing there

# Words from Arabic
The Moors held Lisbon until 1147 and the Algarve until 1249. Christians, Muslims and Jews lived side by side, and many Christians under Muslim rule spoke a Romance dialect full of Arabic terms. As the Portuguese kingdom moved south, it took over farms, irrigation channels, markets and workshops, together with the words for them.
### The telltale al-
Arabic al means "the". Portuguese usually borrowed nouns with the article attached, which is why so many Arabic loans start with al- or a-.

# Sounds that puzzle learners
### Nasal vowels
Portuguese has five nasal vowels, made by letting air flow through the nose as well as the mouth, plus nasal diphthongs such as ão, õe and ãe. They are marked by a tilde or by an m or n closing the syllable. The difference changes meaning, so a learner who drops the nasal can say something quite different.

### Vanishing vowels
In European Portuguese, unstressed vowels shrink or disappear. An unstressed e often vanishes completely, so telefone sounds close to "t'lefon'". An unstressed o turns into a u sound, so porto ends in "u". Strings of consonants result, which is why the language can sound clipped to outsiders.

### Tricky letters
• lh and nh sound like Italian gli and gn, as in filho and vinho
• Initial r and rr are a throaty sound for most speakers, but some older people in the north and interior still roll them
• In the Minho and much of the north, v and b often merge, so vinho can sound like "binho"
• X can sound like "sh" (xadrez), "z" (exame), "ks" (táxi) or "s" (próximo)

# Life under the Estado Novo
António de Oliveira Salazar, an economics professor, became finance minister in 1928 and prime minister in 1932. His 1933 constitution created the **Estado Novo** ("New State"), which lasted until 1974, one of Europe's longest dictatorships.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Salazar: unchallenged leader", "Single party: União Nacional", "PIDE, censors, Legion", "Church, Mocidade, family"]}
### How control worked
• Censors with the famous "blue pencil" cut newspapers, books, films and songs
• The PIDE political police used informers, torture and the Tarrafal prison camp in Cape Verde
• Elections were held, but the opposition was harassed and the results managed
• Strikes were illegal; workers and bosses were organised in state "corporations"
### A poor country
Salazar balanced the budget and kept Portugal out of the Second World War, but he distrusted industry and mass education. Most people lived from small farms; many homes had no electricity or running water.

# The 1976 Constitution
The Constituent Assembly elected on 25 April 1975 spent almost a year drafting the text amid the turmoil of the revolution. The result is long and detailed, with about 300 articles, and gives a large place to social rights such as health, education, housing and work.
### What it contains
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Fundamental principles", "Rights and duties", "Economic organisation", "Organisation of political power", "Guarantees and revision"]}
• Freedoms of expression, assembly, religion and the right to strike
• A ban on the death penalty and on life imprisonment
• A national health service that is universal and tends to be free, and free compulsory schooling
• Principles that no revision may remove, such as the republican form of government, universal suffrage and the separation of Church and State
### How it changes
The Assembly of the Republic may revise the Constitution five years after the last revision, or at any time if four-fifths of MPs agree to open the process. Each change needs a two-thirds majority, and the President cannot veto a revision.
ex: 230 MPs | two-thirds needed | at least 154 votes to change the text

> The 1976 Constitution is the rulebook that every law, government and court in Portugal must obey.

# The President
Portugal's system is often called **semi-presidential**: power is shared between a directly elected President and a Government that answers to parliament. In practice the Prime Minister leads day-to-day policy, while the President's power lies in a few strong but rarely used tools and in public influence.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "The President's powers", "items": ["Appoints PM, hears parties", "Vetoes laws, asks the court", "Dissolves the Assembly", "Commands the armed forces"]}
### The veto
When the Assembly sends a law, the President has 20 days to sign it or veto it, or 8 days to ask the Constitutional Court to check it. The Assembly can override a political veto by confirming the law with an absolute majority of MPs, or two-thirds for some kinds of law. A veto of a government decree is final.
ex: 230 MPs | absolute majority = more than half | 116 votes override a veto
### Dissolution
The President can dissolve the Assembly and call early elections, but not in the six months after it was elected, nor in the last six months of the presidential term, nor during a state of emergency. Because it is so drastic, it is nicknamed the "atomic bomb"; it was used three times between 2021 and 2025.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "President", "items": ["head of state", "referee, arbiter", "Belém Palace"]}, "r": {"t": "Prime Minister", "items": ["head of government", "runs policy", "São Bento"]}}
### Elections
Candidates must be Portuguese citizens by origin, aged at least 35, and nominated by 7,500 to 15,000 voters, not by parties. To win in the first round a candidate needs more than half of the valid votes; otherwise the top two meet in a run-off three weeks later.
!? Every President elected since 1976 has gone on to win a second term.
> The President rarely governs but holds the keys: who becomes Prime Minister and when the people vote again.

# The Assembly of the Republic
### What MPs do
• Pass laws on almost every subject, including all laws on rights, elections and crimes
• Approve the State Budget each year, usually in the autumn
• Question ministers and the Prime Minister in regular debates and committees
• Elect, by two-thirds, 10 of the 13 Constitutional Court judges and the Ombudsman

### Where MPs come from
The country is divided into 22 constituencies: the 18 mainland districts, the Azores, Madeira, and two for Portuguese living abroad, one for Europe and one for the rest of the world, each electing two MPs. The other seats are shared according to the number of registered voters, so Lisbon elects 48 MPs and Portalegre just 2.

### Who can sit
Any voter can stand, but only on the list of a party or coalition; independents can appear on those lists. Since 2019, lists must include at least 40% of each sex. The Assembly is led by its own President, the second-highest office of the state after the President of the Republic.

# The d'Hondt method
### How it works
Each list's votes are divided by 1, 2, 3 and so on. The seats go, one at a time, to the largest numbers in the whole table until all are filled. In effect, each new seat goes to the list that would have the most votes per seat after winning it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Count votes per list", "Divide by 1, 2, 3…", "Rank all the quotients", "Largest ones win seats"]}
### Big and small constituencies
There is no legal minimum share of the vote, but constituency size matters a lot. In Lisbon, with 48 seats, a party can win a seat with around 2% of the vote. In a district with only 2 or 3 seats it may need 20% or more. That is why smaller parties win most of their MPs in Lisbon, Porto and Setúbal.

# Government and Prime Minister
After an election, the President meets every party that won seats and then appoints as Prime Minister the person most likely to have parliament's support, usually the leader of the largest party or coalition. The Prime Minister proposes the ministers, whom the President then appoints.
### Facing parliament
Within ten days, the new Government presents its programme to the Assembly. It does not need a vote of approval: it falls only if an absolute majority of MPs, 116, votes a motion of rejection. This rule lets minority governments take office, which has happened often since 1976.

### How a government ends
• A general election and a new Assembly
• The Prime Minister's resignation or death
• A censure motion passed by an absolute majority, or a lost confidence vote
• Dismissal by the President, allowed only to protect the regular functioning of democratic institutions

### How it works inside
The Council of Ministers, all the ministers chaired by the Prime Minister, meets weekly to approve decree-laws and bills to send to parliament. The Prime Minister's official residence is a mansion behind the parliament building in São Bento.
!? Single-party absolute majorities are rare: they happened only in the elections of 1987, 1991, 2005 and 2022.
> In Portugal a government does not need parliament's approval to start, only to avoid a majority voting against it.

# The Constitutional Court
### How judges are chosen
Ten judges are elected by the Assembly with a two-thirds majority, which in practice requires agreement between the largest parties. These ten then co-opt the remaining three. At least six must be career judges from other courts; the rest are legal experts, often law professors.

### How a law reaches the Court
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Before signing: President asks", "After: MPs, PM, Ombudsman ask", "Any court case, on appeal", "Gaps: a law that is missing"]}
If the Court finds a law unconstitutional before it is signed, the President must veto it. Once a rule is struck down with general binding force, it stops applying everywhere. Any ordinary court must also refuse to apply a rule it considers unconstitutional, and the case can then go to the Constitutional Court on appeal.
Between 2012 and 2014, for example, the Court struck down several budget measures, including cuts to public-sector holiday and Christmas pay, ruling that they treated groups unequally.
### Other guardians

> In Portugal no law is safe until it fits the Constitution, and 13 judges have the last word on that.

# Azores and Madeira
Autonomy answered old grievances: islanders had long felt neglected by Lisbon, and in 1975 small independence movements briefly appeared in both archipelagos. The Constitution gave them self-government within the Portuguese state, and their autonomy is among the principles no revision may remove.

### What they control
• Their own laws on matters such as health, education, transport, tourism, fisheries and farming
• Most taxes raised in the region, with the power to lower some rates within limits
• Their own flags, anthems and regional holidays

### The Representative of the Republic
The President appoints a Representative of the Republic for each region, who signs or vetoes regional laws and can send them to the Constitutional Court. The Representative also appoints the President of the Regional Government in light of the regional election results, much as the President of the Republic does with the Prime Minister.
### Why not the mainland?
The Constitution also foresees administrative regions on the mainland, but in a 1998 referendum about 64% voted against creating eight of them. Turnout was under 50%, so the result was not binding, but no regions have been created since. Mainland planning is coordinated by five regional development commissions, the CCDRs.
!? The Azores spread their institutions across islands: the regional parliament sits in Horta, on Faial, while the regional government is based in Ponta Delgada, on São Miguel.
> Autonomy lets the islands run most of their own affairs while remaining fully part of Portugal.

# Municipalities and parishes
### The municipality
Each municipality has two bodies, both elected every four years. The Câmara Municipal is the executive; it is led by its president, the mayor, who is the first name on the most-voted list. The Assembleia Municipal debates and approves the budget and holds the Câmara to account; it also includes the presidents of all the parish councils.

# Elections and voting
### Election day
Voting takes place on a Sunday, usually in schools. The day before is a "day of reflection": campaigning stops, and opinion polls cannot be published until voting ends. Ballot papers list the parties with their names and symbols, and voters mark a cross.

### Who runs elections
The National Elections Commission, the CNE, watches over fairness and equal treatment of candidates, while the Ministry of Internal Administration handles the logistics. ### Referendums

# From EU entry to the euro
### The catch-up years
Between 1986 and 2000, GDP per person rose from just over half to roughly 70% of the then EU average, measured in purchasing power. Foreign investment arrived, such as the Autoeuropa car plant at Palmela, and EU funds paid for infrastructure and training. As markets prepared for the euro, interest rates fell sharply, making borrowing cheap for families, firms and the state.

### Why growth stalled

• Cheap credit flowed into housing, roads and consumption more than into productive firms
• Wages rose faster than productivity, so firms lost competitiveness

# Bailout and recovery
### Why it happened

# Cork: a world leader

# A small country, a huge sea
Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982), a coastal state has full sovereignty over a territorial sea of 12 nautical miles, about 22 km. Beyond it, out to 200 nautical miles, about 370 km, lies the EEZ. There the state controls fish, oil, gas, minerals and energy, but other countries' ships and planes may pass freely.

# Claiming the seabed
Under UNCLOS, the continental shelf means the seabed and what lies beneath it, not the water above. A state automatically has rights over it out to 200 nm. Further out, usually up to 350 nm, it must show with geological and depth data that the sea floor is a natural prolongation of its territory.
### Years of mapping
A special team, the EMEPC (Task Group for the Extension of the Continental Shelf), was set up in 2005 to gather the evidence. Using research ships, sonar and a remotely operated robot called Luso, able to dive to 6,000 m, it surveyed vast areas of the sea floor.

### Under review
The UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf, a panel of scientists, studies claims one at a time, and each review can take many years. Portugal has added updated data since 2009. Some areas overlap with seabed claimed by Spain to the south, near the Selvagens and the Canary Islands, which would have to be settled between the two countries.

# Cold, rich waters
In summer the Azores High to the west and low pressure over hot Iberia make north winds, the nortada, blow along the coast. Because of the Earth's rotation, moving water in the northern hemisphere is deflected to the right, so the surface layer drifts west, away from the shore. Deep water, cold and full of nutrients, wells up to take its place.

### A food factory
Nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates fertilise microscopic algae, the phytoplankton. They feed tiny animals, the zooplankton, which feed sardines, mackerel and anchovies, and in turn bigger fish, dolphins, seabirds and people. Upwelling coasts cover a tiny share of the world's ocean but supply a large share of the world's fish catch.

### Why the south is warmer
The Algarve's south coast faces away from the north winds and is sheltered, so upwelling there is weaker. Its beaches are often several degrees warmer than those near Lisbon or in the Minho. # Nazaré's giant waves
Normally, as waves approach a coast, the shallow continental shelf slows them and drains their energy. At Nazaré, the canyon, well over 150 km long, cuts straight through the shelf to within about a kilometre of the shore, so waves arrive with much of their power intact.
### How the canyon builds waves
Waves travelling over the deep canyon keep their speed, while those crossing the shallow shelf on either side slow down and bend. The two sets meet near the beach and add together into a towering peak, and local currents can steepen them further.

# Ocean science and protection
Ocean science in Portugal has royal roots. King Carlos I, who reigned from 1889 to 1908, was a keen oceanographer who led research cruises off the coast on his yachts, collecting deep-sea fish and studying the sea. ### Life without sunlight
In the 1990s scientists found hydrothermal vents on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge south-west of the Azores, with names like Lucky Strike, Menez Gwen and Rainbow. Bacteria use these chemicals for energy, and shrimps, mussels and crabs live on the bacteria, in a food web that runs without sunlight.

# Laurisilva of Madeira
Twenty million years ago, much of southern Europe was covered by humid evergreen forest, with trees from the laurel family. As the climate cooled and dried during the ice ages, these forests vanished from the continent. They survived only on the Atlantic islands, where the ocean kept the climate mild and moist.

### A forest that drinks clouds
The laurisilva grows on the misty north side of Madeira. Clouds pushed by trade winds drift through the trees, and droplets collect on leaves, moss and lichens and drip to the ground. This "fog capture" adds a lot of water to what falls as rain.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Trade winds bring cloud", "Fog hits the trees", "Droplets drip down", "Soil and springs fill", "Levadas carry the water"]}
Much of that water ends up in Madeira's **levadas**, narrow irrigation channels built from the 1400s onward, which now double as famous walking trails.
### What lives there
• Trees such as til, vinhático and barbusano, all native to Macaronesia
• The Madeira laurel pigeon (trocaz), found only in this forest
• The Madeira firecrest, one of Europe's smallest birds
• Hundreds of endemic insects, snails, mosses and ferns

!? "Madeira" means "wood" in Portuguese. Early settlers cleared much of the forest by fire; the surviving laurisilva is the largest remaining in the world.

# The Tagus estuary
Where the Tejo meets the Atlantic, it spreads into a huge inner bay of about 300 km² before squeezing out through a narrow neck at Lisbon. Twice a day the tide drains away to reveal vast mudflats packed with worms, snails and small crustaceans: a buffet for birds.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["High tide: birds roost", "Tide falls", "Mudflats exposed", "Birds feed", "Tide rises again"]}
### A stop on a great flyway
The estuary lies on the **East Atlantic Flyway**, a bird migration route from the Arctic to West Africa. Some birds stop to refuel; others spend the whole winter here. In a typical winter, tens of thousands of waders use the estuary, with peak counts of all waterbirds above 100,000.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Greater flamingo", "b": "filters shrimp from brine"}, {"a": "Black-tailed godwit", "b": "stops in huge numbers"}, {"a": "Pied avocet", "b": "sweeps mud with its bill"}, {"a": "Dunlin", "b": "small wader, big flocks"}]}
### Why flamingos are pink
Flamingos eat tiny brine shrimp and algae rich in carotenoid pigments, the same family that colours carrots. Their bodies turn those pigments into pink feathers. A flamingo on a poor diet slowly fades to white.
Old salt pans, rice fields and reed beds around Alcochete, Samouco and the EVOA observatory are among the best places to watch. The Tagus Estuary Nature Reserve was created in 1976.
!? The Vasco da Gama Bridge, finished in 1998 and about 12 km long, was designed and built with measures to reduce disturbance to the birds of the estuary below.
> On Lisbon's doorstep, the tide uncovers a feeding ground that keeps tens of thousands of migrating birds alive every winter.

# Dolphins of the Sado
Most bottlenose dolphins roam the open sea. The Sado estuary, between Setúbal and the Tróia peninsula, is different: it has a **resident** group that lives there year-round. Only a few places in Europe have resident estuarine dolphins, such as the Shannon estuary in Ireland.

### A small, fragile family
Scientists have followed these dolphins since the 1980s and can recognise each one by the nicks and scars on its dorsal fin. The group fell from around 40 to the low 20s by the 2000s, and recent births have brought it back to around 30. With so few animals, every calf matters.

### Pressures
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Boat traffic and noise", "Port dredging and shipping", "Pollution from industry", "Inbreeding in a small group"]}
Dolphin-watching boats from Setúbal must follow a code: slow down, keep your distance, never surround the animals or chase them, and limit time spent nearby.

# The Berlengas
About 10 km off Peniche, the Berlengas are a rugged granite archipelago battered by Atlantic swell. The main island, Berlenga Grande, is less than 1.5 km long. The rest are rocky islets called the Estelas and the Farilhões.

### Seabird city

Yellow-legged gulls boomed on scraps from fishing boats and landfills and crowded out other species. Managers have reduced their numbers to give rarer seabirds and plants room. The islands have plants found nowhere else, such as a Berlengas sea-pink, and their own form of wall lizard. Underwater, the meeting of cold, nutrient-rich water and rocky reefs supports kelp, octopus, sea bream and colourful sea slugs, making it a top diving site.

# Cork oaks
Cork is the bark of the cork oak (*Quercus suber*). The tree grows a thick, spongy outer bark to survive fire and drought. Remarkably, it can be stripped without harming the tree, which then grows a new layer.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tree reaches ~25 years", "First stripping: rough cork", "Regrows for 9 years", "Stripped again by hand", "Year painted on trunk"]}
### A slow harvest
Skilled workers strip cork in early summer using axes, when the bark comes away cleanly. A cork oak can live about 200 years, giving around 15 harvests. The first two are too rough for stoppers; good wine corks come from the third stripping onward, when the tree is over 40.
ex: First harvest at 25 years | next at 34, then at 43 | wine-quality cork from about age 43
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of world cork output, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Portugal", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Spain", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Others", "v": 20.0}]}
### Why cork works
Cork is made of tiny, sealed cells filled with air, coated in a waxy substance called suberin. That makes it light, waterproof, elastic and a superb insulator.
### The montado
Cork oaks grow in open woodlands mixed with pasture, called **montado** in Portugal. This system supports cattle, black pigs, honey and a huge range of wildlife, including the Iberian imperial eagle and the Iberian lynx. It is illegal to fell a cork oak in Portugal without official permission.
!? The cork oak was declared Portugal's national tree in 2011.
> Cork is a renewable harvest that keeps the tree alive and sustains one of Europe's richest farm landscapes.

# Forests and fire
Fire is a natural part of Mediterranean ecosystems, but Portugal's fires have grown more destructive. In 2017, more than 500,000 hectares burnt and over 100 people died, including 66 in the Pedrógão Grande fire in June.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Abandoned farmland fills with scrub", "Huge single-species plantations", "Hotter, drier, longer summers", "Fewer goats and less grazing"]}
### Why the landscape changed
As people left rural villages from the 1960s onward, fields and pastures were abandoned and became dense scrub. Meanwhile, eucalyptus plantations for the paper industry spread fast, along with maritime pine. Eucalyptus is now the most common tree in Portugal's forests, covering roughly a quarter of the area.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Eucalyptus, pine", "items": ["oily, resinous leaves", "dense, continuous", "bark strips spread fire"]}, "r": {"t": "Native oaks", "items": ["moister leaves", "shady, cooler floor", "cork protects trunk"]}}
### Breaking the fire chain
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fuel builds up", "Heatwave and wind", "Fire spreads across scrub", "Mosaic and firebreaks slow it"]}
Fires spread fastest across large, continuous blocks of flammable vegetation. A **mosaic** landscape, with farmland, grazing, native woodland and cleared strips around villages, gives fire fewer paths. Portugal now requires landowners to clear vegetation around houses and uses grazing herds and controlled burns in winter to reduce fuel.
!? Burning eucalyptus bark can peel away in long strips that are carried by the wind, starting new fires hundreds of metres ahead of the main front.
> Fire risk is built into the landscape: abandoned land and continuous plantations become fuel when heatwaves arrive.

# Return of the vultures
Vultures are nature's clean-up crew. A group of griffons can strip a dead sheep or deer in under an hour, removing carcasses that could otherwise spread disease.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Animal dies", "Vultures find carcass", "Strip it in an hour", "Disease risk removed"]}
### How they nearly vanished
• Poisoned bait set for wolves and foxes killed many vultures
• Food shortages after 2001, when EU rules required farmers to remove dead livestock during the "mad cow" crisis
• Disturbance near nesting cliffs
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Griffon vulture", "items": ["pale, cliff-nester", "flocks at carcasses"]}, "r": {"t": "Black vulture", "items": ["huge, dark", "nests in tall trees"]}}
### The comeback
Rules were relaxed so farmers could leave carcasses at approved **feeding stations**. Poison was tackled with patrols and specially trained dogs. Nesting areas in the Douro International and Tejo International natural parks were protected.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1970s", "l": "Black vulture lost"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Carcass rules bite"}, {"y": "2010", "l": "Black vultures breed again"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "Several colonies"}]}
The black vulture, Europe's largest bird of prey with a wingspan up to about 3 m, had stopped breeding in Portugal by the 1970s. It returned to breed in 2010 and now has several small colonies near the Spanish border. Griffons have also increased, and Egyptian vultures arrive in the Douro canyons each spring from Africa.

> Give vultures safe food and protect them from poison, and they return quickly to do their essential clean-up work.

# The Atlantic diet
The name was popularised in the 2000s by doctors and nutritionists in Galicia and northern Portugal, to describe how people along the Atlantic edge of Iberia traditionally ate. It is less a set of rules than a portrait of a farming and fishing kitchen.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Mediterranean", "items": ["olive oil at the centre", "pasta and tomatoes", "dry, sunny farming"]}, "r": {"t": "Atlantic", "items": ["fish at the centre", "potatoes, cabbage, beans", "wet, green farming"]}}
### Built around the sea
Portugal eats more fish per person than any other EU country, about 55–60 kg a year, more than twice the EU average. Sardines, horse mackerel (carapau), hake, octopus and cod are everyday food, not luxuries.
### Soup first
A typical meal still starts with soup: caldo verde, made from finely shredded couve galega (a dark leafy cabbage), potatoes and olive oil, or a simple vegetable purée. Bread is on every table, from the dense maize broa of the north to the wheat loaves of the Alentejo.
• Pulses: chickpeas, broad beans and many kinds of bean
• Seasonal vegetables, often from a family plot
• Fruit for dessert, with sweets kept for feast days
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Soup", "Fish or meat, potatoes", "Fruit", "A short black coffee"]}
### What science says
In 2024 a trial in Galicia found that families who followed the Atlantic diet for six months were less likely to develop metabolic syndrome, a cluster of risks for heart disease and diabetes. One study proves little on its own, but it fits a wider pattern: diets rich in fish, vegetables and olive oil tend to be healthy.
!! Portuguese food is simply Mediterranean => Portugal is included in UNESCO's Mediterranean diet listing, but it has no Mediterranean coast, and its cooking leans on the Atlantic
> The Atlantic diet is simple food from the sea and the garden, eaten slowly and together.

# Bacalhau
Cod lives in cold northern seas. Portuguese sailors were fishing the Grand Banks off Newfoundland soon after 1500. Salt cod suited a Catholic country with many meatless fast days and long sea voyages: it was cheap, light and very hard to spoil.
### The White Fleet
Under the Estado Novo, cod fishing became a national campaign to make Portugal self-sufficient. From the 1930s the White Fleet, ships painted white, sailed north each spring for about six months.
• Each fisherman fished alone from a tiny boat called a dóri, with hand lines
• Fog, ice and storms made it one of Europe's most dangerous jobs
• A hospital ship, the Gil Eannes, sailed with the fleet; it is now a museum in Viana do Castelo
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sail north in spring", "Fish alone from a dóri", "Gut and salt on board", "Dry in the sun at home"]}
The fleet faded in the 1970s, as countries extended their fishing zones to 200 nautical miles and cod stocks collapsed. Portugal now imports almost all its cod, much of it from Norway.
### From the sea to the plate
Salt cod must be soaked (demolhado) for one to three days, changing the water often, before it is cooked.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bacalhau à Brás", "b": "shredded, with egg and fries"}, {"a": "À Gomes de Sá", "b": "Porto style, baked, potatoes"}, {"a": "Com natas", "b": "baked in a creamy sauce"}, {"a": "Pataniscas", "b": "fritters in batter"}]}
!? Bacalhau à Brás is said to be named after Brás, a tavern keeper in Lisbon's Bairro Alto, though almost nothing is known about him.
> Bacalhau is a fish from the far north turned into a Portuguese staple by salt, sun and centuries of habit.

# Sardines
The sardine is a small, oily fish that swims in huge shoals along the Iberian coast. In spring it is thin; through the summer it feeds on plankton and builds up fat. Grilled whole over charcoal with coarse salt, it needs nothing else.
### How to eat them
• Lay a hot sardine on bread, often maize broa in the north
• Eat with your fingers, lifting the flesh off the bones
• Add a salad of tomato and roasted peppers, and boiled potatoes

### The canning towns
Canning arrived in the 19th century and boomed during the two world wars, when armies needed food that kept. Setúbal, Matosinhos, Portimão, Olhão and Peniche were lined with factories, where thousands of women cleaned and packed fish by hand on long shifts. Ramirez, founded in 1853, says it is the oldest canning company in the world still working.

### Fewer fish, then more
Sardine numbers rise and fall naturally. In the 2010s stocks off Portugal and Spain fell so low that catches were tightly limited for several years. They have since recovered, but scientists and fishermen still watch the quotas closely.
!? Some fans age tins of sardines for years, like wine, turning them now and then, and say the fish grow softer and richer in the oil.
> A sardine is at its best simply grilled in summer, and at its most lasting packed in olive oil.

# Regional dishes
Regional cooking follows the land. Where there is rain and grass, there are cows and cabbages; where there are cork-oak plains, there are black pigs; along the coast, there is fish.
### North
Porto people are nicknamed tripeiros, "tripe eaters". ### Centre
Bairrada, between Coimbra and Aveiro, is known for leitão: suckling pig roasted whole in wood ovens, with crisp skin and a peppery sauce, eaten with the region's sparkling wine.

# Olive oil
The word azeite comes from the Arabic az-zayt, "the oil". Olive trees have grown here since Roman times or earlier: an olive tree at Mouriscas, near Abrantes, has been estimated by researchers at over 3,000 years old, among the oldest in Europe.

### The Alqueva effect
The Alqueva dam on the Guadiana, completed in 2002, created one of Western Europe's largest artificial lakes and brought irrigation to the dry Alentejo. # Cheese
The thistle is the cardoon, a wild relative of the artichoke. Its dried purple flowers are soaked and crushed, and the liquid, rich in plant enzymes, curdles the milk. This method is rare outside Portugal and Spain.
### How Serra da Estrela is made

It is made from late autumn to spring around the Serra da Estrela, mainland Portugal's highest range, from the milk of local sheep breeds. When ripe, the paste is so soft that the top crust is cut off like a lid and the cheese is scooped out with a spoon. Aged longer, as Serra da Estrela Velho, it becomes firm and sharp.

• Requeijão: a fresh whey cheese, often eaten with pumpkin jam

# Convent sweets
From the 15th century, sugar from Madeira and later Brazil made Portugal one of the first European countries where sugar was fairly plentiful. Convents had eggs from their farms, sugar from the trade, time and skill. They sold sweets to raise money or gave them to benefactors.

### When the convents closed
In 1834, after the Liberal Wars, religious orders for men were abolished and their property sold; convents for women could stay open until their last nun died. Recipes passed to families and bakeries. In 1837 a shop beside the Jerónimos Monastery began selling the custard tarts now known as pastéis de Belém, from a recipe still kept secret.

Only the Belém shop can call its tarts pastéis de Belém; everywhere else they are pastéis de nata. Both have flaky, rolled pastry and a custard of egg yolks, milk and sugar, scorched in a very hot oven and dusted with cinnamon if you like.

# Wine regions
### Vinho Verde
The region in the green north-west, demarcated in 1908, is one of the largest in Europe. Its best-known grape is Alvarinho, grown around Monção and Melgaço on the Minho river. Light, fairly low in alcohol and sometimes slightly fizzy, the wines suit fish and seafood.

# Coffee culture
Coffee became popular in Portugal in the 18th century, and the empire supplied it: Brazil, then São Tomé, Angola and Timor. In the early 1970s Angola was one of the world's largest producers, and its robusta beans shaped the strong Portuguese blend, which usually mixes arabica and robusta.

# Tempura and beyond

== pt-fado

# What fado is
Nobody knows exactly how fado began. Most historians point to Lisbon in the 1820s–40s, in districts such as Alfama, Mouraria and Bairro Alto, among sailors, dockworkers and the taverns where they drank.
### Many roots
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Possible roots of fado", "items": ["Afro-Brazilian lundum dance", "Brazilian modinha songs", "Sailors' and street songs", "Lisbon tavern culture"]}
The lundum and the modinha came from Brazil, especially after the royal court returned from Rio de Janeiro in 1821. The romantic idea that fado descends from Moorish songs is popular but has little evidence behind it.
### Maria Severa
The first legendary fadista was Maria Severa (1820–1846), a singer from Mouraria whose love affair with an aristocrat, the Count of Vimioso, was retold in plays, a novel and Portugal's first sound film, A Severa (1931). By tradition, the black shawl many fadistas wear mourns her.
### From taverns to the world
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1840s", "l": "Maria Severa sings"}, {"y": "1930s", "l": "Radio and fado houses"}, {"y": "1950s", "l": "Amália tours abroad"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "UNESCO listing"}]}
Records and radio carried fado across Portugal in the 20th century, and emigrants took it to Brazil, France and North America. The Fado Museum in Alfama opened in 1998, and UNESCO's listing in November 2011 recognised fado as a living tradition rather than a museum piece.
!! Fado is always sad => Many fados are cheeky, funny or joyful, like the fast fado corrido and songs in praise of Lisbon's neighbourhoods
> Fado is Lisbon's own song, born in its poor quarters and now heard around the world.

# Fado houses
Fado houses took their modern form in the 1930s, when performances moved from streets and taverns into licensed venues. Today Alfama, Mouraria and Bairro Alto have dozens of them, from tiny family taverns to elegant dining rooms. Some are run by fadistas themselves: the singer Argentina Santos ran Parreirinha de Alfama for decades.
### House rules
The phrase you may hear before a song is "silêncio, que se vai cantar o fado": "silence, fado is about to be sung".
• Talking during a song is a serious faux pas
• Doors may be closed and waiters stand still
• Applause comes at the end, never in the middle

### How a night works
A house usually has a resident guitarist and viola player and several singers, who each sing a short set of three or four fados before the next takes over. Late in the evening, staff, cooks and guests who sing may join in. Many singers of today began this way.

> In a fado house the music comes first: dinner pauses, the room goes dark and everyone listens.

# Lisbon and Coimbra
### Lisbon's fixed fados
Traditional Lisbon fado uses a repertoire of a few hundred named melodies. A singer chooses a melody and a poem with the right rhythm and metre, so the same tune can carry hundreds of different lyrics. The three oldest, the fado menor, fado corrido and fado mouraria, are often called the "castiço" (true-born) fados.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fado menor", "b": "slow, minor key, intense"}, {"a": "Fado corrido", "b": "quick, major key, lively"}, {"a": "Fado mouraria", "b": "named after the old quarter"}, {"a": "Fado canção", "b": "a song with a refrain"}]}
### The art of bending a tune
Lisbon singers talk of estilar: stretching, delaying and ornamenting the melody differently every time, so no two performances are alike. The musicians must follow every pause.
### Coimbra's songs
In Coimbra, songs were usually written by named composers and sung closer to the page, like a Romantic art song. They follow the academic year, above all the Queima das Fitas in May, when finishing students burn the ribbons of their faculty colours.

> Lisbon fado reinvents old tunes with new words; Coimbra fado sings composed songs of student life.

# Guitarra and viola
Fado musicians rarely read sheet music. A guitarist must know hundreds of fados by ear and follow a singer who may hold a note, speed up or drop out without warning. Players describe it as a conversation more than an accompaniment.
### Who does what
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Viola sets key and rhythm", "Guitarra plays the intro", "Singer enters", "Guitarra fills the gaps"]}
The guitarra never plays over the words. It answers in the gaps between lines, echoes a phrase or adds a short run before the next verse. Good accompanists make a singer sound freer, not busier.
### Guitar on its own
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "With a singer", "items": ["follows the voice", "short fills", "stays in the shadow"]}, "r": {"t": "Guitarrada", "items": ["guitar leads", "fast virtuoso runs", "viola supports"]}}
### Players to know
• Raul Nery and José Fontes Rocha were mid-20th-century masters, Fontes Rocha long playing for Amália
• António Chainho took the guitarra into jazz and world music
• Marta Pereira da Costa is among the first women to play it professionally on world stages

> Fado is a conversation in three parts: the voice speaks, the guitarra replies, the viola holds the ground.

# Amália Rodrigues
Amália made her professional debut in 1939 at the Retiro da Severa, a Lisbon fado house. Within a few years she was the city's best-paid fadista, touring Brazil and starring in films.
### A singer of poets
Amália chose lyrics by major poets, from Camões to David Mourão-Ferreira, Pedro Homem de Mello and Alexandre O'Neill. From 1962 the French-born composer Alain Oulman wrote melodies for her that broke with the traditional fado forms. Some purists objected; most now see it as a turning point.

### Fame abroad
The French film Les Amants du Tage (1955) featured her singing, and in 1956 she played the Olympia in Paris. She went on to tour Europe, the Americas, the Soviet Union and Japan, singing in several languages.
### Politics and memory
Because she was famous under the dictatorship, some accused her after 1974 of being the regime's singer. Later accounts say she quietly helped opponents of the regime, and historians still debate her politics.

# Fado and censorship
After the military coup of 1926, the new authorities brought in prior censorship of shows, and from 1927 fado lyrics had to be submitted for approval. Over time, rough tavern songs about crime, prostitution and injustice faded, replaced by songs about love, Lisbon and the nation.
### Licensed and controlled
• Professional fadistas needed a carteira profissional, an official card
• Performances moved into registered houses with set programmes
• Radio, and later television, spread a polished, approved style

### "Fado, Fátima, football"
Critics summed up the regime's culture as the "three Fs": fado, Fátima and football, supposedly used to keep people content. Historians see this as a simplification. Some officials disliked fado as gloomy and low-class, and the shrine and the sport had lives of their own.
### After the revolution
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1927", "l": "Lyrics need approval"}, {"y": "1974", "l": "Censorship ends"}, {"y": "Late 70s", "l": "Fado seen as old-regime"}, {"y": "1990s", "l": "New generation revives it"}]}

# Cante alentejano
Cante belongs to everyday life as much as to the stage. Men gathered in taverns after work and began to sing, one voice at a time, until the whole room joined in. In mining towns such as Aljustrel, singing together was a way of holding a hard-working community together.
### Passed on by ear

### Saving a tradition
By the late 20th century, as villages emptied and farm work was mechanised, cante seemed to be ageing with its singers. Towns in the region prepared the UNESCO bid, and the listing brought new attention and pride.
• More women's and mixed choirs have formed
• Schools in the region have run cante classes
• Pop, jazz and fado musicians have recorded with cante groups

# Grândola, Vila Morena

# Portuguese guitar
The Portuguese guitar descends from the European cittern, a pear-shaped instrument with metal strings. In the 18th century the so-called "English guitar", popular with Porto's British merchant community, became fashionable, and Portuguese makers adapted it. By the late 19th century it had become the voice of fado.
### Why it shimmers
• **12 steel strings in 6 pairs** (courses): each pair is plucked together, so notes ring with a slight chorus
• The lower pairs are tuned an octave apart, adding a bright overtone
• It's played with the thumb and index finger, often with picks called **unhas** ("nails"), in a fast technique called dedilho
• A fan of screw tuners on the head replaces the usual wooden pegs

# Coimbra fado
Coimbra has one of Europe's oldest universities: founded in 1290 and settled in Coimbra for good in 1537. Its students developed their own song tradition in the 19th century, and Coimbra fado is still tied to student life, sung by men in the academic dress of black cape and suit (capa e batina).

### The serenade
The classic setting is a night serenade: a singer and guitarists under a window, or on the steps of the Old Cathedral (Sé Velha). The songs are often addressed to a sweetheart, to the city, or to the student years that will soon end.
### No clapping, please
At a serenade, the audience traditionally doesn't applaud. Instead, people show approval by clearing their throats or coughing softly. Students sometimes wave or flick the edges of their capes.

# Calçada portuguesa
Portuguese pavement is made of small, roughly cubic stones, about 4–6 cm across, set by hand into sand and tapped down with a hammer. White limestone and black basalt are arranged into patterns, from simple waves to ships, stars and coats of arms.

### How it started
The modern tradition began in 1842 at Lisbon's São Jorge Castle, where military engineer Eusébio Furtado had prisoners pave a parade ground in a zigzag. Around 1848–49, Rossio square received its famous wavy design, called **Mar Largo** ("wide sea").

Portuguese workers took the craft to Brazil. Copacabana's beach promenade in Rio copied Rossio's waves, but turned them to run parallel to the sea. # Literature beyond Camões
Camões gave Portugal its epic in 1572, but the country's literature kept renewing itself. Four writers show its range: a realist novelist, a modernist poet of many selves, a poet of the sea and light, and a Nobel novelist.

# Cante alentejano
Cante is the traditional group singing of the Alentejo, the vast plain of southern Portugal. It's sung without instruments, slowly, by groups of about ten to thirty singers standing in rows. UNESCO added it to its list of intangible heritage in November 2014.
### How a song unfolds
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ponto: solo opening", "Alto: high voice joins", "Choir answers", "Alto soars above"]}
The **ponto** starts the song alone. The **alto** then takes over the line, singing higher, and the whole choir enters in harmony. The alto's voice floats above the group, decorating the melody.
### Songs of work and land
Cante grew among farm workers, miners and villages. Songs talk about the land, the wheat fields, the heat, love, longing and hard labour. Singers often link arms and sway with a slow step as they sing, as if walking together.
• Men's groups dominated for most of the 20th century; women's and mixed groups have grown since
• Its origins are debated: church chant, Arab music and older folk singing have all been suggested
• Towns like Serpa, Beja and Cuba hold regular festivals

# Romanesque churches

# Alcobaça
The Cistercians, a reforming order shaped by Bernard of Clairvaux, preached poverty, work and plain buildings without figures or colour. At Alcobaça they ran huge estates, draining marshes and planting orchards, and the monastery became one of the richest in the kingdom.
### A church of light and stone
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Ribbed vault, ~20 m high", "Tall, plain columns", "Aisles almost as high", "Long nave, ~106 m"]}
Alcobaça is a hall church: its side aisles rise almost as high as the central nave, so light spreads evenly instead of falling in shafts. The effect is calm and very tall.
### Pedro and Inês
Inês de Castro was a Galician noblewoman loved by the heir to the throne, Pedro. His father, Afonso IV, feared her family's influence, and in 1355 she was killed by royal courtiers. As king, Pedro took revenge on two of the killers. Their carved Gothic tombs face each other, so that, tradition says, they will see each other first when they rise on Judgement Day.

# Batalha
Work started in 1386 and went on in phases into the early 16th century, under several master builders, first Afonso Domingues and then a master called Huguet. The style blends local Gothic with touches of English Perpendicular, perhaps a nod to the queen, Philippa of Lancaster.

# Calçada portuguesa
### The stones
Lisbon's white limestone traditionally comes from quarries in the limestone hills of Estremadura, north of the city, and the black basalt from the ancient volcanic rocks of the Lisbon area. Elsewhere local stone is used, such as granite in the north, with red or pink stones for colour.
### A craft of the knees
A calceteiro works kneeling, sets each stone by eye and taps it into the sand with a small hammer, using string lines for levels and templates for patterns. Joints are filled with sand and the surface is compacted. A good pavement can last for decades, and single stones are easy to replace.

# Science for the sea
Longitude, a ship's east–west position, could not be measured reliably at sea until the 18th century. Latitude was easier, because the height of a star above the horizon changes as you sail north or south. Portuguese pilots began using this from about the 1450s–1460s, as voyages pushed down the coast of Africa.
### From star to Sun
In the north, the Pole Star sits almost above the North Pole, so its height in degrees roughly equals your latitude. But it sinks towards the horizon near the equator and vanishes south of it. Pilots therefore switched to the Sun at midday, which needed a table of its changing daily position, called its declination.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Measure noon Sun height", "Look up declination", "Apply the rule", "Latitude found"]}
ex: Noon Sun 60° high, due south | Declination that day 10° north | Latitude = 90° − 60° + 10° = 40° N, near Aveiro
### Tools of the trade
• The **quadrant**, a quarter circle with a plumb line, measured the Pole Star
• The **mariner's astrolabe**, a heavy open bronze wheel, resisted the wind on a rolling deck
• The **cross-staff** (balestilha) let a pilot sight the horizon and a star at once
The rules were printed in the "Regiment of the Astrolabe and Quadrant", whose oldest surviving copy dates from about 1509. Its Sun tables drew on Abraão Zacuto, a Jewish astronomer whose Almanach Perpetuum was printed in Leiria in 1496.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~1460", "l": "Pole Star by quadrant"}, {"y": "1485", "l": "Sun method tested"}, {"y": "1496", "l": "Zacuto's tables printed"}, {"y": "~1509", "l": "Navigation manual"}]}
!? An astrolabe from the Esmeralda, a ship of Vasco da Gama's second voyage that sank off Oman in 1503, is thought to be the oldest mariner's astrolabe ever found.
> Portuguese navigation turned astronomy into a routine any trained pilot could follow with a table and an instrument.

# Pedro Nunes' nonius
An astrolabe or quadrant usually showed whole degrees. Dividing them further made the marks too crowded to read. Nunes described his solution in De crepusculis, a 1542 book on the length of twilight, as a tool for astronomers and pilots alike.
### How the nonius worked
Inside the main quarter circle he drew dozens of smaller concentric arcs. The outer one was split into 90 equal parts, the next into 89, the next into 88, and so on. When the sighting arm fell between marks on one arc, it would land almost exactly on a mark of another. Knowing which arc and which mark, a short calculation gave the angle to a fraction of a degree.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Arm crosses many arcs", "Find the arc with a hit", "Note arc and mark number", "Calculate exact angle"]}
### From nonius to vernier
Engraving dozens of arcs accurately was hard, and the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe found it impractical. In 1631 the French engineer Pierre Vernier replaced the arcs with a single small sliding scale. The principle is the same: compare two scales with slightly different spacing.
ex: Main scale marked in mm | 10 vernier marks span 9 mm, so each is 0.9 mm | The mark that lines up gives tenths: 0.1 mm precision
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Main scale", "b": "1 mm divisions"}, {"a": "Vernier scale", "b": "10 marks over 9 mm"}, {"a": "Difference", "b": "0.1 mm per mark"}, {"a": "Reading", "b": "find the line that matches"}]}
!? In German and Portuguese the sliding scale on a calliper is still called a Nonius or nónio, after Nunes' Latin name, Petrus Nonius.
> Two scales with slightly different spacing reveal what neither shows alone: that was Nunes' gift to every workshop.

# Garcia de Orta
Orta studied medicine at Salamanca and Alcalá in Spain, taught briefly in Lisbon and sailed to India in 1534 as a ship's doctor. He settled in Goa, treated viceroys and ordinary patients, and grew many of the plants he wrote about in his own garden. He also held a lease on land on the island of Bombay, today's Mumbai.
### A book of conversations
His "Colloquies on the Simples and Drugs of India" is a series of conversations between Orta and an imaginary visiting doctor, Ruano. The visitor quotes the ancient Greek and Arab authorities; Orta often replies that experience shows otherwise. It was one of the first books printed in India with European type.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Pepper, ginger, cinnamon", "Nutmeg, cloves, camphor", "Bhang (cannabis) and opium", "Early account of cholera"]}
### Across Europe
In 1567 the Flemish botanist Carolus Clusius published a Latin summary, which was reprinted and translated many times. For about a century it was a standard European reference on Asian medicines.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old authorities", "items": ["Greek and Arab texts", "repeated, not tested"]}, "r": {"t": "Orta's method", "items": ["observe and taste", "ask local healers"]}}
### A tragic ending
Orta came from a family of New Christians, Jews forced to convert. After his death in 1568 the Inquisition, newly active in Goa, pursued his family; his sister Catarina was burned in 1569. In 1580 his own remains were dug up and burned.

# Gusmão's flying machine
Gusmão was born in 1685 in Santos, in Portuguese Brazil, and studied at Coimbra. In 1709 he petitioned the king for exclusive rights to a machine for travelling through the air. The court was intrigued, and he was asked to demonstrate.
### The demonstration
Accounts describe several trials at the royal palace in early August 1709. In the best known, a small balloon of thick paper, with a flame burning in a bowl beneath it, rose a few metres before servants knocked it down for fear it would set the curtains alight.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Flame heats the air", "Hot air expands", "Lighter than air around it", "Balloon rises"]}
### The Passarola
A famous drawing shows a bird-like ship with wings, a sail and a canopy. Historians think the drawing was at least partly fanciful, perhaps even meant to mislead rivals, rather than a working design. No reliable record says it flew.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "The Passarola flew over Lisbon", "r": "Only a small hot-air model rose"}]}

# Egas Moniz: Nobel and debate
Born António Caetano de Abreu Freire in 1874, he took the name Egas Moniz from a medieval nobleman. Before medicine made him famous he was a member of parliament, ambassador to Spain and briefly foreign minister, and in 1919 he led Portugal's delegation at the Paris Peace Conference.

• "Improvement" often meant calmer and easier to manage

# Carnaval
Carnaval is the last party before the 40 days of Lent, traditionally a time of fasting. Its spirit is the world turned upside down: masks, men dressed as women, mockery of the powerful and, in villages, battles with flour, water or eggs.
### The caretos of Podence

### Satire in Torres Vedras
Torres Vedras calls its carnival, held since 1923, "the most Portuguese in Portugal". It avoids samba; instead, giant figures and floats mock politicians and celebrities, and groups of men dressed as women, the matrafonas, fill the streets.

### The 2016 final
At the Stade de France, Ronaldo was injured early and left the pitch in tears. Fernando Santos's team held firm, and in the 109th minute the substitute Éder scored with a low shot from outside the area. Ronaldo spent extra time urging on his team-mates from the touchline.
### The Nations League
UEFA created the Nations League in 2018 to replace many friendlies with competitive matches. Portugal hosted the first finals in 2019 and beat the Netherlands 1–0 in Porto, with a goal by Gonçalo Guedes. In June 2025, under Roberto Martínez, they beat Germany and then Spain, drawing 2–2 in the final and winning 5–3 on penalties.

# Roller hockey
Teams have four outfield players and a goalkeeper on a rink about 40 m by 20 m, enclosed by a low barrier. The ball is hard and fast, so goalkeepers wear heavy pads. Fouls can bring a blue card, which sends a player off for a short time, as well as direct free hits and penalties.

### A golden age
Portugal won its first world title in 1947, in Lisbon, and dominated the following years, winning almost every championship until the early 1960s. Matches were broadcast on radio and drew huge crowds, and for decades roller hockey was second only to football in popularity.

# Big waves at Nazaré
Nazaré was known for its fishing boats and its fisherwomen in seven skirts until 2011, when the Hawaiian surfer Garrett McNamara rode a wave of about 24 m there. The images went round the world, and the town became a pilgrimage site for big-wave surfers.

Measuring a moving wave is hard: judges study photos and video to estimate the height from trough to crest. The Brazilian Maya Gabeira holds the women's record and the German Sebastian Steudtner the overall record, both set at Nazaré in 2020.
### How tow-in works
Waves this big move too fast to paddle into. A partner on a jet-ski tows the surfer onto the wave, then races back to pull them out of the impact zone. Spotters on the cliff, rescue teams and inflatable vests are essential.

# Marathon heroes
Carlos Lopes, born in 1947 near Viseu, was a track and cross-country runner long before he was a marathoner. He won 10,000 m silver at the 1976 Olympics and the World Cross Country Championships in 1976, 1983 and 1984.
### Los Angeles 1984
Lopes broke away in the final kilometres and won in 2 hours 9 minutes 21 seconds, an Olympic record that stood for 24 years. In Rotterdam in 1985 he ran 2:07:12, the first marathon under 2 hours 8 minutes.

### Rosa Mota
Rosa Mota, from Porto, won the first women's marathon at a European Championship, in Athens in 1982. She went on to win three European titles, the 1987 world title and Olympic bronze in 1984 before her gold in 1988, and won the Boston Marathon three times.

Porto's domed pavilion in the Crystal Palace gardens carries her name, and the city's road races now draw thousands of amateur runners.

# Olympic champions
Fernanda Ribeiro, from Penafiel, was world champion at 10,000 m in 1995. In Atlanta she followed China's Wang Junxia until the final lap, then overtook her to win in an Olympic record time. She added a bronze in Sydney in 2000.
### Jumping for gold
The triple jump is a hop, a step and a jump from one run-up. Nelson Évora, born in Côte d'Ivoire to Cape Verdean parents and raised in Portugal, won in Beijing in 2008. Pedro Pichardo, born in Cuba and Portuguese since 2017, won in Tokyo in 2021 with 17.98 m, then took silver in Paris.

### Gold on the track
In Paris in 2024 Iúri Leitão and Rui Oliveira won the madison, a 50 km relay race in the velodrome in which partners take turns, handing over with a hand-sling. Leitão also took silver in the omnium, becoming the first Portuguese to win two medals at one Games.

# The Volta a Portugal
The first Volta, in 1927, ran on roads that were mostly unpaved, and riders often had to fix their own bikes. It is one of the oldest national stage races still running, older than Spain's Vuelta.
### The climbs
The race is often decided in the mountains. The steep ascent of Senhora da Graça, near Mondim de Basto, and the long climb to Torre in the Serra da Estrela, almost 2,000 m up, are legendary. Summer heat makes them harder still.

# Sailing and the sea
In London in 1948 the brothers Duarte and Fernando Bello won silver in the two-person Swallow class, Portugal's first Olympic sailing medal. In Atlanta in 1996 Hugo Rocha and Nuno Barreto took bronze in the 470 dinghy.
### Big events
• Cascais hosted a round of the America's Cup World Series in 2011
• Lisbon was a stopover of the Volvo Ocean Race in 2012 and 2018
• The Tall Ships Races have visited Lisbon several times, filling the Tagus with sails

### The Sagres
The navy's training ship, the NRP Sagres, a three-masted barque with red crosses on its sails, is a floating symbol of Portugal. Built in Germany in 1937, it has sailed for Portugal since 1962 and has gone around the world several times.
### Learning to sail
Many coastal towns and river clubs run sailing schools for children, usually starting in the small Optimist dinghy. The windsurfer João Rodrigues competed at seven Olympic Games in a row, from 1992 to 2016.
