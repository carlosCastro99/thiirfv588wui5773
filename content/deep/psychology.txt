== ps-biases

# Two systems
Kahneman, a psychologist who won the 2002 Nobel Prize in economics, popularised the labels in his 2011 book *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. The terms "System 1" and "System 2" were first coined by Keith Stanovich and Richard West in 2000. They are a handy metaphor for two styles of thinking, not two separate parts of the brain.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "System 1", "items": ["fast, automatic", "effortless", "always on"]}, "r": {"t": "System 2", "items": ["slow, deliberate", "tiring", "easily distracted"]}}
### A quick test
Try this before reading on. A bat and a ball cost €1.10 together. The bat costs €1 more than the ball. How much is the ball?
ex: Intuition says 10 cents | Check: bat would be €1.10, total €1.20, wrong | Ball = 5 cents, bat = €1.05, total €1.10
Shane Frederick used this puzzle in his 2005 Cognitive Reflection Test. Around half of students at top American universities gave the fast, wrong answer. They weren't stupid; System 1 offered an answer that felt right, and System 2 didn't bother to check.
### Who does what
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "2 + 2 = ?", "b": "System 1 answers instantly"}, {"a": "17 × 24 = ?", "b": "needs System 2 effort"}, {"a": "Reading a face", "b": "System 1, automatic"}, {"a": "Filling in a tax form", "b": "System 2, slow and careful"}]}
System 1 is not the villain. It lets you drive a familiar road, read emotions and catch a falling glass. Experts build excellent intuitions through practice. Problems come when a situation is new, statistical or designed to mislead, and the fast answer goes unchecked.
!! Your brain has a logical half and an emotional half => The two systems are a model of thinking styles; real brains mix both everywhere
!? Kahneman later admitted that a chapter of his book leaned on priming studies that have since failed to replicate. The two-systems idea survives; some of its famous examples do not.
> System 1 suggests, System 2 is supposed to check, and most errors happen when the check is skipped.

# Confirmation bias
Confirmation bias works at every step: what we search for, how we interpret it and what we remember later. It feels like thinking, which is what makes it so hard to spot.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Hold a belief", "Seek agreeing evidence", "Explain away the rest", "Belief feels stronger"]}
### The classic experiments
In 1960 Peter Wason gave people the sequence 2, 4, 6 and asked them to find the hidden rule. Most guessed "even numbers rising by 2" and tested only sequences that fitted, like 8, 10, 12. The real rule was simply "any increasing numbers". People rarely tried a sequence that could prove them wrong.
In 1979 Charles Lord and colleagues showed supporters and opponents of the death penalty the same two studies, one for and one against. Each side rated the study that agreed with them as better designed and found flaws in the other.
### Where you meet it
• Search engines: typing "is coffee bad for you" finds different pages from "benefits of coffee"
• Social media feeds that learn what you like and show you more of it
• Doctors anchoring on a first diagnosis and reading new symptoms to fit it
• Investors who only follow news that supports the shares they already own
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Search: why I'm right", "r": "Search: best case against me"}, {"w": "Trust agreeable sources", "r": "Rate sources before reading"}]}
!! Correcting someone always makes them believe the myth more => The "backfire effect" mostly failed to replicate; corrections usually help a little
> Ask what evidence would prove you wrong, then go and look for it.

# Anchoring
Anchoring is one of the most reliable findings in psychology. It shows up in shops, salary talks, courtrooms and property prices, and it works even on experts who know about it.
### The wheel of fortune
In a 1974 study by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, people watched a wheel that was rigged to stop at 10 or 65. They were then asked what percentage of United Nations members were African countries.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "median estimate, % of UN members", "items": [{"l": "Wheel showed 10", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Wheel showed 65", "v": 45.0, "hi": true}]}
A number everyone knew was random still moved the answers by 20 points. People adjust away from the anchor, but not far enough.
### Why it works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hear a number", "Use it as a start", "Adjust a little", "Stop too early"]}
A second reason is that the anchor makes us think of reasons it might be right. Asked "is this house worth €400,000?", you start noticing the good kitchen and the view.
### Real-world anchors
• Price tags with a crossed-out "was" price
• The first offer in a negotiation, which often sets the range for the deal
• Asking prices for flats in Lisbon or Porto, which shape what buyers think is fair
• Sentencing demands: German judges' sentences were swayed by numbers from dice in a 2006 study
!? Large replication projects (Many Labs, 2014) confirmed that anchoring is strong. Effects of truly incidental anchors, like the last digits of an ID number, are smaller and less consistent.
> Write down your own number before you hear anyone else's.

# Availability heuristic
A heuristic is a mental shortcut. The availability heuristic swaps a hard question ("how common is this?") for an easy one ("how quickly can I think of an example?"). Usually that works, because common things come to mind easily. It fails when memory is skewed by vivid, dramatic or recent events.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Vivid news story", "Easy to recall", "Feels common", "Risk overestimated"]}
### Flying versus driving
Air crashes are rare but get global coverage. Car crashes are common but rarely reported unless local. In the EU, roughly 20,000 people die on the roads each year, about 600 of them in Portugal.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "deaths per billion passenger-km (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Car", "v": 3.0, "t": "~3", "hi": true}, {"l": "Bus", "v": 0.4, "t": "~0.4"}, {"l": "Plane", "v": 0.05, "t": "<0.1"}]}
After the 11 September 2001 attacks, many Americans drove instead of flying. Gerd Gigerenzer estimated this led to about 1,600 extra road deaths in the following year.
### The letter K test
Tversky and Kahneman asked whether more English words start with K or have K as their third letter. Most said "start with", because those are easier to search for in memory. In fact, words with K in third place are more common.
### Everyday effects
• People buy flood insurance just after a flood, then let it lapse
• A friend's burglary makes your area feel unsafe
• Shark attacks feel likely after a summer of headlines
!! If I can think of many examples, it must be common => Ease of recall reflects media and memory, not frequency
> When judging risk, look for the base rate, not the headline.

# Sunk cost fallacy
A sunk cost is money, time or effort already spent that you can't get back. Logically, only future costs and benefits should guide a decision. Emotionally, walking away feels like admitting a waste.
### The theatre experiment
In 1985 Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer gave some theatre subscribers random discounts on season tickets. Those who paid full price went to more plays in the first half of the season. The money was gone either way, but paying more made people feel they had to "get their money's worth".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sunk cost thinking", "items": ["I've paid already", "Can't waste it", "Keep going"]}, "r": {"t": "Forward thinking", "items": ["Money is gone", "Is the rest worth it?", "Stay or leave"]}}
### From cinema seats to supersonic jets
Britain and France kept funding the Concorde long after it was clear it would never pay back. Economists sometimes call this the **Concorde fallacy**. The same pattern appears in failing IT projects, renovations that spiral, and wars that continue "so the dead didn't die in vain".
ex: You paid €12 for a film | After 30 minutes it's dull | The €12 is gone whether you stay or leave | The only question: is the next 90 minutes worth more than leaving?
### Why we fall for it
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["We hate admitting a waste", "We want to look consistent", "Loss aversion: quitting = loss", "We overrate past effort"]}
There are fair reasons to persevere: a reputation for finishing things, or a project close to paying off. The fallacy is only when the past spending itself is the reason.
!? Seasoned investors have a saying for this: "don't throw good money after bad".
> Ask: if I hadn't spent anything yet, would I start this today?

# Loss aversion
Loss aversion is the heart of **prospect theory**, published by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. It says we judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, usually what we have now, and losses weigh more.
ex: Coin flip: heads you win €150, tails you lose €100 | Expected value = +€25 per flip | Most people still refuse | They need a win of about €200 to accept
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "loss pain ≈ λ × gain joy", "items": [{"s": "λ", "m": "loss aversion ratio"}, {"s": "≈ 2", "m": "typical estimate"}, {"s": "1", "m": "no loss aversion"}]}
### The endowment effect
In a 1990 study, students were given university mugs. Owners asked about twice as much to sell them as other students would pay to buy them. Simply owning something turns giving it up into a loss.
### Where it shapes behaviour
• Investors hold losing shares too long, hoping to get back to even
• Free trials work: cancelling feels like losing something you have
• "Avoid a €5 surcharge" motivates more than "get a €5 discount"
• Football coaches play safe when ahead and gamble when behind
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Facing a gain", "items": ["play it safe", "lock it in"]}, "r": {"t": "Facing a loss", "items": ["take big risks", "chase break-even"]}}
!! Loss aversion is a fixed law of human nature => It varies; for small, routine sums it can be weak, and researchers still debate its size
> The same €50 counts twice: once as money, and again as a loss or a gain.

# The Dunning–Kruger effect
In 1999 David Dunning and Justin Kruger tested students on logic, grammar and humour. Those in the bottom quarter scored around the 12th percentile but rated themselves around the 62nd. Top scorers slightly underrated themselves.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "percentile, bottom-quarter scorers", "items": [{"l": "Actual score", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Self-estimate", "v": 62.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it might happen
Their explanation was a "double burden": the skills you need to do a task well are the same skills you need to judge whether you did it well. A beginner at grammar can't spot their own grammar errors.
### The debate
The effect is more contested than its fame suggests. Part of the pattern appears even with random data, because almost everyone rates themselves "above average" and extreme scores drift towards the middle when measured again. Studies that correct for this still find a modest effect, but much smaller.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Fools are most confident", "r": "Low scorers overrate a bit more"}, {"w": "Curve: peak, dip, rise", "r": "Internet meme, not in the paper"}]}
The famous curve with "Mount Stupid" and a "valley of despair" was never in the original paper. It is a popular cartoon of the idea. Confidence does often wobble while you learn a skill, but that's an observation about learning, not what Dunning and Kruger measured.
!! Dunning–Kruger proves that stupid people think they're geniuses => Almost everyone overrates themselves a little; beginners just have more room to be wrong
> Feedback from reality is the cure, because you can't judge a skill you don't yet have.

# Survivorship bias
Survivorship bias happens when we only see the cases that made it through some filter and treat them as the whole picture. The failures are missing, so the lessons we draw are wrong.
### The bomber planes
In the Second World War, the US military studied bombers returning from missions and mapped the bullet holes. The obvious plan was to add armour where the holes were. Statistician Abraham Wald pointed out the opposite: the planes hit in other places, such as the engines, never came back.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "What they saw", "items": ["holes in wings", "holes in tail"]}, "r": {"t": "What was missing", "items": ["planes hit in engines", "they didn't return"]}}
### More everyday cases
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Old buildings", "b": "only the sturdy ones still stand"}, {"a": "Investment funds", "b": "failed funds vanish from data"}, {"a": "Famous dropouts", "b": "failed dropouts aren't on TV"}, {"a": "Success books", "b": "habits failures shared too"}]}
"They don't build them like they used to" is often survivorship bias. The badly built houses from 1900 were demolished long ago; only the best remain.
### The founder myth
Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg left university, but most successful founders didn't. A 2018 study of US start-ups found that the average age of founders of the fastest-growing firms was about 45, and most had degrees and years of experience.
!? Funds that close are often dropped from databases, so the average fund looks better than the funds investors actually held. Analysts must correct for this to get honest figures.
> Before learning from winners, ask where the losers went.

# The framing effect
A frame is the way a choice is presented. Logically, two descriptions of the same fact should lead to the same decision. In practice, frames that stress losses push people towards risk, and frames that stress gains push them towards safety.
### The disease problem
In a famous 1981 study, Tversky and Kahneman described a disease expected to kill 600 people. People chose between a sure option and a gamble, described either in lives saved or lives lost.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% choosing the sure option", "items": [{"l": "\"200 will be saved\"", "v": 72.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "\"400 will die\"", "v": 22.0}]}
The two sure options were identical. Only the wording changed, and the majority flipped.
### Framing all around you
• "75% lean" mince sells better than "25% fat"
• "Save €200 a year" versus "lose €200 a year by not insulating"
• A 1982 study found doctors were also swayed by survival versus death framing
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "90% survive", "r": "90 live, 10 die of 100"}, {"w": "25% fat", "r": "75 g lean, 25 g fat"}]}
### Defaults are frames too
Portugal is one of several European countries with an opt-out system for organ donation since 1993: you are a donor unless you register to refuse. Countries with opt-out defaults tend to have far more registered donors than opt-in ones, although actual donation rates also depend on hospitals and families.
!? Showing both frames side by side, or using natural numbers ("10 out of 100"), greatly reduces the framing effect.
> Before deciding, restate the choice the other way round and see if you still agree.

# Beating your biases
Knowing about biases helps less than you'd hope. Emily Pronin found in 2002 that people readily spot biases in others but see themselves as objective, a **bias blind spot**. The trick is not willpower but changing the process you use for decisions that matter.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice it matters", "Slow down", "Seek the opposite view", "Decide with a rule"]}
### Tools that work
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Consider the opposite", "b": "list reasons you're wrong"}, {"a": "Pre-mortem", "b": "imagine it failed: why?"}, {"a": "Base rates", "b": "how do similar cases end?"}, {"a": "Cooling-off", "b": "sleep on big purchases"}]}
The **pre-mortem**, proposed by psychologist Gary Klein, asks a team to imagine the project failed a year from now and explain why. It makes it safe to voice doubts.
### Build guardrails
• Set your budget or price limit before you look at offers, so the anchor is yours
• Use checklists, like pilots and surgeons do
• Ask a friend to argue the other side
• Keep a decision diary: write down what you expect, then check later
The EU gives you 14 days to cancel most online purchases. That's a built-in pause for System 2 to catch up with an impulse buy.
!? In forecasting tournaments run by Philip Tetlock, one hour of training in simple techniques like base rates improved accuracy by around 10%.
> You can't remove biases from your head, but you can design decisions so they matter less.

== ps-memory

# Three stages
The three-stage model goes back to Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin in 1968. It's a simplification, but it helps you diagnose why something was forgotten: was it never taken in, did it fade, or is it there but out of reach?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Encoding: take it in", "Storage: keep it", "Retrieval: get it back"]}
### Encoding failures
You have seen thousands of €1 coins, but could you draw one accurately? In a 1979 study, Americans were asked to pick the correct penny from 15 drawings and fewer than half managed it. They had never encoded the details, because they never needed to.
How deeply you process something matters. Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart showed in 1972 that thinking about a word's meaning leads to much better memory than noticing its letters or sound.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Deep: what does it mean?", "Middle: how does it sound?", "Shallow: how does it look?"]}
### Retrieval failures
The tip-of-the-tongue feeling proves a memory can be stored but temporarily unreachable. You might know the first letter and the number of syllables, yet the word won't come. It often pops up later, once the right cue appears.
• Cues help: the smell of a school corridor can bring back a whole year
• Retrieval practice makes paths easier to find next time
• Stress and tiredness make retrieval harder, which is why exam blanks happen
!! Forgotten memories are erased => Many are still stored; what's lost is the route back to them
> To remember something, make sure it gets in deeply and practise getting it out.

# Short-term vs long-term
In 1956 George Miller wrote a famous paper about "the magical number seven, plus or minus two". Later research by Nelson Cowan suggested the true limit is closer to about four meaningful chunks.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Working memory", "items": ["about 4 chunks", "seconds", "easily disrupted"]}, "r": {"t": "Long-term memory", "items": ["no known limit", "years to a lifetime", "needs consolidation"]}}
### A workbench, not a box
Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch proposed in 1974 that short-term memory is really a **working memory**: a mental workbench where you hold and juggle information.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Working memory", "items": ["Central executive", "Phonological loop", "Visuospatial sketchpad", "Episodic buffer"]}
The phonological loop is the inner voice repeating a phone number. The sketchpad holds images and routes. The central executive directs attention. That's why it's hard to do mental maths while someone talks to you: both compete for the same limited space.
### The patient H.M.
In 1953 Henry Molaison had parts of both hippocampi removed to stop severe epilepsy. His short-term memory still worked; he could chat normally. But he could no longer form new long-term memories of facts and events. Yet he improved at a mirror-drawing task over days, without remembering practising. He showed that memory is not one single system.
### Long-term memory lasts
Harry Bahrick tested people's Spanish 50 years after school. Much was lost in the first few years, but what survived then stayed stable for decades. He called it "permastore".
!? Without rehearsal, information in short-term memory fades within about 20 seconds.
> Working memory is a tiny desk; long-term memory is a vast library, and learning is the job of moving things from one to the other.

# The forgetting curve
Ebbinghaus was a German psychologist who used himself as the subject. He memorised lists of meaningless syllables like "DAX" or "BUP" so prior knowledge couldn't help. Then he measured how much time he saved relearning them later.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% retained (Ebbinghaus savings)", "items": [{"l": "20 minutes", "v": 58.0}, {"l": "1 hour", "v": 44.0}, {"l": "1 day", "v": 34.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "6 days", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "1 month", "v": 21.0}]}
### What the curve shows
Forgetting is steepest at the start and then slows. Most of the loss happens in the first hours. What remains after a month tends to stay for a long time.
In 2015 Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros repeated the experiment and got a very similar curve, so this is one of psychology's oldest results that has held up.
### Why reviews flatten it
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Learn", "Start to forget", "Review", "Forget more slowly"]}
Each successful review makes the memory more stable, so the next decline is slower. After several spaced reviews, a fact can last months or years.
### Caveats
• Nonsense syllables are the worst case; meaningful material fades more slowly
• Things linked to what you already know are forgotten far less
• Skills like cycling or swimming barely follow the curve at all
!! If you forget something, you never really learnt it => Forgetting is normal; a quick relearn is much faster than the first time
> Expect to forget fast, and plan reviews for it.

# Spaced repetition
The **spacing effect** is one of the most replicated results in learning research, confirmed in hundreds of studies since Ebbinghaus. The same total study time works much better spread out than crammed together.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Day 1", "l": "Learn it"}, {"y": "Day 2", "l": "First review"}, {"y": "Day 5", "l": "Second review"}, {"y": "Day 12", "l": "Third review"}, {"y": "Day 40", "l": "Fourth review"}]}
### Why waiting helps
When you review just as a memory begins to fade, the effort of retrieving it strengthens it more than an easy review would. Cramming feels productive because everything is fresh, but that fluency fades within days.
### How big should the gaps be?
A large 2008 study by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues found that the best gap depends on how long you want to remember. For a test in a week, a gap of about a day worked well; for remembering a year later, gaps of about three to four weeks.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Remember 1 week", "b": "review gap about 1 day"}, {"a": "Remember 1 month", "b": "gap about 1 week"}, {"a": "Remember 1 year", "b": "gap about 3–4 weeks"}]}
### From card boxes to apps
• 1972: Sebastian Leitner's system of flashcards in boxes; correct cards move to a box reviewed less often
• 1987: Piotr Woźniak's SuperMemo computes intervals for each card
• Today: apps like Anki and this one schedule reviews automatically
!? The spacing effect also works for babies, older adults and even animals; it seems to be a basic property of how memory works.
> Little and often beats a lot at once, as long as the gaps keep growing.

# Active recall
Retrieval doesn't just measure memory, it changes it. Each time you pull something out, the route to it gets stronger. Psychologists call this the **testing effect**.
### The key study
In 2006 Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students learn a passage. Some reread it four times; others read it once and then tried to recall it three times.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% recalled one week later", "items": [{"l": "Reread 4 times", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Read once, test 3×", "v": 61.0, "hi": true}]}
Five minutes after studying, the rereaders did slightly better. A week later the result reversed. Rereading creates a feeling of familiarity, which is easily mistaken for knowing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rereading", "items": ["feels easy", "feels like learning", "fades fast"]}, "r": {"t": "Self-testing", "items": ["feels hard", "shows gaps", "lasts"]}}
### Ways to do it
• Close the book and write down everything you remember
• Use flashcards with the question on the front
• Explain the idea out loud as if teaching a friend
• Try practice questions before you feel ready
### Mistakes are useful
Getting an answer wrong and then seeing the correct one still helps, as long as you get feedback. Even guessing before you study a topic can make the later lesson stick better.
!! Testing is just for measuring what you know => Testing is one of the best ways to learn it in the first place
> If it feels effortful to recall, the effort is the learning.

# Chunking
A chunk is a group of items that your memory treats as a single unit. Because working memory holds only about four chunks, making each chunk bigger lets you hold far more.
ex: 9 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 = 9 separate digits | 912 345 678 = 3 chunks | "FBI CIA EU" = 3 chunks, though it's 8 letters
### Chess masters
In 1973 William Chase and Herbert Simon showed chess positions for five seconds. Masters could rebuild real game positions almost perfectly, while beginners placed only a handful of pieces. With random positions, the masters' advantage almost vanished.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "pieces recalled after 5 s (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Master, real game", "v": 16.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Novice, real game", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Master, random", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Novice, random", "v": 3.0}]}
Masters didn't have better memories; they had thousands of familiar patterns stored in long-term memory, so each pattern became one chunk.
### Expertise is chunking
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Expert: whole patterns", "Learner: words and phrases", "Beginner: single letters"]}
A runner known as S.F. trained in 1980 to raise his digit span from about 7 to about 80. He did it by turning digits into running times he knew, such as "3:49.2, near a mile record". His span for letters stayed at about 7.
### Chunking in daily life
• Your IBAN is printed in blocks of four
• Musicians read chords, not single notes
• Learning common phrases in a language beats learning single words
!? The same trick works for skills: practised movements become one chunk, so a driver changes gear without thinking about each step.
> Memory isn't about holding more things, it's about making each thing bigger.

# Sleep and memory
Sleep isn't downtime for memory. During the night the brain sorts, strengthens and links what you learnt during the day, a process called **consolidation**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Day: hippocampus records", "Deep sleep: replay", "Cortex stores it", "Next day: stable memory"]}
### Replay in the night
In 1994 Matthew Wilson and Bruce McNaughton recorded neurons in rats running a maze. During later sleep, the same cells fired again in the same order, as if the rats were rehearsing the route. Similar replay has since been found in humans.
### Different sleep stages, different jobs
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Deep (slow-wave) sleep", "b": "facts and events"}, {"a": "Sleep spindles", "b": "moving memories to cortex"}, {"a": "REM sleep", "b": "emotions, skills, links"}]}
Most deep sleep comes early in the night, and most REM sleep comes towards morning. Cutting sleep short at either end costs you something.
### Before and after learning
• Sleep after learning protects new memories from interference
• Sleep before learning matters too: a tired brain encodes new information less well
• Even a short afternoon nap can improve recall of material learnt that morning
• A 1924 study found people forgot less over 8 hours of sleep than 8 hours awake
!! You can catch up on sleep before an exam at the weekend => Missed consolidation on the nights after learning isn't fully recovered later
> The night after learning is part of the lesson.

# False memories
Remembering is not like replaying a video. Each time you recall an event, your brain rebuilds it from fragments, filling gaps with expectations, later information and imagination. The rebuilt version is then stored again.
### Car crash experiment
In 1974 Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people films of car accidents and asked how fast the cars were going. Only one verb in the question changed.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average speed estimate, km/h", "items": [{"l": "\"contacted\"", "v": 51.0}, {"l": "\"hit\"", "v": 55.0}, {"l": "\"smashed\"", "v": 65.0, "hi": true}]}
A week later, about a third of the "smashed" group remembered seeing broken glass. There was none.
### Planting whole events
In the 1990s Loftus and colleagues convinced about a quarter of adults that as children they had been lost in a shopping centre. Other studies planted memories of hot-air balloon rides, using doctored photos.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Recall event", "Rebuild from pieces", "Add new details", "Store new version"]}
### Why it matters
• Eyewitness mistakes played a role in about 70% of US convictions later overturned by DNA evidence
• Police lineups in many countries now follow rules to avoid suggestive questions
• Vivid "flashbulb" memories, like where you were for a shocking event, change over the years while confidence stays high
!! A vivid, confident memory must be accurate => Detail and confidence can grow even as accuracy falls
> Memory is a reconstruction, not a recording, so treat even your own with some humility.

# The memory palace
The **method of loci** (Latin for "places") is said to come from Simonides of Ceos in ancient Greece. According to Cicero's retelling, a banquet hall collapsed and Simonides identified the victims by remembering where each had sat. Roman orators used the technique to deliver long speeches without notes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pick a familiar place", "Fix a route through it", "Put a vivid image at each spot", "Walk it to recall"]}
### Why it works
It uses two things the brain is very good at: remembering places and remembering strange images. A plain list has no structure, but a route provides ready-made order and cues.
ex: Shopping list: eggs, olive oil, bread | Front door: eggs cracking on the doormat | Hallway: slipping on a pool of olive oil | Kitchen: a loaf of bread singing fado
### Evidence
In 2017 Martin Dresler and colleagues trained ordinary people in the method for 30 minutes a day over six weeks. Their recall of a 72-word list rose from about 26 words to about 62. Brain scans showed their brain connections becoming more like those of memory champions.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "words recalled out of 72", "items": [{"l": "Before training", "v": 26.0}, {"l": "After 6 weeks", "v": 62.0, "hi": true}]}
### Limits
• It's best for ordered lists, names, numbers and speeches
• It doesn't create understanding, so use it alongside learning the meaning
• You can reuse a palace, but old images can interfere with new ones
!? Memory champions generally don't have unusual brains; studies find that their skill comes from training techniques like this one.
> Your brain remembers places and pictures far better than lists, so turn lists into places.

# A study plan that works
In 2013 John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten popular study techniques. Only two were rated "high utility": practice testing and spreading study over time. Highlighting, rereading and summarising were rated low, even though they're what most students do.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "High utility", "items": ["practice testing", "spaced study"]}, "r": {"t": "Low utility", "items": ["highlighting", "rereading"]}}
### Put it together
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Short session", "Test yourself", "Sleep on it", "Review when due"]}
• Keep sessions short, 10–30 minutes, with a clear goal
• Start each session by recalling last time's material before looking at notes
• Mix topics and problem types instead of doing all of one kind (**interleaving**)
• Stop well before bedtime and protect your sleep
ex: Plan A: 120 minutes once a week | Plan B: 10 minutes a day = 70 minutes a week | B uses less time | B gives 7 retrievals and 7 nights of sleep in between
### Why the small daily habit wins
Daily practice turns each session into a spaced review. Every return to the material is a test of memory, and the gaps grow naturally as you move on. A long weekly session mostly helps in the first hour, then you're rereading what's already fresh.
!! Feeling confident after a long study session means you've learnt it => Fluency right after study fades fast; delayed recall is the real test
> Short, spaced, self-tested and slept on: that's how learning lasts.

== ps-motivation

# The habit loop
The cue–routine–reward loop was popularised by journalist Charles Duhigg in 2012. It draws on brain research such as Ann Graybiel's work at MIT, where rats learning a maze showed busy brain activity throughout at first, and later only at the start and the end, as the route became automatic.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cue: time, place, mood", "Routine: the behaviour", "Reward: a good feeling", "Craving builds"]}
### What a habit really is
To psychologists, a habit is a behaviour triggered automatically by a context, without a conscious decision. Wendy Wood estimated that about 40% of what people do each day is done in the same place almost daily, often while thinking about something else.
Once a habit is strong, the reward matters less. People who always buy popcorn at the cinema ate almost as much stale popcorn as fresh, in a 2011 study by Wood and David Neal. The cue alone was running the show.
### How long does it take?
In 2009 Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London asked 96 volunteers to pick a new daily habit, such as a glass of water after breakfast.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "66 days", "l": "median to become automatic"}, {"v": "18–254", "l": "range of days, by person"}]}
Missing a single day did not reset progress. Simple habits formed faster than demanding ones like a daily gym session.
### Breaking a habit
• Spot the cue: time, place, emotion, people or the previous action
• Remove or avoid the cue where possible
• Keep the cue but swap in a different routine with a similar reward
• Use life changes: moving house or starting a job loosens old habits
!! It takes 21 days to form a habit => That number has no scientific basis; it's usually weeks to months
> Habits live in contexts, so change the context and you change the habit.

# Make it easy
Behaviour often follows the path of least resistance. Every extra step, click or metre of distance is **friction**, and small amounts of friction add up to big changes in what people actually do.
### Defaults are powerful
In a 2001 study of an American company, automatically enrolling new workers in the pension scheme (with an option to leave) raised participation from about 37% to about 86%. The UK's automatic pension enrolment from 2012 produced a similar jump.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of new staff in pension plan", "items": [{"l": "Opt in", "v": 37.0}, {"l": "Opt out (default)", "v": 86.0, "hi": true}]}
### Add friction to bad habits, remove it from good ones
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Make it easier", "items": ["gym bag by the door", "fruit at eye level", "water bottle on desk"]}, "r": {"t": "Make it harder", "items": ["log out of apps", "no snacks at home", "TV remote in a drawer"]}}
### The research
• A 2019 Cochrane review found that putting foods closer or more available increases how much is chosen, though the evidence quality is modest
• Smaller portions and plates reduce how much people eat, according to the same review group
• Moving the stairs into view and the lift around a corner increases stair use
!? Several famous studies on food and friction by Cornell researcher Brian Wansink were retracted from 2017 onwards after serious data problems. The general idea still has support, but treat his specific numbers with caution.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice the habit", "Count the steps", "Remove or add one", "Watch what changes"]}
> Don't rely on willpower; rearrange the room so the easy choice is the good one.

# Tiny habits
Stanford researcher BJ Fogg popularised this approach in his 2019 book *Tiny Habits*. His model says a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt come together at the same moment.
= Behaviour = Motivation × Ability × Prompt
### Why tiny works
Motivation goes up and down. On a bad day you won't do 30 minutes of exercise, but you will do one push-up. Making the habit tiny means it survives low-motivation days, and consistency is what builds automaticity.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "effort needed (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "1 push-up", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "10-min walk", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "1-hour gym", "v": 9.0}]}
### Growing it
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["One push-up daily", "Feels automatic", "Add a few more", "Habit grows naturally"]}
• Start with something that takes under two minutes
• Celebrate straight away, even with a quiet "yes!", to give a reward
• Grow it only when it feels effortless
• On hard days, fall back to the tiny version instead of skipping
### Identity
Writer James Clear argues that each repetition is like a vote for the kind of person you are. A runner is someone who runs, even if only for five minutes. Small wins also feed motivation: Teresa Amabile's research on workers' diaries found that making progress in meaningful work was the biggest everyday boost to mood and drive.
!! Big goals need big changes from day one => Big changes usually collapse; small steps that stick grow into big ones
> Make it so small you can't say no, then let it grow.

# Habit stacking
Psychologists call this kind of plan an **implementation intention**, a term from Peter Gollwitzer in the 1990s. Instead of a vague goal ("I'll exercise more"), you decide exactly when, where and how.
= After I [current habit], I will [new habit].
### The evidence
A 2006 review of 94 studies by Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that "if–then" plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. In a 2011 study at a US company, simply asking staff to write down the date and time they'd get a flu jab raised vaccination rates by about 4 percentage points.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Vague goal", "items": ["I'll read more", "no trigger", "easy to forget"]}, "r": {"t": "If–then plan", "items": ["after dinner, 1 page", "clear cue", "runs on autopilot"]}}
### Why it works
The existing habit is already automatic and happens at a fixed time and place. Linking to it hands the decision to the environment. You no longer need to remember; the coffee cup reminds you.
### Good anchors
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "After I brush my teeth", "b": "I'll floss one tooth"}, {"a": "After I pour coffee", "b": "I'll write three lines"}, {"a": "After I park the car", "b": "I'll take the stairs"}, {"a": "After I sit on the train", "b": "I'll review my cards"}]}
• Choose an anchor that happens every day, at the same point
• Match the location: the new habit should fit where you already are
• Keep the new habit tiny at first
• Chain carefully; long stacks break at the weakest link
!? If–then planning also helps with obstacles: "If I feel like skipping, then I'll do just one minute" is a plan that protects the habit.
> Hang new habits on old hooks.

# Intrinsic vs extrinsic
Extrinsic rewards aren't bad. Wages, grades and bonuses get a lot done. The surprise is that, in some situations, adding a reward can crowd out the joy that was already there. This is called the **overjustification effect**.
### The drawing study
In 1973 Mark Lepper and colleagues watched nursery-school children who already loved drawing with markers. Some were promised a "good player" certificate for drawing. Later, in free time, those children drew about half as much as the others. The activity had become work.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Enjoy an activity", "Get paid for it", "\"I do it for the reward\"", "Reward stops, interest drops"]}
### The day-care fine
In 2000, economists Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini studied Israeli day-care centres that introduced a small fine for late pick-ups. Late arrivals roughly doubled. A moral duty had turned into a price, and parents happily paid it. When the fine was removed, lateness stayed high.
### When rewards help or harm
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dull, routine task", "b": "rewards usually help"}, {"a": "Already enjoyable task", "b": "expected rewards can harm"}, {"a": "Surprise praise", "b": "rarely harms"}, {"a": "Feedback on progress", "b": "often boosts interest"}]}
A large 1999 review of 128 studies found that expected, tangible rewards tied to doing a task reduced intrinsic motivation, while verbal praise did not. Some researchers argue the effect is smaller than claimed, but the basic pattern has held up.
!! More pay always means more motivation => Pay matters, but for interesting work, autonomy and meaning matter at least as much
> Rewards can buy behaviour, but they can also quietly change why we do it.

# Self-determination theory
Edward Deci and Richard Ryan developed self-determination theory from the 1970s onwards at the University of Rochester. It's one of the most widely tested theories of motivation, with studies in schools, workplaces, sport and healthcare across many countries.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Lasting motivation", "items": ["Autonomy: I chose this", "Competence: I'm improving", "Relatedness: I belong"]}
### A spectrum, not a switch
Motivation isn't just "intrinsic or extrinsic". SDT describes a range, from doing something only to avoid punishment to doing it because it reflects who you are.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Intrinsic: it's fun in itself", "Identified: I value it", "Introjected: guilt, pride", "External: reward or punishment"]}
Brushing your teeth is not fun, but most adults do it because they value it. That "identified" motivation is nearly as durable as enjoyment.
### In practice
• Teachers who explain why and offer choices get more engaged students than those who only give orders
• Patients who feel supported, not lectured, stick better to treatment plans
• Video games are gripping partly because they satisfy all three needs: choices, levelling up and teammates
### Killing motivation
Controlling language ("you must", "you should"), constant surveillance and deadlines with threats all reduce autonomy. Impossible tasks crush competence, and isolation starves relatedness.
!? Studies in countries as different as Russia, South Korea and the US suggest the three needs matter across cultures, though how autonomy shows up can differ.
> People stick with what they choose, can get better at and share with others.

# Dopamine
Dopamine is a chemical messenger made by a small group of neurons deep in the brain. Pop culture calls it the "pleasure chemical", but research suggests its main job is signalling that something is worth pursuing.
### The monkey and the juice
In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys. At first, they fired when unexpected juice arrived. After the monkeys learnt that a light predicted juice, the neurons fired at the light instead. If the juice then failed to arrive, activity dropped below normal.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Better than expected", "b": "dopamine burst"}, {"a": "Exactly as expected", "b": "no change"}, {"a": "Worse than expected", "b": "dopamine dip"}]}
This is called a **reward prediction error**: dopamine tracks surprise about rewards, which helps the brain learn what to chase.
### Wanting is not liking
Kent Berridge found that rats with almost no dopamine still made "yum" faces when given sugar, but wouldn't work to get it. Liking survived; wanting disappeared.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Wanting", "items": ["dopamine-driven", "anticipation", "pulls you to act"]}, "r": {"t": "Liking", "items": ["other brain systems", "enjoyment itself", "can be weak"]}}
### Why feeds hook us
Unpredictable rewards produce the strongest pull. B.F. Skinner showed decades ago that animals rewarded at random intervals keep pressing a lever longest. Slot machines, pull-to-refresh and endless scrolling all deliver rewards on this kind of uncertain schedule.
!! A "dopamine detox" resets your brain's dopamine levels => You can't drain dopamine by avoiding your phone; taking breaks helps by changing habits and cues
> Dopamine is the brain's "this might be good, go get it" signal, and uncertainty turns it up.

# Delayed gratification
Walter Mischel ran the original studies at a Stanford University nursery in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Children aged about four to five sat alone with a treat and could wait up to around 15 minutes to get a second one.
### The famous follow-up
Years later, Mischel's team found that children who had waited longer tended to have better exam scores and coping skills as teenagers. The test became a symbol of self-control as a key to success.
### The replication
In 2018 Tyler Watts and colleagues repeated the idea with about 900 children from more varied backgrounds. The link with later achievement was about half as large, and most of it disappeared once family income, home environment and early abilities were taken into account.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Waiting = willpower", "r": "Waiting reflects trust too"}, {"w": "Test predicts success", "r": "Background explains much"}]}
### Trust matters
In 2012 Celeste Kidd and colleagues first had an adult make a promise to each child. For some, the adult kept it; for others, the adult broke it. Children who'd met a reliable adult waited about four times longer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average wait, minutes", "items": [{"l": "Unreliable adult", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Reliable adult", "v": 12.0, "hi": true}]}
For a child whose world is unpredictable, eating the treat now is a reasonable choice, not a lack of character.
### What helped children wait
• Covering the treat or looking away
• Singing, playing or inventing games
• Thinking of the marshmallow as a fluffy cloud instead of something tasty
!? A 2018 study found that children today wait longer on average than children did in the 1960s and 1980s.
> Self-control is partly a skill and partly a sensible response to how reliable the world has been.

# Flow
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, a Hungarian-American psychologist, studied flow from the 1970s. He used the **experience sampling method**: people carried pagers that beeped at random times, and each time they noted what they were doing and how they felt.
### Challenge meets skill
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "High challenge, low skill", "b": "anxiety"}, {"a": "Low challenge, high skill", "b": "boredom"}, {"a": "Both low", "b": "apathy"}, {"a": "Both high and matched", "b": "flow"}]}
Flow appeared in surgeons, rock climbers, chess players, musicians and factory workers. It was less about the activity itself and more about its structure.
### What flow feels like
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Clear goals", "Instant feedback", "Total concentration", "Self-consciousness fades", "Time feels distorted"]}
### Finding more of it
• Choose tasks a little beyond your current skill
• Make goals concrete: "draft the intro", not "work on the report"
• Remove interruptions; flow takes time to build and seconds to break
• Raise the challenge as you improve, or boredom creeps in
Csikszentmihalyi found that people often reported more flow at work than in leisure time, yet still said they'd rather be at leisure. Passive activities like TV rarely produce flow.
!? Flow isn't always good. Gamblers and gamers can experience it, and the absorption can make it hard to stop.
> Flow lives on the edge of your ability: hard enough to need everything, possible enough to succeed.

# Building a reading habit
This plan combines every tool from the lesson: a clear cue, a tiny routine, friction removed, temptation moved away and an instant reward.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cue: dinner ends", "Sit on sofa, book ready", "Read one page", "Tick the calendar"]}
### Why each part matters
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "After dinner", "b": "stacks on a daily anchor"}, {"a": "One page", "b": "too small to skip"}, {"a": "Phone in another room", "b": "removes the main rival"}, {"a": "Calendar tick", "b": "instant visible reward"}]}
### Watching it grow
One page rarely stays one page. Once you've sat down and started, you'll often read ten. But the rule stays one page, so a tired night still counts and the chain isn't broken.
ex: 10 pages a day | × 365 days = 3,650 pages a year | Average book ≈ 300 pages | About 12 books a year
### Making it stick
• Keep the book where you'll sit, not on a shelf in another room
• Choose something you enjoy, not what you think you should read
• If you miss a day, restart the next day; one gap doesn't undo the habit
• After a few months, the cue alone will pull you to the sofa
!! Readers are born, not made => Reading regularly is mostly a habit, and habits can be built at any age
> A habit is a decision you made once and never have to make again.

== ps-social

# Conformity
Solomon Asch ran his line experiments at Swarthmore College in the early 1950s. One real participant sat among about seven people who were secretly working with the experimenter. On 12 of 18 rounds, everyone else confidently gave the same wrong answer.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~1%", "l": "errors when alone"}, {"v": "~33%", "l": "answers that conformed"}, {"v": "~75%", "l": "conformed at least once"}]}
### What makes it stronger or weaker
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Group of 3+ others", "b": "conformity near its peak"}, {"a": "One ally disagrees", "b": "conformity falls sharply"}, {"a": "Answer written privately", "b": "conformity drops"}, {"a": "Unclear task", "b": "conformity rises"}]}
The single ally was the most striking finding. When just one other person gave the right answer, conformity fell to about a quarter of its usual level. You don't need a majority to resist, only company.
### Why people go along
• **Normative influence**: fear of looking odd or being rejected
• **Informational influence**: "maybe they see something I don't"
• Many conformers said afterwards they knew the group was wrong
### Culture and era
A 1996 review of 133 Asch-type studies in 17 countries found more conformity in more collectivist cultures, and declining conformity in the US since the 1950s. A 1980 British replication found almost no conformity at all.
!! Asch showed that most people are sheep => Most answers were independent; about a quarter never conformed once
> One dissenting voice gives everyone else permission to tell the truth.

# Obedience to authority
Stanley Milgram ran his studies at Yale University from 1961, partly to understand how ordinary Germans took part in the Holocaust. A "teacher" was told to give a "learner" increasingly strong shocks for wrong answers, up to 450 volts. The learner was an actor.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "65%", "l": "went to 450 V (1963)"}, {"v": "100%", "l": "reached 300 V"}, {"v": "40", "l": "men in first study"}]}
### Variations told a fuller story
Milgram ran about 20 versions. Obedience depended heavily on the situation.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% obeying to the end", "items": [{"l": "Standard", "v": 65.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Orders by phone", "v": 21.0}, {"l": "Touch the learner", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Two peers rebel", "v": 10.0}]}
### The criticism
Archive research by Gina Perry found that experimenters sometimes went off script and that some participants suspected the shocks were fake. Psychologists Alex Haslam and Stephen Reicher argue people continued because they identified with the scientific goal, not out of blind obedience. When told bluntly "you have no other choice, you must go on", most refused.
### Replications
Full replications are now banned on ethical grounds. A partial 2009 US version, stopping at 150 volts, found similar rates to Milgram's. A 2017 study in Poland also found high obedience.
!? Milgram's experiments helped create modern research ethics, including informed consent and the right to withdraw at any time.
> Ordinary people can do harmful things when responsibility seems to belong to someone else, and support for the goal makes it easier.

# The bystander effect
Research on bystanders began after the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York. A newspaper reported that 38 neighbours watched and did nothing. Later investigations showed this was badly exaggerated: few saw the attack, and at least one person called the police. But the story inspired important research.
### The seizure experiment
In 1968 John Darley and Bibb Latané had students talk over an intercom. One voice then appeared to have a seizure.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% helping before the seizure ended", "items": [{"l": "Alone", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "With 1 other", "v": 62.0}, {"l": "With 4 others", "v": 31.0}]}
### Five steps to helping
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice the event", "See it as an emergency", "Feel responsible", "Know what to do", "Act"]}
Each step can fail. In a crowd, people look calm because everyone is checking everyone else, so the event seems less serious. And responsibility is spread thin: **diffusion of responsibility**.
### A more hopeful picture
A 2020 study by Richard Philpot and colleagues analysed real CCTV footage of 219 public conflicts in Amsterdam, Lancaster and Cape Town. In about 90% of them, at least one bystander stepped in. More bystanders meant a higher chance that someone helped.
• As an individual in a crowd, you're a little less likely to act
• As a crowd, someone usually does
• Naming one person and giving a task removes the doubt
!? 112 works across the EU, including Portugal, from mobile phones, even without credit.
> If you need help, pick one face and give one clear instruction.

# Social proof
Robert Cialdini named **social proof** in his 1984 book *Influence*. It's a sensible shortcut: when you don't know a city, a busy restaurant full of locals is often a good bet. It goes wrong when the crowd is uninformed, manipulated or copying each other.
### Looking up
In 1969 Stanley Milgram and colleagues had people stand on a New York pavement and stare up at a building. The more people staring, the more passers-by stopped and looked up too.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of passers-by who looked up", "items": [{"l": "1 person staring", "v": 42.0}, {"l": "15 people staring", "v": 86.0, "hi": true}]}
### When the crowd decides
In 2006 Matthew Salganik and colleagues created an online music market with about 14,000 participants. In some groups, people could see download counts. Social influence made hits bigger and more unpredictable: the same song could be a hit in one "world" and a flop in another.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Early downloads", "Song looks popular", "More people try it", "More downloads"]}
### Where you meet it
• Star ratings and review counts
• "12 people are looking at this hotel"
• Canned laughter on sitcoms
• Tip jars "seeded" with a few coins
### Fake proof
Because social proof is so powerful, it's also faked. Since 2022, EU consumer law bans fake reviews and requires sellers to say whether and how they check that reviews come from real customers.
!! If lots of people do it, it must be good => Crowds can be wrong, copied or bought; check who the crowd is
> Social proof is useful information, as long as the crowd knows more than you do.

# The halo effect
The term comes from psychologist Edward Thorndike in 1920. He noticed that army officers rating their soldiers gave suspiciously consistent scores: a soldier rated as handsome was also rated as intelligent, loyal and a good leader.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "One visible trait", "items": ["seems smarter", "seems kinder", "seems more honest", "seems more capable"]}
### Faces and votes
In 2005 Alexander Todorov showed people pairs of faces of US election candidates for about one second and asked who looked more competent. Their snap judgements predicted the winner in roughly 70% of Senate races.
### Unaware of our own halos
In a 1977 study, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson showed students a video of a lecturer who was either warm or cold. Students who saw the warm version rated his looks, mannerisms and accent as appealing; the cold version, as irritating. Students insisted his warmth had nothing to do with it.
### The halo at work
• Job interviews: confident speakers get credited with skills they never showed
• Brands: a great phone makes the same company's watch seem good
• Courts: studies suggest attractive defendants can get milder treatment
• The **horn effect** is the reverse: one bad trait drags everything down
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Overall gut rating", "r": "Score each skill separately"}, {"w": "Judge from a CV photo", "r": "Anonymise first"}]}
!? Portugal's national secondary-school exams are marked anonymously, which removes any halo from the marker knowing the student.
> Judge one quality at a time, or one quality will judge all the others for you.

# In-groups and out-groups
In the early 1970s Henri Tajfel at the University of Bristol divided schoolboys into groups on almost meaningless grounds, such as preferring paintings by Klee or Kandinsky. The boys never met their group. Yet when sharing out rewards, they favoured their own group, sometimes even choosing to maximise the gap rather than the total.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Arbitrary label", "Sense of \"us\"", "Favour own group", "Distrust \"them\""]}
### Social identity
Tajfel and John Turner proposed that part of our self-esteem comes from groups we belong to. Making "our" group look good makes us feel good, which explains why fans say "we won" after a match they only watched.
### Shared identity can widen
In a 2005 study by Mark Levine, Manchester United fans were more likely to help an injured stranger wearing a United shirt than a Liverpool one. But when they were first reminded that they were football fans in general, they helped the Liverpool fan too.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Divides groups", "items": ["competition for scarce prizes", "no contact", "stereotypes"]}, "r": {"t": "Unites groups", "items": ["shared goals", "equal contact", "common identity"]}}
### Reducing hostility
• A 2006 review of over 500 studies found that contact between groups reliably reduces prejudice
• Shared goals that need both groups work especially well
!? The famous 1954 Robbers Cave camp study, where boys' groups became rivals, has been criticised: in an earlier attempt, the boys refused to turn on each other, and that version was rarely mentioned.
> "Us and them" is easy to start, but the circle of "us" can always be drawn bigger.

# Cognitive dissonance
Leon Festinger proposed the theory in 1957. He had studied a small group that predicted the world would end on 21 December 1954. When it didn't, some members became more committed, not less, telling everyone that their faith had saved the world.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Act or belief clash", "Feel discomfort", "Change belief or excuse", "Discomfort eases"]}
### The $1 lie
In 1959 Festinger and James Carlsmith paid students either $1 or $20 to tell the next participant that a very boring task was fun. Later, the $1 group rated the task as more enjoyable. With $20, the lie was justified by money. With $1, the easiest fix was to believe the task wasn't so bad.
### Everyday forms
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "After buying", "b": "we praise what we chose"}, {"a": "After hard effort", "b": "we value the result more"}, {"a": "Smoking", "b": "\"the risks are exaggerated\""}, {"a": "Doing someone a favour", "b": "we come to like them more"}]}
### How solid is it?
The general idea is widely accepted and fits much everyday behaviour. But some classic experiments have struggled in modern tests. A large 2024 replication across about 40 labs did not find the predicted attitude change in the "paid to argue" set-up. The theory is alive, but its lab demonstrations are less sturdy than textbooks suggest.
!? Benjamin Franklin noticed something similar in the 1700s: asking a rival to lend you a book can make them like you more, since they must explain why they helped.
> When actions and beliefs clash, we often bend the belief to protect our sense of being sensible.

# Reciprocity
Reciprocity is found in every known culture. It allows trade and cooperation between people who aren't related: I help you now, you help me later. Because the rule is so deep, even small or unwanted gifts trigger it.
### The can of Coke
In a 1971 study by Dennis Regan, a stranger either bought participants a Coke or didn't. Later he asked them to buy raffle tickets. Those who got the drink bought about twice as many tickets, even if they didn't like him.
### Mints and tips
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "tip increase, % (US restaurant)", "items": [{"l": "One mint", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Two mints", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "Mint + \"extra one\"", "v": 23.0, "hi": true}]}
The largest effect came when the waiter turned back and offered a second mint as if just for that table. A personal, unexpected gift triggers the strongest pull.
### Concessions count too
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Big request", "Refusal", "Smaller request", "Feels like a concession", "Yes"]}
In a 1975 study, only 17% agreed to take young offenders to the zoo for a day. When first asked to mentor them for two years, and after refusing, 50% agreed to the zoo trip. This is the **door-in-the-face** technique.
### Watch out for
• Free samples and "gifts" before a sales pitch
• Unasked-for favours that make refusing feel rude
• Gifts to professionals: many countries, including Portugal, require drug companies to declare payments and gifts to doctors
!? In 1976 a sociologist sent Christmas cards to hundreds of strangers. A surprising number sent one back, some with long family letters.
> A gift you didn't ask for can still feel like a debt, so notice who's giving and why.

# The spotlight effect
The study was published by Thomas Gilovich and colleagues in 2000. Students had to wear a T-shirt with a big picture of singer Barry Manilow, considered deeply uncool by their peers, into a room of others.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of room who noticed the T-shirt", "items": [{"l": "Wearer's guess", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Actually noticed", "v": 23.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why we overestimate
We are at the centre of our own experience, so we start from our own view and adjust too little for the fact that others are busy with theirs. Everyone in the room is mostly thinking about themselves.
### Related illusions
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Spotlight effect", "b": "others notice us less"}, {"a": "Illusion of transparency", "b": "nerves show less than we think"}, {"a": "Bad hair days", "b": "others rarely track them"}]}
### Using it
In a 2003 study, people about to give a speech were told that nervousness is much less visible than it feels. Their speeches were rated as better and more relaxed than those of speakers who weren't told.
• A stumble in a presentation is usually forgotten by the audience within minutes
• Others rarely notice that you wore the same jumper twice this week
• The same bias means you also miss most of other people's small slips
!? The spotlight effect works for good things too: people overestimate how much others noticed their brilliant comment in a meeting.
> You are the star of your own film, but just an extra in everyone else's.

# Using it for good
The towel study was published by Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius in 2008, in an American hotel. Cards in bathrooms asked guests to reuse towels using different messages.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of guests reusing towels", "items": [{"l": "Save the planet", "v": 35.0}, {"l": "Most guests reuse", "v": 44.0}, {"l": "Guests in this room", "v": 49.0, "hi": true}]}
The more similar the "others" were to the guest, the stronger the effect. A **descriptive norm**, what people actually do, beat a moral appeal.
### More examples
• Letters saying "9 out of 10 people in your area pay their tax on time" increased on-time payments in the UK
• Energy bills comparing you with similar neighbours cut use by about 2% on average
• Showing a smiley face to below-average users stopped them drifting up to the average
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "\"Many people litter here\"", "r": "\"Most visitors keep it clean\""}, {"w": "Only shame bad users", "r": "Also praise good ones"}]}
### A warning sign can backfire
In a US national park, a sign saying many visitors stole petrified wood increased theft. It told people that stealing was normal. Messages should describe the good behaviour most people do, not the bad behaviour of a few.
### The ethics
Using social psychology for good means being honest: real norms, real numbers, and choices people can still refuse.
!? Governments in many countries, including the UK, the Netherlands and Germany, have set up behavioural teams to test such nudges in public services.
> Tell people what most people like them actually do, and many will follow.

== ps-emotions

# Basic emotions
In the late 1960s Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen travelled to the highlands of Papua New Guinea to study the Fore people, who had seen almost no Western media. Told short stories ("her child has died"), people picked the matching face from photos well above chance. Fore faces, photographed in turn, were understood by Americans.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Happiness", "Sadness", "Fear", "Anger", "Surprise and disgust"]}
### Reading faces
Ekman later built the Facial Action Coding System (1978), which breaks every expression into small muscle movements. A genuine smile, for example, also tightens the muscles around the eyes, named the **Duchenne smile** after a 19th-century French doctor.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "recognition, % (illustrative pattern)", "items": [{"l": "Happiness", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Surprise", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Anger", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Fear", "v": 60.0}]}
Happiness is almost always recognised best. Fear and disgust are confused more often, especially across cultures.
### The debate
Ekman's view, that a few emotions are built-in and universal, is still influential but contested. Psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that emotions are built by the brain from body signals, past experience and culture.
• When people describe faces in their own words, instead of choosing from a list, agreement drops a lot
• In 2016 researchers found that Trobriand Islanders read the Western "fear face" as threatening, not scared
• Real faces in real life often don't match the textbook expressions
!? Ekman later added a seventh candidate, contempt: a one-sided tightening of the mouth.
> Some expressions travel well across cultures, but faces are clues to feelings, not a reliable readout.

# What emotions are for
Charles Darwin argued in 1872 that emotional expressions evolved because they were useful. Modern research largely agrees: emotions are not the opposite of reason but a fast way of steering behaviour.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fear", "b": "escape or freeze"}, {"a": "Anger", "b": "defend, remove a block"}, {"a": "Disgust", "b": "avoid contamination"}, {"a": "Sadness", "b": "withdraw, seek comfort"}]}
### Faces with a job
In 2008 Joshua Susskind and colleagues showed that a fear face, with wide eyes and flared nostrils, actually widens the field of view and lets in more air. The disgust face does the opposite: narrowed eyes and a wrinkled nose limit what gets in.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fear face", "items": ["eyes wide", "more air in", "see more"]}, "r": {"t": "Disgust face", "items": ["eyes narrowed", "nose wrinkled", "let less in"]}}
### Decisions need feelings
Neurologist Antonio Damasio studied a patient he called Elliot, who lost part of his frontal lobes to a tumour. His intelligence tests were normal, but he felt very little. He could spend half an hour choosing between two dates for an appointment. Without emotional signals marking options as good or bad, every choice was endless.
### Social signals
• Tears tell others you need help
• Embarrassment shows you know you broke a rule, which often wins forgiveness
• Smiles invite cooperation
!! Emotions get in the way of good decisions => Without emotions, people struggle to make decisions at all
> Emotions are the brain's quick advice about what matters right now.

# Fight or flight
The phrase "fight or flight" was coined by American physiologist Walter Cannon in 1915. Researchers now often add "freeze": many animals, and people, go still when danger appears.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Threat spotted", "Amygdala raises alarm", "Hypothalamus signals", "Adrenal glands: adrenaline", "Body ready in seconds"]}
### Two speeds
The fast route uses nerves and adrenaline, working in seconds. A slower route, the hormone chain known as the **HPA axis**, releases cortisol over minutes and keeps you alert for longer.
### What changes in the body
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Heart and breathing", "b": "faster, more oxygen"}, {"a": "Pupils", "b": "wider, more light"}, {"a": "Digestion", "b": "slows: \"butterflies\""}, {"a": "Liver", "b": "releases sugar for fuel"}]}
Neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux showed that the amygdala gets a rough signal from the senses before the thinking cortex has worked out what's happening. That's why you jump at a stick that looks like a snake before you realise it's a stick.
### Rethinking the racing heart
In a 2014 study by Alison Wood Brooks, people who said "I am excited" before singing or public speaking performed better than those who said "I am calm". It's easier to relabel arousal than to switch it off. A 2012 study found that telling students their nerves could help them improved their maths test scores.
• Short bursts of stress sharpen attention
• Long-lasting stress, with cortisol staying high, harms sleep, mood and health
!? Shelley Taylor proposed in 2000 that, alongside fight or flight, people often "tend and befriend": they protect others and seek support under stress.
> Your body can't tell a bear from a boardroom, but you can tell it what the racing heart is for.

# Naming emotions
In a 2007 brain-scanning study, Matthew Lieberman and colleagues showed people photos of angry or scared faces. When they chose a word for the emotion, activity in the amygdala dropped, and a region at the front of the brain linked to self-control became more active. Choosing a matching name, instead of matching gender, made the difference.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Strong feeling", "Put it into words", "Prefrontal cortex engages", "Amygdala calms"]}
### Spiders and words
In a 2012 study, people afraid of spiders approached a tarantula. Those who described their feelings ("I'm anxious that this ugly spider will jump on me") showed less physical stress when they faced it again a week later than those who tried to think about it more positively or distracted themselves.
### Emotional granularity
Some people describe their feelings in broad terms ("I feel bad"). Others are more precise ("I feel disappointed and a bit embarrassed"). People with more precise words tend to cope better with stress and are less likely to drink heavily to manage it.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "I feel bad", "r": "I'm disappointed and tired"}, {"w": "I'm stressed", "r": "I'm worried about the deadline"}]}
### Ways to practise
• Pause and finish the sentence "Right now I feel…"
• Look for two or three words instead of one
• Write for 15 minutes about something upsetting; James Pennebaker found this can help, though effects are modest
!? Languages carve up emotions differently. Portuguese **saudade**, a longing for something absent and maybe lost, has no exact English word.
> Name it to tame it: a precise word gives the thinking brain something to hold.

# The hedonic treadmill
Psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell coined the phrase in 1971. Like running on a treadmill, we keep going after better things but stay in roughly the same place.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Something good happens", "Happiness rises", "New normal sets in", "Want the next thing"]}
### The lottery study
A famous 1978 study found that lottery winners were not much happier than other people a year or so later. But it involved only 22 winners. In 2020, a study of thousands of Swedish lottery players found that large wins did raise life satisfaction, and the effect lasted for more than a decade.
### Adaptation isn't complete
Later research by Ed Diener, Richard Lucas and others refined the idea:
• People don't return to a neutral point; each person has their own typical level
• Some events leave lasting marks: long-term unemployment, losing a partner, severe disability
• Adaptation to good things is often faster than to bad ones
### Money and happiness
In 2023 rivals Daniel Kahneman and Matthew Killingsworth worked together on the question. For most people, happiness kept rising with income, but in proportion: each doubling helps about the same. Only for the unhappiest people did happiness level off above about $100,000 a year.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "wellbeing gain (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "€20k → €40k", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "€40k → €80k", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "€80k → €82k", "v": 0.3, "hi": true}]}
### Slowing the treadmill
• Spend on experiences and other people, not just things
• Add variety so pleasures don't become routine
• Pause to savour good moments
!! A big win would make me happy forever => It helps, but you adapt; daily habits and relationships matter more
> We adapt to almost anything, which is both the problem and the comfort.

# What predicts happiness
The **Harvard Study of Adult Development** began in 1938 with 268 Harvard students, including future US president John F. Kennedy. A second group of 456 boys from poor Boston neighbourhoods was added soon after. Researchers have followed them, and later their children, ever since.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1938", "l": "Study begins"}, {"y": "1940s", "l": "Boston boys added"}, {"y": "2003", "l": "Waldinger directs"}, {"y": "2023", "l": "\"The Good Life\" book"}]}
### The central finding
Director Robert Waldinger reported that how satisfied people were with their relationships at 50 predicted their health at 80 better than their cholesterol levels did. People in warm relationships also kept their memories sharper for longer.
### Backed by other studies
A 2010 review of 148 studies, involving around 300,000 people, found that people with strong social ties had about a 50% higher chance of surviving during the study period. The size of the effect was comparable to that of quitting smoking.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Close relationships", "Health and sleep", "Meaningful work", "Enough money"]}
### Limits
• The original groups were all men, mostly white Americans
• It's observational, so it shows links, not proof of cause
• Happy, healthy people may also find it easier to keep friends
• Even so, experiments and other countries' data point the same way
!? Waldinger's 2015 TED talk on the study is one of the most-watched talks ever, with tens of millions of views.
> The best investment for a good old age is in people, not only in a pension.

# Gratitude
Research on gratitude took off in the early 2000s. Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked people to write weekly about things they were grateful for, things that annoyed them, or neutral events. After ten weeks, the grateful group felt more optimistic and reported fewer physical complaints.
### Two classic exercises
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Three good things", "items": ["nightly, for a week", "and why they happened", "slow, lasting lift"]}, "r": {"t": "Gratitude letter", "items": ["write to someone", "read it to them", "big but short boost"]}}
In a 2005 study by Martin Seligman and colleagues, the gratitude visit produced the largest boost after one month, which faded by three. The "three good things" effect was smaller at first but lasted up to six months for people who kept going.
### How big is the effect?
Honest answer: real but modest. Reviews of dozens of trials find gratitude exercises improve wellbeing a little compared with doing nothing, and less compared with other positive activities. They are not a treatment for depression on their own.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Note good things", "Attention shifts", "Notice more good", "Mood lifts a little"]}
### Making it work
• Be specific: "my neighbour carried the shopping", not "my neighbour"
• Vary what you write, or it becomes a dull ritual
• Once or a few times a week may work as well as daily
• Don't force it during real grief or crisis
!? In a 2018 study, people writing thank-you letters underestimated how happy recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward it would be.
> Gratitude doesn't change your life, it changes what you notice in it.

# Exercise and mood
A 2024 review in the *BMJ* pooled 218 trials with over 14,000 people with depression. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all reduced symptoms, with effects comparable to talking therapies. Harder exercise tended to help more.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Walking or jogging", "b": "clear benefit"}, {"a": "Yoga", "b": "clear benefit"}, {"a": "Strength training", "b": "clear benefit"}, {"a": "Mixed programmes", "b": "also helpful"}]}
### The 1999 trial
In a US study of older adults with depression, 16 weeks of aerobic exercise worked about as well as an antidepressant. Ten months later, those who kept exercising were less likely to relapse.
### Why it helps
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Exercise lifts mood", "items": ["Better sleep", "Brain growth factors", "Sense of achievement", "Contact with others"]}
The "runner's high" was long credited to endorphins. Research now suggests endocannabinoids, the body's own cannabis-like chemicals, may play a bigger role, because endorphins don't easily reach the brain from the blood.
### How much?
The World Health Organization recommends 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults, plus muscle strengthening twice a week. But mood benefits start far below that. A single brisk 10-minute walk can lift mood within minutes.
!! Exercise only helps if it's intense and long => Any movement helps; consistency matters more than intensity at the start
!? For severe depression, exercise works best alongside professional treatment, not instead of it.
> Movement is one of the cheapest and best-tested mood treatments we have.

# Growth mindset
Carol Dweck, a psychologist at Stanford, set out the idea in her 2006 book *Mindset*. A **fixed mindset** sees ability as a set amount; a **growth mindset** sees it as something built through effort, good strategies and help.
### Praise that backfires
In a 1998 study by Claudia Mueller and Dweck, children solved puzzles and were praised either for being smart or for working hard. Those praised for intelligence later avoided harder puzzles, enjoyed them less after failing and were more likely to inflate their scores.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fixed mindset", "items": ["\"I'm not a maths person\"", "avoids challenges", "failure = verdict"]}, "r": {"t": "Growth mindset", "items": ["\"I can't do it yet\"", "seeks challenges", "failure = feedback"]}}
### The replication story
Mindset became hugely popular in schools, and later evidence has been more sober.
• A 2018 set of meta-analyses found only a weak link between mindset and grades
• A large US study in 2019 found a one-hour online lesson raised grades slightly, mainly for lower-achieving students
• A 2023 review argued that, once weaker studies were removed, average effects were close to zero
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Mindset transforms results", "r": "Small effects, for some"}, {"w": "Just praise effort", "r": "Praise effort and strategy"}]}
### What still holds
The brain does change with learning: London taxi drivers who memorised the city's streets grew larger hippocampi. And how people interpret struggle matters. Dweck herself warns against a "false growth mindset" where effort is praised even when it isn't working.
> Abilities can grow, but belief alone does little without good strategies and support.

# A 2-minute reset
This routine combines three well-supported ideas: slow breathing, naming the feeling and shrinking the problem to one step.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Breathe in 4, out 6", "Repeat for a minute", "Name the feeling", "Choose one small step"]}
### Why long exhales calm you
Your heart speeds up slightly when you breathe in and slows when you breathe out. This is controlled by the vagus nerve, part of the "rest and digest" system. Making exhales longer gives the slowing phase more time.
ex: In 4 seconds + out 6 seconds = 10 seconds per breath | 60 ÷ 10 = 6 breaths per minute | Resting adults usually breathe 12–20 times a minute
Breathing at about six breaths a minute is linked to higher heart rate variability, a sign that the calming system is active. A small 2023 study at Stanford found that five minutes a day of breathing with long exhales improved mood over a month, slightly more than mindfulness meditation.
### The other two steps
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Name the feeling", "b": "calms the alarm system"}, {"a": "One small step", "b": "turns worry into action"}, {"a": "Long exhale", "b": "slows the heart"}]}
A next step should be tiny and concrete: send one email, drink a glass of water, write a list. Stress shrinks when the problem stops feeling vague.
!? If stress or panic happens often or feels unmanageable, talk to a doctor. In Portugal, SNS 24 (808 24 24 24) also offers psychological support by phone.
> You can't think your way out of a racing heart, but you can breathe your way back to thinking.

== ps-decisions

# Decision fatigue
The idea that self-control and decision-making draw on a limited resource is called **ego depletion**. Roy Baumeister proposed it in 1998, after people who had resisted eating cookies gave up sooner on an impossible puzzle.
### The replication crisis hits
In 2016, 23 labs with over 2,000 participants ran a pre-registered test of ego depletion. They found an effect close to zero. A second large project in 2021 found at most a very small one. The idea that willpower runs on blood sugar has also been largely rejected.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "effect size (d)", "items": [{"l": "Early studies", "v": 0.62, "t": "0.6", "hi": true}, {"l": "2016 replication", "v": 0.04}]}
### The hungry judges
A 2011 study claimed Israeli parole judges granted about 65% of requests after a meal break, falling to nearly zero before the next break. Critics later pointed out that cases weren't in random order: prisoners without lawyers tended to come last in each session. The size of the effect is very doubtful.
### So is it real?
Something like fatigue does show up in real data. Doctors prescribe slightly more antibiotics later in a clinic shift, and fewer patients get offered vaccines late in the day. But tiredness, time pressure and hunger may all play a part, not a "willpower tank".
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Willpower is a fuel tank", "r": "Effort and motivation shift"}, {"w": "Eat sugar to decide better", "r": "Take breaks, rest"}]}
Barack Obama told a journalist in 2012 that he wore only grey or blue suits to save decisions. Reducing trivial choices is sensible, even if the science behind it is shakier than once thought.
> Make important decisions when rested and routine ones automatic, but don't blame a "willpower tank" for every bad choice.

# The paradox of choice
The jam study was run by Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper at a supermarket in California in 2000. A tasting stand showed either 6 or 24 jams.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of passers-by", "items": [{"l": "Stopped: 24 jams", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "Stopped: 6 jams", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Bought: 24 jams", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Bought: 6 jams", "v": 30.0, "hi": true}]}
The big display attracted more people, but far fewer bought. Psychologist Barry Schwartz made it famous in his 2004 book *The Paradox of Choice*.
### The replication story
In 2010 a review of 50 experiments, with over 5,000 people, found that on average more choice had almost no effect. Sometimes it hurt, sometimes it helped. A 2015 review identified when overload does happen.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Options hard to compare", "You don't know what you want", "The choice is complex", "You want it over quickly"]}
### Real-world example
In 2000 Sweden reformed its pensions and let people choose from about 450 funds. Many chose actively at first, picking funds that had done well recently, and later did worse on average than the default fund chosen by experts.
• Restaurants with short menus often feel easier to choose from
• Comparison tools, filters and good defaults reduce overload
!! More choice always makes people unhappy => It depends: choice helps when you know what you want and options are easy to compare
> Choice is a gift until comparing becomes the job.

# Satisficers and maximisers
Economist Herbert Simon coined the word **satisficing** in 1956, combining "satisfy" and "suffice". He argued that real people, with limited time and information, can't find the best option, so they look for one that's good enough. He won the 1978 Nobel Prize in economics, partly for this work.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Maximiser", "items": ["checks every option", "compares endlessly", "more regret"]}, "r": {"t": "Satisficer", "items": ["sets a standard", "picks first that fits", "moves on"]}}
### Better outcomes, worse feelings
In a 2006 study, Sheena Iyengar, Rachel Wells and Barry Schwartz followed graduating students looking for jobs. Maximisers landed starting salaries about 20% higher, yet felt more pessimistic, stressed and unhappy about the process and the job.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "starting salary, $ thousands", "items": [{"l": "Satisficers", "v": 37.0}, {"l": "Maximisers", "v": 44.5, "hi": true}]}
### Not all maximising is bad
Later work split maximising into parts. Having high standards is fine and can even help. The harmful parts are endless searching and agonising over choices.
### Choosing like a satisficer
• Write down your must-haves before looking
• Pick the first option that meets them
• Limit how many options you'll consider, say three flats or five laptops
• Don't keep browsing after you've bought
• Save maximising for decisions that really matter
!? People prefer choices they can reverse, but a 2002 study found they were happier with photos they had to keep than with ones they could swap.
> "Good enough" is often the best choice, because it leaves you time to enjoy it.

# Defaults
A default is what happens if you do nothing. Defaults work through three forces: it takes effort to change, a default feels like a recommendation, and switching away feels like a loss.
### Organ donation in Europe
In 2003 Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared European countries' organ donor registration.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% consenting to donate (2003)", "items": [{"l": "Germany (opt-in)", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Netherlands (opt-in)", "v": 28.0}, {"l": "Portugal (opt-out)", "v": 99.6, "hi": true}, {"l": "Austria (opt-out)", "v": 99.98}]}
Neighbours Germany and Austria are culturally similar, yet their rates were wildly different because of the form.
### Registration isn't donation
Actual donations depend on far more than the law. Spain, an opt-out country, has had the world's highest rate of deceased organ donors for decades, mainly thanks to trained transplant coordinators in every hospital. In practice, families are usually still consulted.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why defaults stick", "items": ["Effort to change", "Seems recommended", "Leaving feels like a loss"]}
### Defaults everywhere
• Automatic enrolment in workplace pensions
• Green electricity set as standard: a German study found almost everyone kept it
• Cookie banners: "accept all" highlighted as the easy choice
• Software settings most people never change
!? Wales switched to opt-out donation in 2015 and England in 2020. The Netherlands moved in 2020 to a system where people who don't register are recorded as having no objection.
> Whoever sets the default makes most people's choice for them, so choose defaults with care.

# Pre-mortem
Psychologist Gary Klein described the pre-mortem in 2007. A post-mortem explains why something died; a pre-mortem imagines the death in advance so you can prevent it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Explain the plan", "Imagine it failed", "Each writes reasons alone", "Share all reasons", "Fix the plan"]}
### Why it works
A 1989 study found that imagining an event has already happened, called **prospective hindsight**, made people come up with about 30% more reasons for an outcome. "It failed, why?" is easier to answer than "could it fail?"
It also gives permission. In teams, doubters often stay quiet to avoid seeming disloyal, a pattern psychologist Irving Janis called **groupthink**. A pre-mortem makes finding problems the task.
### Optimism is the usual risk
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "10 years", "l": "late: Berlin's new airport"}, {"v": "~14×", "l": "Sydney Opera House cost"}]}
Berlin Brandenburg Airport was due to open in 2011 and opened in 2020. Sydney's Opera House cost roughly 14 times its first budget. Researchers call the habit of underestimating time and cost the **planning fallacy**.
### Running one
• Do it after the plan is drafted but before committing
• Have everyone write privately first, so loud voices don't dominate
• Turn the top risks into actions and owners
• Works for personal plans too: a house move, a career change, a holiday
!? Daniel Kahneman described the pre-mortem as one of the most useful decision techniques he knew.
> Imagine the failure today so you don't have to explain it next year.

# 10/10/10
Business writer Suzy Welch popularised the 10/10/10 rule in a 2009 book. It has no single study behind it, but it builds on several well-tested ideas about how time changes our feelings.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "10 min", "l": "raw emotion"}, {"y": "10 months", "l": "practical effects"}, {"y": "10 years", "l": "values and identity"}]}
### Why distance helps
Close-up, we think in concrete details and feelings. From a distance, we think about values and the bigger picture. Ethan Kross and colleagues found that imagining how you'll feel about a problem in ten years, called **temporal distancing**, reduces distress.
### Regret over time
Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec found in 1995 that in the short term people regret things they did. Over a lifetime, they regret things they didn't do far more.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Short-term regret", "items": ["mistakes made", "\"I shouldn't have\""]}, "r": {"t": "Long-term regret", "items": ["chances missed", "\"I wish I had\""]}}
ex: Should I take a job in Lisbon? | 10 minutes: anxious, sad to leave Porto | 10 months: settled, new colleagues | 10 years: career shaped by the move, or not
### Using it well
• Write the three answers down; it slows you down
• Watch for the 10-minute answer dominating
• The 10-year answer isn't always right: fun and rest also count
!! Long-term thinking always means the sensible choice => It can also reveal that a bold choice is worth the short-term pain
> Ask the version of you ten years from now; they are usually calmer and braver.

# Reversible or not?
Jeff Bezos laid out the idea in his 1997 and 2015 letters to Amazon shareholders, calling them Type 1 and Type 2 decisions. His worry was that large organisations treat every decision like a one-way door and become slow.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "One-way door", "items": ["hard to undo", "decide slowly", "gather evidence"]}, "r": {"t": "Two-way door", "items": ["easy to reverse", "decide fast", "learn by trying"]}}
### Examples
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Selling a family house", "b": "one-way"}, {"a": "Having a tattoo", "b": "mostly one-way"}, {"a": "Trying a new gym", "b": "two-way"}, {"a": "Online purchase in EU", "b": "two-way: 14 days to return"}]}
### Two kinds of mistakes
• Treating a two-way door as one-way: endless meetings, missed chances, slowness
• Treating a one-way door as two-way: rushing something you can't undo
Bezos also argued that most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you'd like. Waiting for 90% usually means being too slow.
### Turn one-way into two-way
Many big decisions can be split into smaller, reversible steps. Rent in a new city before buying. Run a pilot before a full launch. Take a short course before changing career.
!? Reversibility has a cost: people often agonise more when they can change their minds. Once a two-way choice is made, commit to it for a while before reviewing.
> Spend your caution on the doors that lock behind you.

# Overconfidence
Overconfidence is one of the most widespread biases. Psychologists split it into three types: overestimating your ability, believing you're better than others and being too sure about how accurate your beliefs are.
### Too-narrow ranges
Ask people for a range they're 90% sure contains an answer, such as the length of the Douro river. In many studies, the true answer falls inside only about half the time. Ranges are far too narrow.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of answers right", "items": [{"l": "How sure people felt", "v": 90.0}, {"l": "How often right", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}]}
### Everyone's above average
In a 1981 study, about 93% of American students and 69% of Swedish students rated themselves as safer drivers than the median. Only half can be.
### Planning too
In a 1994 study, students predicted they'd finish their thesis in about 34 days. On average it took about 56, and fewer than a third finished by their own estimate.
ex: Your guess: renovation takes 2 months | Similar projects took 3–5 months | Use the outside view: plan 4 months | Then add your own specifics
### Who is well calibrated?
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Well calibrated", "items": ["weather forecasters", "bridge players"]}, "r": {"t": "Poorly calibrated", "items": ["most of us", "many pundits"]}}
Weather forecasters get fast, clear feedback every day, so when they say 70% chance of rain, it rains about 70% of the time.
• Give ranges, not single numbers
• Check the **base rate**: how often does this usually work?
• Keep score of your predictions and review them
> Confidence is a feeling, not evidence, so check it against your track record.

# Gut vs analysis
In 2009 Daniel Kahneman, a sceptic of intuition, and Gary Klein, its champion, wrote a joint paper. They agreed that intuition can be trusted when two conditions are met.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Regular, predictable world", "Lots of practice", "Quick, clear feedback"]}
### The firefighter
Klein interviewed fire commanders. One ordered his crew out of a burning house without knowing why; seconds later, the floor collapsed. The fire was in the basement. His gut had noticed that the room was too hot and too quiet for a kitchen fire. Years of fires had trained that pattern.
### When intuition fails
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Trust the gut", "items": ["chess, firefighting", "nursing, driving"]}, "r": {"t": "Use analysis", "items": ["stock picking", "hiring, forecasts"]}}
Political experts in Philip Tetlock's long study did barely better than chance at long-term forecasts. Stock markets and politics give noisy, slow feedback, so experience builds confidence more than skill.
### Formulas versus experts
Since Paul Meehl's work in 1954, studies have compared expert judgement with simple scoring rules. A 2000 review of 136 studies found the formula did as well or better in the large majority of cases, from medical diagnosis to predicting parole success.
• Unstructured job interviews are weak predictors; structured ones with set questions do better
• Use the gut as one input, after the analysis, not before it
!? Intuition is often recognition. As Herbert Simon put it, it's "nothing more and nothing less than recognition" of patterns learnt through experience.
> Trust your gut where it has been trained by fast, honest feedback; elsewhere, check the numbers.

# A simple decision matrix
A weighted decision matrix forces you to make your priorities explicit. Benjamin Franklin described a similar "moral algebra" in a 1772 letter: list pros and cons over several days, then cross out items of equal weight.
### A worked example: choosing a flat

### Steps
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["List options", "Pick 3–5 criteria", "Weight them", "Score each option", "Sum, then sense-check"]}
### Why the gut check matters
If the winner feels wrong, that's information. Maybe a criterion is missing, like "near my parents", or a weight is off. Adjust and rerun. A close result, like 3.5 versus 3.6, really means "both are fine".
!? In a 1991 study, people who analysed their reasons for liking jams ended up with rankings less in line with expert tasters. For matters of taste, too much analysis can mislead.
### Tips
• Decide criteria and weights before scoring, to avoid fiddling
• Score each criterion across all options before moving on
• Use it for big choices: jobs, homes, schools, cars

== ps-persuasion

# Cialdini's principles
Robert Cialdini, an American social psychologist, published *Influence* in 1984. To write it, he combined lab experiments with years of undercover fieldwork: he answered job adverts and trained as a salesperson, fundraiser and advertiser to see which tactics professionals actually used.
### The seven levers
His first six principles were joined by a seventh, **unity**, in his 2016 book *Pre-Suasion*.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Reciprocity", "b": "we repay what we receive"}, {"a": "Commitment", "b": "we stay consistent"}, {"a": "Social proof", "b": "we copy people like us"}, {"a": "Unity", "b": "we favour 'one of us'"}]}
The other three are authority, liking and scarcity, each covered in this lesson.
### Why shortcuts work
Each principle is a mental shortcut that is usually sensible. Repaying favours keeps relationships fair; following the crowd is often wise when you don't know better. Persuaders exploit the shortcut by triggering it when it doesn't fit.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Trigger appears", "Shortcut fires", "Automatic yes", "Reasons found later"]}
### Reciprocity in a restaurant
In a 2002 US study, waiters who left one sweet with the bill got slightly higher tips than those who left none. Giving two sweets raised tips more. The biggest effect came when a waiter left one, walked away, then turned back and offered a second "just for you".
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "tip increase vs no sweet, %", "items": [{"l": "One sweet", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Two sweets", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "One, then 'for you'", "v": 23.0, "hi": true}]}
This was a small field study, so treat the exact numbers with caution, but the general power of reciprocity is one of the best-supported findings in social psychology.
!! Persuasion only works on gullible people => The levers work on everyone because they piggyback on shortcuts we all use
> Influence works by triggering useful mental shortcuts at moments when they don't actually fit.

# Scarcity
We value things more when they are rare, running out or about to be taken away. Part of this is sensible, because scarce things often are valuable. Part of it is **loss aversion**: losses hurt more than equal gains please us, so missing out feels like losing.
### The cookie jar
In a 1975 study, people tasted biscuits from a jar holding either ten or two. The same biscuits from the nearly empty jar were rated more desirable. They were rated highest when the jar went from ten to two because "others wanted them".
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "desirability (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Plenty in jar", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Always scarce", "v": 6.5}, {"l": "Newly scarce", "v": 7.5, "hi": true}]}
### Reactance
The psychologist Jack Brehm described **reactance** in 1966: when our freedom to have something is threatened, we want it more. That's why "limited edition", banned books and "while stocks last" grab attention.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Option threatened", "Freedom feels at risk", "Desire rises", "Hasty decision"]}
### Scarcity online
• "Only 2 rooms left at this price!"
• "15 people are looking at this right now"
• Countdown timers that restart when you reload the page
In 2019 the UK competition regulator made major hotel booking websites promise to stop misleading pressure claims. EU consumer law already bans falsely saying a product is available only for a very limited time to rush a decision.
!? Scarcity can make us dumber in other ways: research on poverty suggests that juggling scarce money or time uses up mental bandwidth, making other decisions harder.
> When something suddenly seems rare, ask whether you wanted it before you were told it was running out.

# Authority
From childhood we learn that doctors, teachers and police usually know best. Following experts is often wise. The trouble is that we respond to the **symbols** of authority, such as titles, uniforms and confident tone, even when the expertise behind them is missing.
### Milgram's experiment
In 1961–62 at Yale, Stanley Milgram asked volunteers to give what they believed were increasingly strong electric shocks to a "learner" whenever he made a mistake. In the best-known version, about 65% went all the way to the maximum 450 volts when a man in a grey lab coat told them to continue.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% obeying to the end (selected versions)", "items": [{"l": "Yale, lab coat", "v": 65.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Rundown office", "v": 48.0}, {"l": "Orders by phone", "v": 21.0}]}
### How solid is it?
A partial replication in 2009 found similar obedience up to 150 volts, the point where the learner first protests strongly. But archive research by Gina Perry and others showed the procedure varied, many participants doubted the shocks were real and the experimenter sometimes improvised pressure. Obedience is real; the exact 65% is less solid than textbooks suggest.
### Symbols that work
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Titles: Dr, Professor", "Uniforms and white coats", "Expensive clothes and cars", "Confident, fluent speech"]}
• In a 1974 study, people on the street were more likely to obey a stranger dressed as a security guard than one in ordinary clothes
• Adverts use actors in white coats or claims like "recommended by dentists"
In 2007 the UK advertising watchdog banned Colgate's claim that "80% of dentists recommend" it, because the survey let dentists name several brands.
!! Milgram proved most people would kill on command => The setting, the doubts of participants and the pressure used all mattered; obedience varied hugely between versions
> Before deferring, ask whether the person has real expertise in this question, or just the costume.

# Foot in the door
Start small, then ask for more. Once someone has agreed to a tiny request, they are more likely to agree to a bigger, related one later.
### The yard sign study
In 1966 Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser asked Californian homeowners to put a large, ugly "Drive Carefully" sign on their front lawn. Most refused. But people who, two weeks earlier, had agreed to put a small 8 cm sticker in their window were far more likely to say yes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% agreeing to the big sign", "items": [{"l": "Asked directly", "v": 17.0}, {"l": "After small 'yes'", "v": 76.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it works
The psychologist Daryl Bem's **self-perception theory** suggests we learn about ourselves partly by watching what we do. "I agreed to help" becomes "I'm the sort of person who supports this cause", and turning down the next request would feel inconsistent.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Small yes", "'I'm a helper'", "Bigger request", "Yes, to stay consistent"]}
### How strong is it?
Later reviews found the effect real but usually much smaller than in the 1966 study, and it fails if the first request is too trivial or the second too big. It works best when people freely choose the first step and the two requests are linked.
### Where you meet it
• Free trials that turn into subscriptions
• "Can you answer one quick question?" followed by a long survey
• A charity asking you to sign a petition, then to donate
• Online shops asking for an email, then an account, then a purchase
!? A close cousin, the **low-ball** technique, gets a yes to a good deal and then changes the terms. Many people stick with their decision even though the reason for it has gone.
> A small yes can quietly change how you see yourself, and that self-image makes the next yes easier.

# Door in the face
This is the reverse of foot in the door. The persuader first asks for something large that you are almost certain to refuse. Then they "retreat" to the request they wanted all along, which now seems modest.
### The zoo trip
In a 1975 study led by Robert Cialdini, students on a campus were asked to chaperone young offenders on a two-hour trip to the zoo. Asked directly, about 17% agreed. When first asked to be an unpaid counsellor two hours a week for two years, and refusing, about half then agreed to the zoo trip.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% agreeing to zoo trip", "items": [{"l": "Asked directly", "v": 17.0}, {"l": "After big refusal", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Huge request", "You refuse", "They back down", "You 'meet halfway'"]}
• **Reciprocal concessions**: they made a concession, so you feel you should too
• **Contrast**: next to the first request, the second looks small
• **Guilt**: saying no twice feels rude
### Does it still hold?
Many classic social psychology findings have shrunk or vanished when retested. This one has held up relatively well: a 2021 direct replication of the zoo study found an effect of similar size. It works best when the same person makes both requests, soon after each other.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Foot in the door", "items": ["small first", "then bigger", "drives: consistency"]}, "r": {"t": "Door in the face", "items": ["big first", "then smaller", "drives: reciprocity"]}}
### In real life
Salary talks, house prices and fundraising all use it. A charity letter may ask for €100 a month before suggesting "even €10 would help".
!! A higher first offer always helps => If the first request seems absurd or insincere, people feel manipulated and the effect disappears
> When someone retreats quickly to a "smaller" ask, check whether the smaller ask was the target all along.

# Liking
We prefer to say yes to people we like. That sounds obvious, but what makes us like someone can be surprisingly superficial, and persuaders know it.
### What makes us like people
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Similarity: like me", "Compliments", "Familiarity: seen often", "Cooperation: same team", "Physical attractiveness"]}
### Similarity
In a 2004 study, students were asked to read and critique an essay. When they were told they shared a birthday with the person asking, about 62% agreed, compared with 34% otherwise. Similar names, home towns and hobbies have shown similar effects.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% agreeing to the favour", "items": [{"l": "No link", "v": 34.0}, {"l": "Same birthday", "v": 62.0, "hi": true}]}
### Familiarity
Robert Zajonc showed in 1968 that simply seeing something repeatedly makes people like it more, the **mere exposure effect**. Brand logos, jingles and politicians' faces on posters rely on it.
### The halo effect
One appealing trait colours our view of everything else about a person. Attractive people are often judged as kinder and more competent, which helps in sales, job interviews and even court.
### In the sales room
• Tupperware-style parties, where the seller is a friend
• Salespeople finding common ground: "Oh, you're from Braga too?"
• Car salesman Joe Girard, who sent every customer a card each month saying "I like you"
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "I like the seller", "r": "Is the deal good alone?"}]}
!? Compliments can work even when we know they are insincere: a flattering remark can leave a lasting good feeling after we have consciously dismissed it.
> Separate the person from the offer: ask whether you'd still say yes if a stranger made it.

# Stories beat statistics
In a 2007 study, Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic gave people a chance to donate to Save the Children. Some read about Rokia, a seven-year-old girl in Mali. Others read statistics about millions facing hunger in Africa.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average donation, US$", "items": [{"l": "Statistics only", "v": 1.14}, {"l": "Rokia's story", "v": 2.38, "hi": true}, {"l": "Story + statistics", "v": 1.43}]}
Adding the numbers to the story actually reduced giving. The facts seemed to switch people into a cooler, calculating mood.
### The identifiable victim effect
The economist Thomas Schelling noticed in 1968 that one named child in danger draws more help than many anonymous ones. Paul Slovic calls the opposite **psychic numbing**: as numbers grow, feelings fade.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["One named child", "Strong feeling", "Large numbers", "Feeling fades"]}
### How solid is it?
The effect is real but usually smaller than in the famous studies. A 2016 meta-analysis found a small average effect that varies a lot between studies, depending on how the victim is presented.
### Why stories work
• They trigger emotions and mental images, numbers rarely do
• We are drawn into a narrative and argue with it less
• One person feels helpable; millions feel hopeless
In 2015 the photograph of a drowned Syrian boy, Alan Kurdi, led to a short, sharp spike in donations to refugee charities, which faded within weeks.
!! "One death is a tragedy, a million a statistic" is a proven Stalin quote => It's widely attributed to him, but there's no reliable source that he said it
> Stories move hearts; statistics show scale. Good communicators use the story to open the door and numbers to fill the room.

# Changing minds
When someone holds a belief tied to identity, values or group, piling on facts often feels like an attack. People defend themselves with **motivated reasoning**: they search harder for flaws in unwelcome evidence.
### The backfire effect that wasn't
A 2010 study suggested corrections could make people believe a myth more. Large follow-up studies in 2019 found this "backfire" is rare. Corrections usually shift beliefs a little in the right direction, even if they don't change votes or loyalties.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Arguing", "items": ["lists facts", "feels like an attack", "both dig in"]}, "r": {"t": "Listening", "items": ["asks questions", "respects values", "room to reflect"]}}
### Deep canvassing
In 2016 David Broockman and Joshua Kalla found that a 10-minute door-to-door conversation, where canvassers listened, shared stories and asked people to reflect on their own experiences, reduced prejudice against transgender people for at least three months. Their study came after an earlier, similar claim was retracted as fraudulent, which made the careful replication all the more notable.
### Techniques that help
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ask, don't tell", "Find shared values", "Ask how it works in detail", "Let them save face"]}
• Asking people to explain exactly how a policy would work can soften extreme views, because they discover gaps in their own understanding
• **Moral reframing**: present an argument in terms of the other person's values, such as fairness, loyalty or freedom
• Keep the relationship: people change their minds later, in private, not in the middle of an argument
!? In a 2024 study in *Science*, conversations with an AI chatbot that answered each person's specific evidence reduced belief in a chosen conspiracy theory by about 20%, and the effect lasted at least two months.
> To change a mind, make it safe to change: listen first, then give facts a respectful way in.

# Dark patterns
The designer Harry Brignull coined the term **dark patterns** in 2010 for interface designs that trick people into doing things they didn't mean to, such as buying extras, sharing data or staying subscribed.
### Common tricks
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Roach motel", "b": "easy to join, hard to cancel"}, {"a": "Sneak into basket", "b": "extras added for you"}, {"a": "Confirmshaming", "b": "'No, I hate saving money'"}, {"a": "Hidden costs", "b": "fees appear at checkout"}]}
They exploit the same mental shortcuts as other persuasion: we accept defaults, we skim, and we hate losing what we already have.
### How common are they?
A 2019 Princeton study crawled about 11,000 shopping websites and found dark patterns on more than 1,200 of them. In a 2022 EU sweep, authorities found manipulative design on 148 of 399 online shops checked.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "37%", "l": "EU shops in 2022 sweep"}, {"v": "11%", "l": "sites in 2019 US crawl"}]}
### What the EU bans
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2014", "l": "No pre-ticked extras"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "GDPR: real consent"}, {"y": "2019", "l": "Pre-ticked cookies void"}, {"y": "2024", "l": "Digital Services Act"}]}
• The Consumer Rights Directive bans pre-ticked boxes that add paid extras
• In 2019 the EU Court of Justice ruled that a pre-ticked cookie box is not valid consent
• The Digital Services Act forbids online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users
Enforcement lags behind, so many tricks persist, and new ones appear in apps and games.
!! If I clicked it, I agreed => Under EU law, consent obtained by deceptive design or pre-ticked boxes isn't valid consent
> When a website makes one choice big and bright and the other tiny and grey, the design is making the decision for you.

# Spot it in the wild
Online shops are persuasion laboratories. They test layouts on millions of visitors and keep what sells. Once you can name the tactics, they lose much of their grip.
### A tactics checklist
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Scarcity", "b": "'Only 3 left'"}, {"a": "Social proof", "b": "'Bestseller', star ratings"}, {"a": "Anchoring", "b": "'Was €99'"}, {"a": "Urgency", "b": "'Ends tonight', timers"}]}
### Check the "was" price
**Anchoring** works because the first number we see sets our sense of value. Since 2022, EU rules say a shop announcing a price cut must compare with its lowest price in the previous 30 days, to stop fake "was" prices.
ex: Headphones "was €99, now €59" | Lowest price in the last 30 days was €65 | Real saving is €6, not €40
### A simple routine
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice the pressure", "Name the tactic", "Pause a day", "Compare elsewhere"]}
• Put the item in the basket and leave it overnight
• Check price-history tools or other shops
• Ask: would I buy this at this price with no countdown?
• Read the worst reviews, not just the stars
### Social proof needs scrutiny
Reviews can be fake or selectively shown. EU law now requires shops to say whether and how they check that reviews come from real buyers, and fake reviews are banned.
!? Black Friday deals are often no better than prices a few weeks earlier. Consumer groups in Europe regularly find that many "discounts" were matched or beaten in the previous months.
> Name the tactic, then wait: urgency is a feeling the seller creates, not a fact about the product.

== ps-development

# Piaget's stages
Jean Piaget (1896–1980) was a Swiss biologist turned psychologist. In the 1920s he worked in Paris marking children's answers to intelligence tests and became fascinated by the wrong answers: children of the same age made the same kinds of mistakes. He concluded they weren't less knowledgeable adults but thinkers with a different logic.
### Four stages
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Formal operations: 11+", "Concrete operations: 7–11", "Preoperational: 2–7", "Sensorimotor: 0–2"]}
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sensorimotor", "b": "learn by touch and movement"}, {"a": "Preoperational", "b": "symbols, pretend, egocentric"}, {"a": "Concrete", "b": "logic about real things"}, {"a": "Formal", "b": "abstract, hypothetical ideas"}]}
### How children build knowledge
Piaget saw children as little scientists. They form mental models called **schemas**. New experiences either fit an existing schema (**assimilation**) or force it to change (**accommodation**). A toddler who calls every four-legged animal "dog" is assimilating; learning "cat" is accommodating.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Existing schema", "New experience", "Doesn't fit: confusion", "Schema changes"]}
### What has held up, and what hasn't
• Children really do reason differently at different ages
• But clever experiments show babies and young children know more than Piaget thought
• Development looks more gradual and uneven than neat stages suggest
• Many adults never use formal, abstract logic consistently
Piaget often based ideas on observing a few children, including his own three. Later researchers tested them on much larger groups.
!! Piaget's ages are fixed milestones => They are rough averages; children vary a lot and skills depend on the task and culture
> Children are not mini-adults with less knowledge; they build their understanding of the world step by step.

# Object permanence
**Object permanence** is knowing that things continue to exist when you can't see, hear or touch them. Adults take it for granted, but Piaget thought babies have to build it.
### What Piaget saw
If he covered a toy with a cloth in front of a 5-month-old, the baby lost interest, as if it had gone. By about 8 months, babies pulled the cloth away to find it. Peek-a-boo becomes thrilling around then, because a face that disappears and comes back is a delicious surprise.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "0–4 mo", "l": "Out of sight, forgotten"}, {"y": "~8 mo", "l": "Searches under cover"}, {"y": "8–12 mo", "l": "A-not-B error"}, {"y": "18–24 mo", "l": "Tracks hidden moves"}]}
### The A-not-B error
Hide a toy under cloth A several times; the baby finds it each time. Then, in full view, hide it under cloth B. Many 8–12-month-olds still search under A. They know objects exist, but struggle to override a habit, which links to the slow development of the brain's frontal lobes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Toy hidden at A", "Baby finds it, repeat", "Toy moved to B", "Baby still searches A"]}
### Earlier than Piaget thought?
In the 1980s Renée Baillargeon used **looking time**: babies stare longer at things that surprise them. Babies of 3–5 months looked longer when a screen seemed to pass through a hidden box, suggesting they knew the box was still there.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Searching", "items": ["needs reaching skill", "~8 months"]}, "r": {"t": "Looking time", "items": ["just watch", "hints at 3–5 months"]}}
These findings are still debated. Longer looking might reflect novelty or perception rather than real understanding, and some looking-time results have been hard to replicate.
!? Peek-a-boo is played in cultures all over the world, from Portugal's "cucu!" to Japan's "inai inai baa".
> Babies may sense that objects persist before they can act on that knowledge; reaching lags behind understanding.

# Conservation
In Piaget's sense, **conservation** means understanding that a quantity stays the same when only its appearance changes. Pour water into a taller glass, roll a ball of clay into a sausage or spread a row of coins out, and the amount hasn't changed.
### Why young children get it wrong
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Centration", "b": "focus on height, ignore width"}, {"a": "Irreversibility", "b": "can't mentally pour it back"}, {"a": "Appearance", "b": "looks more, so is more"}]}
### Different types, different ages
Piaget found children don't master all kinds of conservation at once.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical age mastered, years", "items": [{"l": "Number", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Liquid", "v": 7.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mass (clay)", "v": 8.0}, {"l": "Weight", "v": 9.5}]}
### Naughty Teddy
In 1974 James McGarrigle and Margaret Donaldson repeated the number test with a twist. Instead of the adult spreading out a row of counters, a "naughty teddy" puppet messed it up by accident. Many more 4–6-year-olds then said the number was the same.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of 4–6-year-olds conserving", "items": [{"l": "Adult moves row", "v": 16.0}, {"l": "Teddy moves row", "v": 63.0, "hi": true}]}
Their point: when an adult deliberately changes something and asks the same question twice, a child may reasonably think a different answer is expected. Part of the "failure" was about reading the social situation, not just logic. Later studies found the teddy effect smaller but still present.
!! Young children simply can't think logically => They often can, but the way a question is asked strongly affects what they answer
> Children's answers depend not only on what they understand but also on what they think the adult wants to hear.

# Theory of mind
**Theory of mind** is the ability to understand that other people have their own beliefs, desires and knowledge, which can differ from yours and from reality. It is the foundation of empathy, cooperation, teaching, and also lying.
### The Sally–Anne test
In 1985 Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie and Uta Frith used two dolls. Sally puts her marble in a basket and leaves. Anne moves it to a box. Sally returns. Where will she look first?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sally hides marble", "Sally leaves", "Anne moves it", "Where will Sally look?"]}
Most four-year-olds say "the basket", because Sally didn't see the move. Most three-year-olds say "the box", where it really is: they can't yet separate what they know from what Sally knows.
### The original study
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% passing in 1985 study", "items": [{"l": "Typical children", "v": 85.0}, {"l": "Down syndrome", "v": 86.0}, {"l": "Autistic children", "v": 20.0, "hi": true}]}
These were small groups, and later work showed autism is far more varied. Many autistic people pass such tests but may process social information differently, and the idea of a simple "mindblindness" is now widely criticised.
### Earlier signs?
A 2005 study suggested 15-month-olds' looking patterns showed implicit understanding of false belief. Large replication attempts since then have produced mixed results, so whether babies have this ability is an open question.
### In everyday life
• Hide-and-seek: toddlers "hide" by covering their eyes
• First deliberate lies usually appear around ages 2–4
• Surprise parties and keeping secrets need theory of mind
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~18 mo", "l": "Knows others want things"}, {"y": "~4 yrs", "l": "Passes false belief"}, {"y": "~6–7 yrs", "l": "Beliefs about beliefs"}]}
!? Children with older siblings tend to pass false-belief tests slightly earlier, perhaps because family life gives more practice in arguing, teasing and tricking.
> Around four, children discover that minds can be wrong, and that changes how they play, share and lie.

# Attachment
In the 1950s the British psychiatrist John Bowlby argued that babies are born ready to bond with caregivers, because staying close to a protector helped infants survive. This **attachment** gives a child a safe base to explore from and a safe haven to return to when frightened.
### Comfort, not just food
In the 1950s Harry Harlow raised baby monkeys with two artificial "mothers": one of bare wire with a milk bottle, one of soft cloth with none. The babies fed from the wire one but spent most of their time clinging to the cloth one, especially when scared. The experiments are now considered cruel, but they changed how people thought about childcare.
### The Strange Situation
Mary Ainsworth created a 20-minute lab procedure in which a toddler aged about 12–18 months is briefly separated from a parent and left with a stranger. How the child reacts at reunion is the key.

Proportions vary between countries and studies.
### What builds security
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Baby signals need", "Carer responds", "Baby feels safe", "Baby explores"]}
• Responsive care matters more than perfect care; repairing missed moments counts
• Babies can be securely attached to several people: parents, grandparents, nursery staff
• Attachment style in infancy predicts later life only modestly; it is not destiny
!! Parents must bond in the first hours after birth => The 1970s "critical bonding" idea wasn't supported; attachment builds over months
> Security grows from many ordinary moments of responsive care, not from getting everything right.

# Language explosion
Learning a language is one of the most impressive things a child does, and they do it without lessons. Long before the first word, babies are listening hard.
### Before words
Newborns prefer their mother's voice and the rhythm of the language heard in the womb. Up to about 6 months, babies can tell apart speech sounds from any language. By around 10–12 months, they lose sensitivity to many sounds not used in their home language.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~6 mo", "l": "Babbling: ba-ba-ba"}, {"y": "~12 mo", "l": "First words"}, {"y": "~18 mo", "l": "Word spurt begins"}, {"y": "~2 yrs", "l": "Two-word phrases"}, {"y": "~6 yrs", "l": "Thousands of words"}]}
### The word spurt
Children often learn words slowly at first, then much faster from around 18 months. They can pick up a rough meaning from a single exposure, called **fast mapping**. Estimates suggest a typical six-year-old understands around 10,000 words, though numbers vary widely.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. words known (estimates)", "items": [{"l": "12 months", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "18 months", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "2 years", "v": 300.0, "hi": true}]}
### Clever mistakes
Children say "I goed" or "two mouses", words they have never heard. Portuguese children may say "eu fazi" instead of "eu fiz". These errors show children are extracting rules, not just copying.
### Talking with children
A famous 1995 study claimed poorer children heard 30 million fewer words by age 3. Later work questioned the size of that gap; counting all speech around children, not just speech to them, shrinks it. What seems to matter most is back-and-forth conversation, not just the number of words heard.
• Children growing up with two languages may mix them, which is normal, and catch up quickly
!? A baby's vocal babble starts to follow the rhythms of the home language before the first year: French and Arabic babies sound subtly different.
> Children don't just store words; they work out the rules, which is why their mistakes are so clever.

# Play is learning
To adults, play can look like a break from learning. For young children, it is often how learning happens. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child recognises play as a right in Article 31.
### Kinds of play
In 1932 the American researcher Mildred Parten described how social play changes in the preschool years.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Cooperative: shared goals", "Associative: play together", "Parallel: side by side", "Solitary: alone"]}
### What play builds
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Pretend play", "b": "symbols, roles, emotions"}, {"a": "Building blocks", "b": "maths, spatial skills"}, {"a": "Rough-and-tumble", "b": "strength, self-control"}, {"a": "Games with rules", "b": "turn-taking, fairness"}]}
### Self-control through pretending
Vygotsky argued that pretend play makes children follow invisible rules. A child playing "shop" has to act like a shopkeeper, waiting, speaking politely and counting change. Holding a role takes the same self-control children later need at school.
### What the evidence says
Children who play a lot often have better language and social skills. But a 2013 review led by Angeline Lillard found that the evidence that pretend play **causes** these benefits is weaker than often claimed: playful children may simply be more advanced in the first place. Play is clearly valuable; exactly how much each type adds is still being studied.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Free play", "items": ["child leads", "creative, social"]}, "r": {"t": "Guided play", "items": ["adult sets a goal", "child explores it"]}}
### Outdoor and risky play
Climbing trees and balancing on walls help children judge risk and build confidence. Many Nordic countries build outdoor play into nursery days, whatever the weather.
!? Pretend play usually appears around 18 months, when a toddler might hold a banana as a phone. It is one of the first signs of thinking with symbols.
> Play is children's work: it is how they practise thinking, feeling and getting along with others.

# Vygotsky's zone
Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934) was a Soviet psychologist who died of tuberculosis at 37. His work was suppressed for years under Stalin and only became widely known in the West after English translations in 1962 and 1978. Where Piaget stressed the child as a lone scientist, Vygotsky stressed learning with others.
### The zone of proximal development
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Can't do yet, even with help", "ZPD: can do with help", "Can do alone"]}
The **zone of proximal development** is the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with guidance from someone more skilled. Teaching aimed there stretches the child without overwhelming them.
### Scaffolding
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Adult shows and guides", "Child does more", "Help is reduced", "Child does it alone"]}
Tying shoelaces is a classic example. A parent first does most of it, then holds one loop while the child makes the other, then just gives verbal hints. Eventually the child ties both alone, and the zone moves on to something harder.
### Private speech
Vygotsky noticed young children talk to themselves while doing difficult tasks. He saw this as thinking out loud: words learnt from adults turning into a tool for guiding oneself. Over time it becomes silent inner speech, though adults still mutter during tricky jobs.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Piaget", "items": ["child discovers alone", "development first"]}, "r": {"t": "Vygotsky", "items": ["learning with others", "learning drives growth"]}}
### In the classroom
• Pair a learner with a slightly more skilled peer
• Ask guiding questions rather than giving answers
• Remove support gradually
!! "Scaffolding" is Vygotsky's word => It was coined in 1976 by Wood, Bruner and Ross, building on his ideas
> The best teaching aims just past what a child can do alone, then gradually steps back.

# Praise effort
Carol Dweck, a psychologist at Stanford, distinguishes a **fixed mindset** (ability is set) from a **growth mindset** (ability can be developed). She argued that the way adults praise children nudges them towards one or the other.
### The 1998 praise study
Claudia Mueller and Dweck gave 10–11-year-olds a set of puzzles and then praised them either for intelligence ("you must be smart") or effort ("you must have worked hard"). They then gave everyone a much harder set.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "'You're so clever'", "items": ["chose easy tasks", "enjoyed less after failure", "did worse later"]}, "r": {"t": "'You worked hard'", "items": ["chose challenges", "kept enjoying it", "did better later"]}}
### Why it might work
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Praised for talent", "Failure means 'not clever'", "Avoid challenges", "Stop improving"]}
### What replication shows
The idea became hugely popular in schools, but the evidence is more modest than the hype.
• A 2018 meta-analysis found that mindset interventions had very small effects on grades on average
• A large 2019 US study found a small benefit, mostly for lower-achieving students
• A 2019 attempt to replicate the praise study with Chinese children found no effect
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "effect size, approx. (2018 review)", "items": [{"l": "Cohen's 'large'", "v": 0.8}, {"l": "Mindset on grades", "v": 0.08, "hi": true}]}
### Better praise
Dweck herself warned against a "false growth mindset": praising effort that isn't working. Useful praise is specific and points to **strategy**: "you tried a different method when the first one failed".
!! Just say "well done for trying" => Effort alone isn't enough; praise the process and help with strategies that work
> Praise the process, not the person, but expect modest effects, not miracles.

# Teenagers
Adolescence is not childhood with attitude. The brain changes a lot between about 10 and the mid-20s, and these changes make teenagers behave in predictable ways.
### Two systems maturing at different speeds
The psychologist Laurence Steinberg proposed a **dual systems** model. Brain areas that respond to rewards and social excitement become very active around puberty. The prefrontal cortex, which helps plan and hold back impulses, keeps developing into the twenties.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Reward system", "items": ["revs up at puberty", "craves excitement"]}, "r": {"t": "Control system", "items": ["matures slowly", "plans, puts brakes on"]}}
### The audience effect
In a 2005 driving-game study, teenagers took far more risks when friends were watching; adults barely changed. Brain scans later showed peers boost activity in reward areas. Even adolescent mice drink more alcohol in company.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "risk-taking (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Teen alone", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Teen with friends", "v": 8.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Adult with friends", "v": 4.5}]}
This is why many countries have restrictions for new young drivers.
### Sleep shifts too
Melatonin, the sleep hormone, is released up to about two hours later in teenagers than in younger children. They are not lazy for getting up late: their body clocks have shifted. Several studies suggest later school start times improve sleep and attendance.
### A time of opportunity
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Fast learning and creativity", "Strong drive to explore", "Sensitivity to fairness", "Building identity and friendships"]}
The same sensitivity to peers and rewards helps teenagers learn, take healthy risks such as trying out for a team, and leave home.
!! The brain is finished at 25 => There is no sudden finishing line; development slows gradually and the brain changes throughout life
> Teenage risk-taking is a feature of a developing brain built to explore, best guided rather than just blamed.

== ps-personality

# The Big Five
The Big Five grew out of the **lexical hypothesis**: if a trait matters in daily life, language will have a word for it. In 1936 Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert found about 18,000 personality words in an English dictionary. Later researchers used statistics (factor analysis) to see which words cluster together, and the same five broad groupings kept appearing.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Openness", "b": "curious, imaginative"}, {"a": "Conscientiousness", "b": "organised, dependable"}, {"a": "Extraversion", "b": "sociable, energetic"}, {"a": "Agreeableness", "b": "warm, cooperative"}]}
The fifth is **Neuroticism**, the tendency to feel anxiety, sadness and irritation easily. Its opposite end is often called emotional stability.
### Why scientists trust it
• It has been found in dozens of languages and cultures, including Portuguese-speaking samples
• Scores are fairly stable over years and match ratings by friends and partners
• The traits predict real outcomes: health, relationships, job performance
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Each trait", "items": ["has facets", "is a spectrum", "predicts behaviour"]}
### Facets inside each trait
Each broad trait splits into narrower facets. Extraversion, for example, includes warmth, assertiveness, activity level and a taste for excitement. Two people with the same extraversion score can differ in which facets drive it.
### Not the only model
The HEXACO model adds a sixth trait, Honesty–Humility, which captures sincerity and fairness better than the Big Five does. Most researchers see the two models as close relatives rather than rivals.
> The Big Five are not invented boxes but patterns found again and again in how people describe each other.
!? The traits were discovered from everyday words, not from theory, which is partly why they travel so well across cultures.

# Spectrums, not types
Personality scores follow roughly a bell curve. Most people cluster in the middle, and very few sit at the extremes. A "type" system cuts that curve in half and labels everyone on each side as different kinds of people.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of people (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Very low", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Low", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Middle", "v": 40.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "High", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Very high", "v": 5.0}]}
### The Myers–Briggs problem
The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) was built in the 1940s by Katharine Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers, inspired by Carl Jung's ideas. It sorts people into 16 four-letter types such as INTJ or ESFP.
• **Test–retest**: in several studies, a large share of people (often quoted as up to about half) get a different type when retested weeks later
• **False splits**: someone just above the midpoint and someone just below get opposite letters, though they are nearly identical
• **Prediction**: types predict job performance poorly compared with Big Five scores
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Type model", "items": ["16 boxes", "middle is split", "labels feel fixed"]}, "r": {"t": "Trait model", "items": ["continuous scores", "middle is normal", "shows degree"]}}
### Why it stays popular
The MBTI is easy to remember, flattering (no type is "bad") and gives teams a shared vocabulary. That can make it a pleasant conversation starter. The problems start when it is used to hire, promote or rule people out.
> Personality is a matter of degree, and most of us are somewhere in the middle.
!! Your MBTI type reveals who you really are => Type labels are unstable and split people who are almost the same

# Introverts and extraverts
Hans Eysenck proposed in the 1960s that extraverts have lower baseline brain arousal, so they seek stimulation to feel their best, while introverts are already near their comfortable level. Evidence for this exact mechanism is mixed, but the pattern of preferring more or less stimulation holds up well.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "extraversion", "items": [{"l": "Introvert", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Ambivert", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Extravert", "v": 8.0}]}
### Introversion is not shyness
• **Introversion** is about preference: quieter settings, fewer people, time alone to recharge
• **Shyness** is about fear: worry about being judged
• An introvert can be a confident public speaker; an extravert can be shy and still crave company
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Introvert", "items": ["prefers calm", "recharges alone", "can be confident"]}, "r": {"t": "Shy person", "items": ["fears judgement", "avoids attention", "may want company"]}}
### Most people are ambiverts
Because extraversion is a spectrum, most people sit near the middle and can shift with the situation. Sales research by Adam Grant (2013) found that moderate extraverts often sold slightly more than strong extraverts or introverts, though the effect was modest.
### A surprising finding
In "act extraverted" experiments, people asked to behave sociably for a while usually report feeling happier, introverts included. Some later studies suggest introverts also feel more tired afterwards, so the cost depends on how long it lasts.
> Introversion is about where you get your energy, not about fear of people.
!? Extraversion is one of the most reliable predictors of positive mood across cultures, yet introverts report similar life satisfaction when their lives fit their preferences.

# Conscientiousness pays
Conscientiousness combines self-discipline, orderliness, dutifulness and a drive to achieve. Large reviews of workplace studies find it predicts job performance in almost every type of job, not just office work.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Better grades at school", "Better job performance", "Healthier habits", "Longer life on average"]}
### The longevity link
The Terman study followed about 1,500 gifted Californian children from 1921 for their whole lives. Howard Friedman and Leslie Martin found that the most conscientious children tended to live longest. A 2007 review by Brent Roberts and colleagues found conscientiousness predicts mortality about as well as social class or IQ.
### Why it works
• Conscientious people keep appointments, take medicines and wear seatbelts
• They save more and get into less debt
• They choose healthier routines and stick to them
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plans and self-control", "Good daily habits", "Small gains add up", "Better outcomes"]}
### Some caveats
The correlations are real but modest: conscientiousness explains a slice of success, not most of it. Very high levels can tip into perfectionism or rigidity. And it matters more for steady, structured tasks than for jobs that need improvisation.
> Conscientiousness wins through many small, boring good habits that compound over decades.
!! Intelligence matters far more than character for success => Conscientiousness predicts grades and job performance about as strongly as many ability measures

# Genes and environment
Twin studies compare identical twins, who share almost all their genes, with non-identical twins, who share about half. If identical twins are more alike in a trait, genes are likely involved. This gives an estimate called **heritability**.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of personality differences (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Genes", "v": 45.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Shared family", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Unique experience", "v": 50.0}]}
### What heritability does not mean
Heritability is about differences between people in a group, not about one person. Saying a trait is 45% heritable does not mean 45% of your personality came from your parents' genes.
### The surprise: shared family matters little
Growing up in the same home makes siblings only slightly more alike in personality. Adopted siblings raised together end up barely more similar than strangers. Most environmental influence is **non-shared**: different friends, teachers, illnesses, chance events and how each child experiences the same family.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Non-shared environment", "items": ["friends and peers", "chance events", "different roles at home"]}
### Many genes, tiny effects
No single "personality gene" exists. Genome studies suggest thousands of variants each nudge a trait by a very small amount. Genes also shape which environments people seek, so nature and nurture keep interacting.
> Genes set tendencies, but most of what shapes personality is experience unique to each person, not the family home.
!! Parents mould their children's personality => Shared upbringing explains surprisingly little; unique experiences and genes explain more

# Personality changes
Studies following thousands of people over decades show clear average shifts with age. A 2006 review by Brent Roberts and colleagues combined about 90 long-term studies and found most change happens between 20 and 40.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Conscientiousness rises", "Agreeableness rises", "Emotional stability rises", "Openness dips slowly in later life"]}
### Why it happens
The **social investment** idea suggests adult roles push change: a job, a partner, children and responsibilities reward reliability and calm. People who take on these roles earlier often mature earlier too.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["New adult roles", "New expectations", "New habits", "Shifted traits"]}
### Rank order versus level
Two kinds of stability are different. Your rank among your peers stays fairly stable, so the most outgoing 25-year-old is still likely to be relatively outgoing at 60. But the average level of the whole group shifts.
### Can you change on purpose?
Studies by Nathan Hudson and colleagues found that people who set concrete weekly goals, such as "start a conversation with a colleague", showed small but measurable trait changes over a few months. Therapy for anxiety or depression also tends to lower neuroticism.
> Personality is stable enough to be "you" but flexible enough to grow, and it usually grows kinder and calmer.
!? Some long-term studies find the correlation between personality at 14 and at 77 is close to zero, suggesting very long-term change can be large.

# Situations matter too
In 1968 Walter Mischel argued that traits predict behaviour in any single situation surprisingly weakly. This sparked the **person–situation debate**, which ran for about 20 years.
### How it was resolved
• A single act is noisy; averaged over many situations, traits predict behaviour well
• People show stable **if–then** patterns: "if criticised, gets defensive; if praised, relaxes"
• Strong situations, like a funeral or a job interview, make everyone behave alike
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strong situation", "items": ["clear rules", "everyone similar", "traits hidden"]}, "r": {"t": "Weak situation", "items": ["few rules", "people differ", "traits show"]}}
### Personality as a range
William Fleeson's experience-sampling studies asked people to report their behaviour several times a day. Most people showed almost their whole possible range of extraversion within a week. Their personality was best seen as the average and the spread of their behaviour, not a fixed setting.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "Behaviour = P × S", "items": [{"s": "P", "m": "person: traits, goals"}, {"s": "S", "m": "situation: rules, people"}]}
This formula is shorthand for Kurt Lewin's 1936 idea that behaviour depends on both the person and the environment.
> Traits tell you how someone usually behaves; the situation tells you how they behave right now.
!! People are either consistent or they aren't => Behaviour varies a lot moment to moment, yet the average pattern is stable

# Barnum statements
The name comes from circus showman P. T. Barnum, who supposedly had "something for everyone". The effect is also called the **Forer effect**, after a 1948 classroom study.
### The Forer experiment
Bertram Forer gave his students a "personal" personality profile, supposedly based on a test they had taken. Every student actually received the same text, assembled largely from a horoscope book.
ex: Students rate how well the profile fits them, 0–5 | Average rating: about 4.3 | Everyone got identical text
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Vague and flattering", "Two-sided (\"at times...\")", "True of almost anyone", "Seemingly from an expert"]}
### Why we fall for it
• We remember the hits and forget the misses
• Flattering statements are easier to accept
• Two-sided claims ("you can be outgoing, but also reserved") can't be wrong
• Trust in the source makes us read in meaning
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "It describes me exactly", "r": "Would it fit my friend too?"}]}
### Spotting it
A useful test is to swap the reading with a friend's. If both feel accurate, the statements are Barnum ones. Real Big Five reports give specific scores that clearly differ between people.
> If a description would fit almost anyone, it isn't really about you.
!? The effect has been replicated many times since 1948, and it works on astrology fans and sceptics alike.

# Dark triad
Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams named the **dark triad** in 2002. All three traits share a core of callousness and putting oneself first, but each has its own flavour.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Narcissism", "b": "grandiosity, need admiration"}, {"a": "Machiavellianism", "b": "cold, strategic manipulation"}, {"a": "Psychopathy", "b": "impulsive, little remorse"}]}
### Traits, not diagnoses
In this research the traits are measured in ordinary people with questionnaires such as the 12-item "Dirty Dozen". Everyone has some level of each. High scorers are not necessarily clinical narcissists or psychopaths, which are rarer diagnoses.
### What they predict
• Short-term success: confident first impressions, sometimes faster promotion
• Long-term costs: broken trust, conflict, counterproductive work behaviour
• More cheating, aggression and risky choices on average
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Short term", "items": ["charming", "self-promoting", "bold"]}, "r": {"t": "Long term", "items": ["distrust", "conflict", "burned bridges"]}}
### A fourth member?
Some researchers add everyday **sadism**, enjoying others' suffering, to make a "dark tetrad". Others argue all of these overlap so much that they are mainly low Honesty–Humility from the HEXACO model.
> Dark traits often look good at first meeting and cost more the longer you know someone.
!! Psychopaths are violent criminals => Most people with high psychopathy scores are not violent; the trait is about callousness and impulsivity

# Knowing your style
Personality research gives practical advice: rather than hoping to become a different person, change your surroundings so your natural tendencies lead to good results. Economists call this **choice architecture**.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Low conscientiousness", "b": "automate, use reminders"}, {"a": "High neuroticism", "b": "plan buffers, routines"}, {"a": "Introvert", "b": "schedule recovery time"}, {"a": "High openness", "b": "build in variety"}]}
### Systems beat willpower
• Set up an automatic transfer to savings on payday
• Put the gym bag by the door the night before
• Use one shared calendar with alerts
• Break big tasks into tiny first steps
ex: Goal: save €100 a month | Willpower plan: transfer "when I remember" | System: standing order on day 1 of each month
### Work with your traits
Someone high in openness may abandon routines that feel dull, so rotating tasks helps. Someone low in agreeableness can be an excellent negotiator but may need to soften feedback deliberately.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Know your traits", "Spot the weak point", "Design a system", "Let the system work"]}
> You don't need a new personality; you need an environment designed for the one you have.
!? Research on habits suggests highly self-controlled people succeed less by resisting temptation and more by arranging life so they meet fewer temptations.

# Pavlov's dogs
Ivan Pavlov was a Russian physiologist studying digestion, and won the 1904 Nobel Prize for that work. He noticed his dogs began to salivate before food arrived, when they heard the assistants or equipment. He turned that nuisance into the study of **classical conditioning**.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Food → salivation", "Bell + food, repeated", "Bell alone", "Bell → salivation"]}
### The vocabulary
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Unconditioned stimulus", "b": "food: works naturally"}, {"a": "Neutral stimulus", "b": "bell: means nothing yet"}, {"a": "Conditioned stimulus", "b": "bell after pairing"}, {"a": "Conditioned response", "b": "salivating to the bell"}]}
### Extinction and recovery
If the bell rings again and again with no food, salivation fades: **extinction**. But after a rest, the response can briefly return, called spontaneous recovery. The learning was suppressed, not erased.
### In everyday life
• The smell of a hospital can make some people anxious
• A phone notification sound triggers a jolt of attention
• Feeling sick at the sight of a food that once made you ill
Food aversion is special: one pairing can be enough, even with hours between eating and feeling sick. John Garcia showed this in rats in the 1960s.
> Your body learns to react to signals that predict important events, often without your awareness.
!? Pavlov mostly used a metronome and other sounds; the famous bell appears less in his own writing than in popular retellings.

# Operant conditioning
Classical conditioning is about reflexes triggered by cues. **Operant conditioning** is about voluntary actions shaped by their consequences. B. F. Skinner built on Edward Thorndike's 1898 "law of effect" and designed a box where a rat or pigeon could press a lever for food.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Positive reinforcement", "b": "add good thing → more"}, {"a": "Negative reinforcement", "b": "remove bad thing → more"}, {"a": "Positive punishment", "b": "add bad thing → less"}, {"a": "Negative punishment", "b": "remove good thing → less"}]}
### Shaping
Complex behaviour is built step by step by rewarding closer and closer approximations. Skinner taught pigeons to turn in circles and even play a simple form of ping-pong this way. Animal trainers use clickers to mark the exact moment of a correct action.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Reward a small step", "Raise the bar", "Reward a closer step", "Full behaviour"]}
### Reward beats punishment
Punishment can stop a behaviour quickly, but it says what not to do rather than what to do. It can also cause fear, hiding the behaviour or resentment. Reinforcing a better alternative usually works more reliably.
!! Negative reinforcement means punishment => It means removing something unpleasant, like a seatbelt beep stopping when you buckle up
> Behaviour that pays off gets repeated, so to change behaviour, change what follows it.

# Variable rewards
Skinner tested different **schedules of reinforcement**: rewarding every response, every tenth, after a fixed time or at random intervals. The most persistent behaviour came from rewards on a variable ratio, where the next one could come at any moment.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fixed ratio", "b": "every 10th press: piecework"}, {"a": "Variable ratio", "b": "random count: slot machine"}, {"a": "Fixed interval", "b": "set time: monthly salary"}, {"a": "Variable interval", "b": "random time: checking email"}]}
### Why unpredictability hooks us
Dopamine neurons respond most to rewards that are better than expected, a "prediction error". When rewards are unpredictable, every check carries the possibility of a surprise. And because pauses between rewards are normal, stopping rewards does not quickly signal that the game is over, so behaviour resists extinction.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Urge to check", "Check", "Sometimes a reward", "Expectation grows"]}
### Where you meet it
• Slot machines and loot boxes in video games
• Pull-to-refresh feeds and notifications
• Fishing, lotteries and online auctions
Several European countries have looked at regulating loot boxes; Belgium treated paid loot boxes as gambling in 2018.
> Uncertain rewards are the strongest glue for habits, which is why the most addictive designs keep you guessing.
!? A slot machine "near miss" activates reward areas of the brain similarly to a win, even though you lost.

# Learning by watching
In 1961 Albert Bandura at Stanford showed preschool children an adult hitting, kicking and shouting at an inflatable Bobo doll. Children who saw this were later much more likely to attack the doll in the same ways than children who saw a calm adult.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Child watches adult", "Adult hits Bobo doll", "Child left with doll", "Child copies actions"]}
### Four steps of observational learning
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Attention: notice the model", "Retention: remember it", "Reproduction: able to do it", "Motivation: reason to do it"]}
### Vicarious reinforcement
In a 1965 follow-up, children who saw the adult punished for aggression copied less. But when offered a reward, they could all reproduce the actions. Learning had happened; the consequences shown affected whether they performed it.
### Limits of the study
A Bobo doll is designed to be hit and bounce back, so hitting it may not equal real aggression towards people. Links between violent media and real violence remain debated. Still, the basic finding that people learn by watching is very well established.
### Everyday examples
• Apprentices learning a craft by watching a master
• Children picking up accents and table manners
• Learning a recipe from a cooking video
> We don't have to experience consequences ourselves to learn; watching others is enough.
!! We only learn from our own rewards and punishments => Much learning happens by observing others and what happens to them

# Deliberate practice
In 1993 Anders Ericsson and colleagues studied violinists at a Berlin music academy. The best had built up roughly 10,000 hours of solitary practice by age 20, and lesser players far fewer. Writer Malcolm Gladwell popularised this as the "10,000-hour rule" in 2008.
### What makes practice deliberate
• Targets a specific weakness just beyond current skill
• Gets fast, accurate feedback, often from a coach
• Requires full concentration, so sessions are short
• Is often not enjoyable
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Plain repetition", "items": ["comfortable tasks", "autopilot", "little feedback"]}, "r": {"t": "Deliberate practice", "items": ["targets weaknesses", "full focus", "constant feedback"]}}
### What later research found
A 2014 meta-analysis by Brooke Macnamara and colleagues found deliberate practice explained about a quarter of performance differences in games, about a fifth in music and less in sport and professions. Practice matters a lot, but so do starting age, genes, working memory and good teaching.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of skill differences explained (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Games", "v": 26.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Music", "v": 21.0}, {"l": "Sports", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "Education", "v": 4.0}]}
### The number is not magic
Ericsson himself said 10,000 was an average for one group at one age, not a threshold. Some chess players reached master level with about 3,000 hours; others never did after 20,000.
> How you practise matters more than how long you practise.
!! 10,000 hours makes anyone an expert => Hours vary hugely between fields and people, and quality of practice matters most

# Interleaving
**Blocked** practice means doing one type of problem repeatedly (AAA BBB CCC). **Interleaved** practice mixes them (ABC BCA CAB). Interleaving feels harder and slower, yet tests days later usually show better results.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Blocked", "items": ["AAA BBB CCC", "feels fluent", "weaker later"]}, "r": {"t": "Interleaved", "items": ["ABC BCA CAB", "feels harder", "stronger later"]}}
### A classic study
In a 2007 study by Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor, students learned to calculate volumes of different solids. Blocked learners did better during practice, but one week later the interleaved group scored about three times higher.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% correct one week later", "items": [{"l": "Blocked", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Interleaved", "v": 63.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it works
• You must work out which method fits, not just repeat the last one
• Comparing problem types side by side highlights their differences
• It naturally spaces out practice of each type
### Where it helps
Maths, sport skills, music and even telling painters' styles apart have shown benefits. It works best when the mixed types are similar enough to be confused. Mixing completely unrelated topics gives less benefit.
> Mixing problem types forces you to choose the right strategy, which is the skill real tests demand.
!? In studies, most learners believe blocked practice worked better even after their own test scores showed the opposite.

# Desirable difficulties
Robert Bjork coined the term **desirable difficulties** in 1994. His key distinction is between performance during learning and long-term learning. Things that make practice feel smooth often produce weak memories; mild struggle strengthens them.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Retrieval practice: test yourself", "Spacing: review over days", "Interleaving: mix topics", "Generation: try before told"]}
### The testing effect
In a 2006 study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, students who read a text once and then took recall tests remembered more a week later than students who reread it several times. Rereading feels productive because the text looks familiar, but familiarity is not recall.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% recalled after one week (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Reread x4", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Read + tests", "v": 61.0, "hi": true}]}
### The spacing effect
Hermann Ebbinghaus described the forgetting curve in 1885. Reviewing just as you begin to forget makes each memory last longer. Flashcard apps such as Anki schedule reviews at growing intervals for this reason.
ex: Learn on day 1 | Review on day 2, day 5, day 12 | Each gap grows as memory strengthens
### When difficulty is not desirable
The difficulty must be one you can overcome. A text in a foreign language you don't know is just hard, not helpful.
> If studying feels too easy, you may be learning less than you think.
!! Rereading and highlighting are effective study methods => They feel good but are among the weakest; self-testing and spacing work far better

# Learning styles myth
The idea is called the **meshing hypothesis**: teaching matched to a student's preferred style (often VAK: visual, auditory, kinaesthetic) should help them learn more. Surveys have found that over 80% of teachers in several countries believe it.
### What the evidence says
In 2008 Harold Pashler and colleagues reviewed the research. To test meshing properly, you need to group students by style, teach each group both ways and check whether each group learns best its own way. Very few studies used this design, and those that did mostly found no match benefit.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Myth", "items": ["match teaching to style", "visual learners see", "test labels students"]}, "r": {"t": "Evidence", "items": ["no match benefit", "fit mode to content", "everyone gains from mix"]}}
### What is true
• People do have preferences, and they can say which they like
• Preferences don't predict how well they learn each way
• The best format depends on the content: maps are visual, music is auditory, swimming is physical
• Combining words and pictures helps almost everyone (**dual coding**)
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Geography", "b": "maps and diagrams"}, {"a": "Pronunciation", "b": "listening and speaking"}, {"a": "Knot tying", "b": "hands-on practice"}]}
### Why it persists
It sounds respectful of individual differences, and labelling oneself feels insightful. Some tests sold to schools also gave it commercial momentum. The risk is wasted effort and students avoiding useful formats.
> Teach to the content, not to a learning-style label.
!? In a 2018 study by Polly Husmann and Valerie O'Loughlin, students who studied in line with their own VARK style did no better in their anatomy course.

# The Feynman technique
The method is named after Richard Feynman, the Nobel-winning physicist famed for clear teaching. He didn't write it down as a "technique"; the four-step version was popularised later, inspired by how he worked and taught.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Pick a concept", "Explain it simply", "Find the gaps", "Go back and simplify"]}
### Why it works
• **Generation**: producing an explanation is a form of retrieval practice
• **Self-explanation**: research by Michelene Chi from the 1980s showed students who explain steps to themselves learn more
• It beats the **illusion of explanatory depth**: in a 2002 study, people were confident they understood zips and toilets until asked to explain how they work
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "I recognise this, so I know it", "r": "Can I explain it without notes?"}]}
### Try it
ex: Concept: inflation | Explain: "prices rise, so each € buys less" | Stuck on "why prices rise" | Reread, then explain again with an example
### Tips
Write the explanation on paper, avoiding jargon. Use an analogy from daily life. If you must use a technical word, define it. Teaching a friend works even better because they will ask questions you didn't expect.
> If you can't explain it simply, you have found exactly what to study next.
!! Understanding a topic means being able to recognise the terms => Real understanding shows when you can explain it in your own plain words

# Learning a language
Adults can learn languages well. Children often reach native-like accents more easily, but adults usually progress faster at first because they can use strategies and already know how language works.
### Key ingredients
• **Spaced flashcards** for vocabulary: the most frequent 2,000 or so words cover most everyday speech
• **Comprehensible input**: listening and reading just above your level
• **Speaking early**: trying to say things exposes gaps that listening hides
• **Accepting mistakes**: errors are feedback, not failure
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Real conversations", "Listening and reading daily", "Core vocabulary", "Spaced short sessions"]}
### Why daily beats weekly
The spacing effect means seven 10-minute sessions beat one 70-minute session. Each short review catches memories before they fade. Sleep between sessions also helps consolidate new words.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Once a week", "items": ["1 long session", "much forgotten", "hard to restart"]}, "r": {"t": "Every day", "items": ["short sessions", "steady retrieval", "habit forms"]}}
ex: 10 min a day | × 365 days | ≈ 60 hours a year, with daily review
### A European angle
The Common European Framework (CEFR) levels A1 to C2 are used across Europe to measure progress. Portuguese learners of English benefit from shared Latin roots in much academic vocabulary, and from subtitled (rather than dubbed) TV, which research links to stronger English skills.
> Little and often, with real people and real mistakes, is the fastest route to a new language.
!? The US Foreign Service Institute estimates about 24–30 weeks of full-time study for English speakers to reach working proficiency in Portuguese or Spanish.

== ps-attention

# Limited spotlight
Your senses take in far more than you could ever handle. Your eyes alone send millions of signals a second to the brain, but you are consciously aware of only a tiny slice. Attention is the gatekeeper that decides which slice gets through.
The spotlight image comes from psychologist Michael Posner's work in the 1980s. He showed that people respond faster to things in a spot they were already attending to, even without moving their eyes. Attention can move independently of your gaze, like a beam sweeping across a dark stage.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Huge sensory input", "Attention filter", "Small focus area", "Conscious awareness"]}
### Two ways the spotlight moves
• **Top-down**: you steer it on purpose, such as searching for your keys
• **Bottom-up**: something grabs it, such as a flash, a loud bang or your name
• The two compete all day, which is why a phone buzz can hijack a carefully planned hour
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Top-down", "items": ["chosen by you", "goal-driven", "effortful"]}, "r": {"t": "Bottom-up", "items": ["grabbed by events", "stimulus-driven", "automatic"]}}
### Why filtering is good
Without filtering you would be overwhelmed by the hum of the fridge, the feel of your socks and every face in the street. The cost is that the filter works silently, so you rarely know what you have missed.
> Attention is not a camera recording everything; it is a narrow beam, and what falls outside it barely reaches you.
!? The sharp, detailed part of your vision covers only about the width of your thumbnail at arm's length; your eyes jump around three times a second to build the full picture.

# The invisible gorilla
In 1999 Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris at Harvard filmed two teams passing basketballs. Viewers had to count the passes made by the team in white. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the middle, thumped their chest and walked off, on screen for about nine seconds.
About half of the viewers did not see it. When shown the clip again, many were sure it had been swapped.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~50%", "l": "of viewers missed it"}, {"v": "9 s", "l": "gorilla on screen"}, {"v": "83%", "l": "radiologists missed one"}]}
### Why it happens
Counting passes takes up your attention. The brain filters out whatever does not match the task, here anything black or not passing a ball. This is **inattentional blindness**: failing to see something in plain view because attention is elsewhere.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Demanding task", "Attention narrows", "Unexpected object", "Never reaches awareness"]}
### Everyday consequences
• Drivers who "looked but did not see" a cyclist or motorbike
• Radiologists searching scans for nodules: in a 2013 study, most missed a small gorilla image pasted into a lung scan
• Witnesses who confidently miss key events
This finding has been repeated many times with different set-ups, so it is one of the more solid results in psychology.
> Looking is not the same as seeing: what you notice depends on what you are paying attention to.
!! If something important happened in front of me, I'd notice => Many people miss large, obvious events when their attention is busy

# The cocktail party effect
The name comes from British scientist Colin Cherry, who in 1953 studied how people follow one voice among many. He played different messages into each ear through headphones and asked people to repeat one aloud. They could do it, but afterwards they knew almost nothing about the other message, not even if it switched language.
### Your name cuts through
In 1959 Neville Moray found that people did notice their own name in the ignored ear. Later studies found about a third of people do. So the brain is not fully ignoring the other channel: it processes it lightly and lets through anything highly relevant.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Many voices", "Focus on one", "Others processed lightly", "Your name breaks through"]}
### Who notices?
Interestingly, people with a larger working memory are less likely to notice their name. In a 2001 study, about 20% of high-capacity people noticed it, compared with about 65% of low-capacity people. Better focus means better blocking.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% who noticed their name (2001)", "items": [{"l": "High working memory", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Low working memory", "v": 65.0, "hi": true}]}
### Helping the brain
• Seeing the speaker's lips makes a voice much easier to follow
• Hearing with both ears helps locate and separate voices
• Hearing loss makes noisy cafés especially tiring, which is why many people with mild hearing loss avoid them
> The brain does not switch off what you ignore; it keeps a quiet watch for anything that matters to you.
!? Restaurants with hard floors and bare walls can reach 80 decibels or more, which makes the cocktail party problem much harder for everyone.

# Multitasking is switching
When you think you are doing two demanding things at once, your brain is usually alternating between them very fast. Easy, automatic tasks can run together, such as walking and talking. But two tasks that both need language, decisions or working memory compete for the same mental machinery.
### The hidden bottleneck
Psychologists describe a "central bottleneck": the step where you choose a response can only handle one task at a time. The second task waits in line, even if only for a fraction of a second.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Can combine", "items": ["walking + talking", "music + ironing"]}, "r": {"t": "Must switch", "items": ["email + meeting", "texting + driving"]}}
### What switching costs
• Time: every switch needs a moment to reload the rules of the other task
• Errors: mistakes rise, especially when tasks are similar
• Memory: you remember less of what you did in each task
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Task A", "Save and drop A", "Load rules of B", "Task B"]}
### The driving case
Talking on a hands-free phone while driving still slows reactions, because the conversation takes attention, not just hands. Researchers David Strayer and Jason Watson found only about 2.5% of people could do both without losing performance; they called them "supertaskers".
> Doing two hard things at once usually means doing two things worse, one after the other.
!! Heavy multitaskers get better at it => There is no good evidence of that; some studies even suggest they filter distractions worse

# Switching costs
Gloria Mark at the University of California, Irvine, followed office workers through their days. Their work came in short bursts of about 11 minutes before switching projects. When a task was interrupted, it took on average about 23 minutes to get back to it, usually with two other tasks done in between.
### Attention residue
In 2009 business researcher Sophie Leroy described **attention residue**: when you leave a task unfinished, part of your mind stays with it. The next task then gets only part of your attention, and performance drops.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Deep work", "Interruption", "Other small tasks", "Back after ~23 min"]}
### Shrinking screen attention
Mark's later studies timed how long people stay on one screen before switching. It fell from about 2.5 minutes in 2004 to roughly 47 seconds in more recent years. Many of these switches are self-interruptions, not someone else's fault.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average seconds on one screen", "items": [{"l": "2004", "v": 150.0, "t": "~150"}, {"l": "2012", "v": 75.0, "t": "~75"}, {"l": "Late 2010s", "v": 47.0, "t": "~47", "hi": true}]}
ex: 6 interruptions a day | each costs ~10 minutes of refocusing | about 1 hour of focus lost daily
### Reducing the cost
• Before stopping, jot down the next step, so restarting is quicker
• Batch emails and messages at set times
• Agree "quiet hours" with your team
> Interruptions don't just take the minutes they last; they take the time needed to climb back into focus.
!? Most switches people make at work are self-started, such as checking email without a prompt.

# Phones nearby
In 2017 Adrian Ward and colleagues at the University of Texas asked about 800 people to do tests of working memory and reasoning. Some left their phone in another room, some in a bag or pocket, and some face down on the desk, silenced. Those with the phone in another room scored best, and those with it on the desk scored worst.
### The "brain drain" idea
The authors suggested that part of your mind works to *not* check the phone, using up resources you need for the task. People who felt most dependent on their phones showed the biggest effects.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "test performance (illustrative order)", "items": [{"l": "Other room", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Bag or pocket", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "On the desk", "v": 1.0}]}
### What later studies found
This is where honesty matters. Several later replications, including larger ones, found smaller effects or none at all. The idea is plausible, and it matches what many people feel, but the evidence is mixed. That is why the card says "slightly" and "some studies".
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Phones make you dumber", "r": "Small effect, mixed evidence"}]}
### Simple steps that cost nothing
• Leave the phone in another room for focused work or exams
• Use "Do not disturb" or a focus mode with only key contacts allowed
• Some schools in Portugal and across Europe now restrict phones during lessons and breaks
> Even if the effect is small, out of sight is an easy, free way to remove one source of temptation.
!! A switched-off phone can't distract you => Some studies suggest its mere presence pulls a little attention, though results vary

# Attention span myths
In 2015 a Microsoft Canada marketing report claimed humans' attention span had dropped to 8 seconds, below a goldfish's 9. Headlines everywhere repeated it. But the figures came from a website that cited no traceable research, and nobody has found the original studies.
### Problems with the claim
• "Attention span" is not one fixed number; it depends on task, interest, sleep and setting
• People who "can't focus" still watch a two-hour film or play a game for hours
• Goldfish can learn tasks and remember them for months
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Our attention span is 8 seconds", "r": "Focus depends on task and interest"}, {"w": "Goldfish forget in seconds", "r": "Goldfish remember for months"}]}
### What really has changed
What has changed is our environment. With alerts, tabs and feeds, switching is easy and constant. Studies of screen use show people switch far more often than 20 years ago. That is a habit and a design problem, not a broken brain.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Myth", "items": ["brains have shrunk", "fixed span of seconds"]}, "r": {"t": "Reality", "items": ["more triggers to switch", "focus is trainable"]}}
### Why this matters
If you believe your attention is ruined, you may give up. If you see switching as a habit shaped by your surroundings, you can change the surroundings.
> Our capacity to focus has not collapsed; we just live among far more things designed to pull it away.
!? The "8-second attention span" statistic is still quoted in talks and books, though no study behind it has ever been found.

# Flow needs focus
**Flow** is the state of being so absorbed that time vanishes and the work feels effortless. The term was coined by Hungarian-American psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, who from the 1970s interviewed climbers, chess players, surgeons and artists about their best moments.
### Conditions for flow
• Clear goals: you know what you are trying to do next
• Immediate feedback: you can tell how well it is going
• A challenge that matches your skill, slightly stretching it
• No interruptions to break the absorption
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "High challenge, low skill", "b": "anxiety"}, {"a": "Low challenge, high skill", "b": "boredom"}, {"a": "Both high and matched", "b": "flow"}]}
### Why interruptions kill it
Flow takes a while of steady, uninterrupted work to build. A single notification can break it, and then you have to climb back in. That is why calendars full of 30-minute gaps rarely produce deep, satisfying work.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clear goal", "Start, feel resistance", "Settle into task", "Absorbed: flow"]}
### Making room for it
Block two-hour slots for one task, turn off alerts and choose tasks that stretch you a bit. Many people find flow more often in hobbies than at work, simply because hobbies come with fewer interruptions.
> Flow is not luck: it appears when a clear, stretching task meets unbroken attention.
!? Csikszentmihalyi found people often reported more flow at work than in their free time, when they mostly watched TV.

# The Pomodoro technique
The method was invented by Italian student Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. He used a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato, **pomodoro** in Italian, to make himself study for short, focused bursts.
### The basic recipe
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Work 25 min", "Break 5 min", "Repeat 4 times", "Long break 15–30 min"]}
ex: 4 pomodoros | 4 × 25 = 100 minutes of focused work | plus 3 short breaks and 1 long break ≈ 2 hours 10 minutes
### Why it can help
• **Starting is easier**: committing to 25 minutes feels manageable, which beats procrastination
• Breaks are planned, so you don't need to "earn" them
• You learn how long tasks really take, counted in pomodoros
• Interruptions get written down for later instead of acted on
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Pick one task", "Set timer for 25 min", "Note distractions, don't act", "Short break, then repeat"]}
### What the evidence says
There is little direct research on Pomodoro itself, and some studies suggest the fixed 25 minutes can break flow for people deep in a task. Many users adapt it: 50 minutes on and 10 off, for example. The core principles, single-tasking and regular breaks, are well supported.
> Pomodoro's real trick is making a start feel small and making breaks part of the plan.
!? The 25-minute length did not come from research; it was simply the interval Cirillo found worked best for him.

# Attention restoration
Psychologists Rachel and Stephen Kaplan proposed **attention restoration theory** in 1989. They distinguished two kinds of attention. Directed attention is the effortful kind you use to concentrate; it tires with use. "Soft fascination" is the gentle, effortless interest that clouds, leaves or water hold.
### Why nature helps
Natural scenes hold your interest without demanding anything, giving directed attention a rest. City streets demand constant vigilance: traffic, signs, crowds.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Park", "items": ["soft fascination", "no need to be alert"]}, "r": {"t": "Busy street", "items": ["traffic to watch", "directed attention"]}}
### A classic test
In a 2008 study at the University of Michigan, people walked for about 50 minutes either in an arboretum or through downtown Ann Arbor. Afterwards those who walked in nature did better on a memory task that needs focus. Even looking at nature photos helped a little.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tiring mental task", "Walk in nature", "Attention rests", "Focus improves"]}
### How strong is it?
Later reviews support small to moderate benefits, mostly on attention tasks, but studies are often small and the effects vary. It is a helpful nudge, not a miracle cure.
• Even a 15–20 minute walk among trees may help
• A view of greenery from a window can help a little
• In Portugal, city parks like Lisbon's Monsanto or Porto's Parque da Cidade are easy options
> When concentration runs out, a walk among trees may refill it better than scrolling on your phone.
!? Japan's "forest bathing" (shinrin-yoku) promotes slow walks in forests partly for the same restorative effect.

== ps-stress

# Stress isn't the enemy
In the 1930s, Hungarian-Canadian researcher Hans Selye popularised the word "stress" for the body's response to any demand. The response is ancient: it prepared our ancestors to fight or flee.
### What happens in your body
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Brain detects a demand", "Adrenaline surges", "Cortisol released", "Energy and focus rise"]}
Within seconds, adrenaline raises your heart rate and breathing. Within minutes, cortisol releases sugar into the blood for energy. Digestion and repair are put on hold. After the challenge passes, the system normally calms down.
### Good stress and bad stress
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Short-term", "items": ["sharpens focus", "boosts energy", "then switches off"]}, "r": {"t": "Chronic", "items": ["never switches off", "poor sleep", "wears the body down"]}}
Short bursts help you perform in an exam, a match or a job interview. Chronic stress, such as long-term caring, money worries or a toxic job, keeps the system running. Over years this is linked to high blood pressure, weaker immunity, poor sleep and low mood. Scientists call this accumulated cost **allostatic load**.
### How you see it matters
Studies by Jeremy Jamieson and others suggest that seeing your pounding heart as "fuel for performance" rather than danger can improve performance and blood-flow responses. The effect is modest, but reframing is a free tool.
> Stress is a tool for meeting challenges; the problem is when it never gets switched off.
!! All stress is bad for you => Brief, manageable stress helps you perform and can build confidence

# Anxiety
Anxiety is the emotion of anticipated threat: your mind and body preparing for something that might go wrong. It is closely related to fear, which responds to a danger that is right in front of you.
### Normal or a disorder?
Feeling anxious before an exam is normal and useful. Anxiety becomes a disorder when it is out of proportion to the threat, lasts for months and makes you avoid things you need or want to do.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Everyday anxiety", "items": ["linked to real events", "passes", "manageable"]}, "r": {"t": "Anxiety disorder", "items": ["out of proportion", "lasts months", "limits your life"]}}
### Common types
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Generalised anxiety", "b": "constant worry about many things"}, {"a": "Panic disorder", "b": "sudden waves of intense fear"}, {"a": "Social anxiety", "b": "fear of being judged"}, {"a": "Specific phobia", "b": "fear of one thing, e.g. heights"}]}
### How common is it?
The World Health Organization estimated that about 300 million people worldwide had an anxiety disorder in 2019, about 4% of the population, making it the most common group of mental disorders. A national survey in Portugal in 2013 found that about one in five adults had a mental disorder in the previous year, with anxiety disorders the most frequent.
### Body as well as mind
Anxiety shows up physically: racing heart, tight chest, stomach upsets, muscle tension and poor sleep. Many people first see a doctor for these symptoms.
> Anxiety is a normal alarm system; it becomes a problem when the alarm goes off too often or too loudly.
!? Panic attacks often feel like a heart attack, and peak within about 10 minutes, though they are not dangerous in themselves.

# Avoidance feeds fear
Imagine you are afraid of lifts. You take the stairs and feel relief. That relief is a reward, so next time you avoid again. But you never learn that the lift was safe, so the fear stays, or grows.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Feel fear", "Avoid the situation", "Quick relief", "Fear grows stronger"]}
### Why avoidance is so tempting
Psychologists call this **negative reinforcement**: a behaviour becomes more likely because it removes something unpleasant. It works brilliantly in the short term and badly in the long term. Small "safety behaviours", like always sitting near the exit, work the same way.
### How exposure works
Exposure therapy means facing the feared situation gradually and staying long enough for anxiety to fall, or for the brain to learn that the feared outcome did not happen. People usually build a ladder of steps.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Ride lift alone, 10 floors", "Ride lift with a friend", "Stand in lift, doors open", "Watch lifts from the lobby"]}
### How well it works
Exposure is one of the most effective treatments in psychology, especially for phobias, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder. For specific phobias, a single long, guided session helps most people a lot.
• Go step by step, starting with something about 3–4 out of 10 in difficulty
• Stay until anxiety drops or you learn something new
• Repeat often; once is rarely enough
> Every time you avoid a fear, you teach your brain it was dangerous; every time you face it, you teach it otherwise.
!! Facing fears makes trauma worse => Gradual, planned exposure is among the best-supported treatments for anxiety

# Thinking traps
In the 1960s American psychiatrist Aaron Beck noticed that depressed patients had a stream of quick, negative "automatic thoughts" that went unquestioned. He built **cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)** around catching and testing them. His student David Burns later made a popular list of "cognitive distortions".
### Common traps
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Catastrophising", "b": "assuming the worst will happen"}, {"a": "Mind-reading", "b": "knowing what others think"}, {"a": "All-or-nothing", "b": "total success or total failure"}, {"a": "Overgeneralising", "b": "once means always"}]}
• **Should statements**: "I should never make mistakes"
• **Labelling**: "I'm an idiot" instead of "I made an error"
• **Discounting the positive**: "they were just being nice"
### Why the traps matter
Thoughts, feelings and actions feed each other. A thought like "they think I'm boring" triggers anxiety, you go quiet, people respond less, and the thought seems confirmed.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Thought: they're bored", "Feeling: anxious", "Action: go quiet", "Seems to confirm it"]}
### Everyone has them
These patterns are not signs of a broken mind. Everyone uses them sometimes, especially when tired or stressed. The aim is to notice them, not to never have them.
> Thoughts are guesses, not facts, and some guesses follow predictable, unhelpful patterns.
!? Beck first trained in psychoanalysis; his own attempts to test Freudian ideas led him to develop CBT instead.

# Challenging thoughts
Once you spot a thinking trap, CBT asks you to treat the thought like a hypothesis, not a fact. This is called **cognitive restructuring**, and it often uses a written "thought record".
### A thought record
ex: Situation: boss says "come see me" | Automatic thought: I'm going to be fired | Evidence for: she looked serious | Evidence against: good review last month | Balanced thought: she may just want an update | Anxiety drops from 80% to 40%
### Useful questions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["What's the evidence for and against?", "What would I tell a friend?", "What else could explain it?", "Will this matter in a year?"]}
The "friend" question works because we are usually kinder and more realistic about others than about ourselves.
### Balanced, not positive
The goal is not to replace "I'll fail" with "I'll be brilliant". It is to find a more accurate thought, such as "I'm prepared, and if it goes badly I can retake it". Forced positive thinking tends to bounce off.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "I'll definitely fail", "r": "I've prepared; I can cope"}]}
### Does it work?
CBT is one of the most researched therapies. It is recommended for anxiety and depression by many European health bodies. Its effects are real but moderate, and some analyses suggest they have shrunk a little since early studies, partly due to better research methods.
> You don't have to believe every thought you have; you can ask it for evidence.
!? Writing thoughts down helps more than just thinking them through, because it slows the spiral and lets you see patterns.

# Resilience
For years psychologists assumed trauma and loss usually led to lasting damage. Research by George Bonanno at Columbia University changed this. Following people after bereavement, injury or disasters, he found the most common pattern was resilience: a short dip, then a fairly steady return to normal functioning.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Resilience", "b": "brief dip, steady functioning"}, {"a": "Recovery", "b": "struggles, then slowly improves"}, {"a": "Chronic distress", "b": "stays high for years"}, {"a": "Delayed", "b": "problems appear later"}]}
### "Ordinary magic"
Psychologist Ann Masten called resilience "ordinary magic": it comes from ordinary resources, not superhuman traits. Children who thrive despite hardship usually have at least one caring adult, some skills to solve problems and a sense of hope.
### What builds it
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Purpose and meaning", "Flexible thinking", "Healthy sleep and movement", "Supportive relationships"]}
• **Relationships** are the strongest foundation
• **Flexible coping**: using different strategies for different situations
• **Purpose**: something worth getting up for
• Basic health habits: sleep, exercise and not drinking heavily
### What it is not
Resilience doesn't mean feeling no pain or never asking for help. Resilient people grieve and struggle; they just keep functioning and recover. And it can depend on the situation: someone can be resilient in one crisis and not another.
> Bouncing back is the normal human response to hardship, and it is built mainly from relationships and habits.
!! Resilient people don't feel distress => Resilience means struggling and still functioning, not feeling nothing

# Post-traumatic growth
In the 1990s psychologists Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun coined **post-traumatic growth** for positive changes people report after a major crisis. They developed a questionnaire covering five areas.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Closer relationships", "New possibilities", "Personal strength", "Appreciation of life", "Spiritual or existential change"]}
### Growth and pain together
Growth does not mean the trauma was "worth it". People can report deeper friendships and still have nightmares. Many describe it as making meaning from something they would never have chosen.
### A healthy dose of caution
Researchers have questioned how real some of the reported growth is. When people are asked to judge how much they changed, they may tell a hopeful story. Studies that measured people before and after a crisis found that reported growth often did not match measured changes. So perceived growth may partly be a coping strategy, which can still be valuable.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Perceived growth", "items": ["what people report", "helps cope"]}, "r": {"t": "Actual change", "items": ["measured before/after", "often smaller"]}}
### What seems to help
• Talking or writing about the experience over time
• Supportive people who listen without rushing to fix
• Deliberate reflection, rather than endless rumination
> Hardship can lead to growth, but growth is not required, and it does not cancel the pain.
!! Everyone should find a silver lining => Pressure to "grow" can add guilt; recovery without growth is fine

# Worry time
Worry often feels urgent: if a thought pops up, it seems you must deal with it now. **Worry postponement**, developed by Thomas Borkovec and colleagues in the 1980s, teaches the brain that worries can wait.
### How it works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Worry pops up", "Write it down", "Postpone it", "Worry at set time"]}
• Pick a fixed 15-minute slot, same time and place each day, not just before bed
• When a worry appears, jot it down in a few words, then return to what you were doing
• In worry time, go through the list: is it solvable? If yes, plan a step; if no, let it be
### Why it helps
Postponing breaks the habit of worrying everywhere. Many worries look smaller or irrelevant hours later, which shows they were less urgent than they felt. It also gives you a sense of control: you choose when to worry.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Solvable worry", "items": ["make one small plan", "take a step"]}, "r": {"t": "Unsolvable worry", "items": ["accept uncertainty", "let it pass"]}}
ex: 10 worries noted over the day | at worry time, 6 no longer matter | 2 get a plan, 2 are let go
### The evidence
Studies show worry postponement reduces how much people worry, with small to moderate effects. It is often part of CBT for generalised anxiety.
> Worries rarely need answering the moment they arrive; giving them an appointment often shrinks them.
!? Trying hard to suppress a thought often makes it come back more, so postponing works better than banning.

# Social support
Humans evolved in groups, and the presence of trusted people acts as a signal of safety. Our stress systems respond to it directly.
### Evidence from the lab
• In a study published in 2003, men gave a stressful public speech. Those who brought a supportive friend had lower cortisol, the main stress hormone
• In a 2006 brain-scanning study by James Coan, women facing the threat of a mild electric shock showed less threat-related brain activity when holding their husband's hand, more so in happier marriages
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "threat response (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Alone", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Stranger's hand", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Partner's hand", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why talking helps
Putting worries into words organises them. A friend can offer facts, a different view or practical help. Even feeling that support is available reduces stress, whether or not you use it.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Social support", "items": ["Emotional: listening", "Practical: help", "Information: advice", "Company: belonging"]}
### Isolation works the other way
Lonely people tend to see social situations as more threatening and sleep worse. Worries grow without someone to check them against reality.
### Not all support is equal
Unwanted advice or someone who turns your problem into theirs can add stress. The best support is responsive: it fits what you need, whether listening or practical help.
> A trusted person beside you is one of the most powerful stress-reducers known.
!? Simply asking "do you want advice, or should I just listen?" makes support far more helpful.

# When to get help
Most people go through periods of worry or sadness. A rough guide for when to seek professional help is when symptoms last more than about two weeks, are getting worse, or interfere with sleep, work, studies or relationships.
### Signs worth acting on
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Low mood most days for 2+ weeks", "Avoiding work, people or places", "Sleep or appetite badly changed", "Relying on alcohol to cope"]}
### Treatments that work
• **Psychological therapies**, especially CBT, help most people with anxiety and depression
• **Medication**, such as antidepressants, helps many, especially for moderate to severe symptoms
• Combining therapy and medication often works best for severe cases
• Self-help books and guided online programmes help with milder problems
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Specialist services", "Therapy and/or medication", "GP or psychologist", "Self-help and support"]}
### Where to start in Portugal
Your family doctor (médico de família) at the health centre is a common first step. SNS 24 (808 24 24 24) offers psychological counselling by phone. In an emergency, call 112. Many employers and universities also offer free confidential support.
### Breaking the stigma
Many people wait years before seeking help, often from shame. But seeing a professional for your mind is no different from seeing one for a broken leg, and earlier help usually means faster recovery.
> Asking for help early is a smart, effective step, and good treatments exist for most people.
!! Therapy is only for serious mental illness => Therapy helps with everyday struggles too, from stress to grief

== ps-relationships

# We need each other
In 2010 psychologist Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues combined 148 studies following about 300,000 people for an average of 7.5 years. People with stronger social relationships were about 50% more likely to be alive at the end of the studies. The size of that effect was comparable to well-known risks like smoking and larger than physical inactivity.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "148", "l": "studies combined (2010)"}, {"v": "~50%", "l": "higher odds of survival"}, {"v": "1 in 6", "l": "people lonely (WHO)"}]}
### How loneliness gets under the skin
• It keeps the stress system on alert, raising cortisol and blood pressure
• It disrupts sleep, so the body repairs itself less well
• Lonely people may eat worse, move less and see doctors later
• Without others, there is no one to notice when something is wrong
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Feeling isolated", "Brain on alert for threat", "Stress and poor sleep", "Higher health risks"]}
### A careful reading
Most of these studies show associations, not proof of cause: illness can also make people lonely. The "as bad as smoking" headline is a simplification. Still, the link holds after controlling for many factors, and in 2023 the World Health Organization launched a commission on social connection as a global health priority.
### Alone is not the same as lonely
Loneliness is the painful gap between the connection you want and what you have. Some people live alone and feel connected; others feel lonely in a busy marriage. Quality matters more than numbers.
> Social connection is not a luxury; for a social species, it is part of staying healthy.
!? In 2018 the United Kingdom appointed a minister for loneliness, and Japan followed in 2021.

# Attachment styles
In the 1950s British psychiatrist John Bowlby argued babies are born with a drive to stay close to a caregiver, which kept them safe in our evolutionary past. In the 1970s Mary Ainsworth tested this with the "Strange Situation": a toddler briefly separated from and reunited with their mother.
### What Ainsworth saw
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Secure", "b": "upset, then easily soothed"}, {"a": "Avoidant", "b": "seems indifferent on return"}, {"a": "Anxious", "b": "clingy, hard to calm"}]}
A fourth "disorganised" pattern, with confused or frightened behaviour, was added later.
### From babies to adults
In 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver applied the idea to romantic love. Roughly half or more of adults describe themselves as secure in most surveys.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of adults (1987 survey)", "items": [{"l": "Secure", "v": 56.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Avoidant", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Anxious", "v": 19.0}]}
### Two dimensions, not boxes
Modern researchers measure attachment on two sliding scales: anxiety (fear of rejection) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness). Most people are somewhere in between rather than one pure type.
• Styles are only moderately stable from childhood to adulthood
• A loving, steady relationship can move people towards security, sometimes called "earned security"
• Online quizzes that sort you into a type are simplified and should be taken lightly
> Early bonds give us a template for closeness, but it is a draft, not a life sentence.
!! Your attachment style is fixed for life => Attachment can shift with new relationships, therapy and experience

# The magic ratio
Since the 1970s John Gottman, with Robert Levenson, filmed couples discussing a conflict while measuring their heart rate and sweating. Gottman later ran a lab at the University of Washington in Seattle nicknamed the "Love Lab".
### Five to one
During conflict, stable couples showed about five positive interactions (interest, humour, affection, agreement) for each negative one (criticism, irritation). Couples heading for divorce were closer to one to one.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "positives per negative, during conflict", "items": [{"l": "Stable couples", "v": 5.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Couples who split", "v": 0.8, "t": "about 1"}]}
### Why negatives weigh more
Negative moments hit harder and are remembered longer than positive ones, a pattern psychologists call **negativity bias**. It takes several positives to balance one sharp remark.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Positives", "items": ["humour", "affection", "showing interest"]}, "r": {"t": "Negatives", "items": ["criticism", "eye-rolling", "raised voice"]}}
ex: A 20-minute argument with 6 cutting remarks | about 30 positive moments keeps the 5:1 balance | small gestures count: a nod, a smile, "fair point"
### A note of caution
Gottman's claims to predict divorce with over 90% accuracy were made by fitting the data after the outcomes were known, which inflates accuracy. The 5:1 ratio is a useful rule of thumb from his observations, not a precise law.
> Fights are not the problem; what matters is how much warmth surrounds them.
!? Outside conflict, Gottman reported even higher ratios in happy couples, around 20 positives to one negative.

# The four horsemen
Gottman borrowed the image of the four horsemen of the apocalypse for four communication habits that, in his studies, predicted couples splitting up. Each has an "antidote".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Criticism", "b": "attack character, not behaviour"}, {"a": "Contempt", "b": "mockery, sneering, eye-rolling"}, {"a": "Defensiveness", "b": "excuses, counter-blame"}, {"a": "Stonewalling", "b": "shutting down, silence"}]}
### Why contempt is the worst
Contempt says "I am better than you". Sarcasm, mocking and eye-rolling communicate disgust. Gottman found it the single strongest predictor of divorce, and even linked it to more illness in the partner on the receiving end.
### The antidotes
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "You never help, you're lazy", "r": "I'm tired; can you do the dishes?"}, {"w": "Yes, but you...", "r": "You're right, I did forget"}]}
• Criticism → gentle start-up: complain about the situation, not the person
• Contempt → build a habit of appreciation and respect
• Defensiveness → take responsibility for even a small part
• Stonewalling → take a break and calm down
### Flooding
Stonewalling often happens when someone is "flooded": heart rate above about 100 beats per minute, unable to think clearly. Gottman suggests a break of at least 20 minutes, doing something calming, not rehearsing arguments.
> Couples don't fail because they argue; they fail when arguments turn into contempt.
!? The horsemen also appear in friendships and workplaces; contempt in a team corrodes trust in the same way.

# Turning towards
Gottman called small attempts at connection "bids": a comment, a question, a touch, "look at that bird". Each bid can be met in three ways.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Turning towards", "b": "\"Oh, lovely! What kind?\""}, {"a": "Turning away", "b": "ignoring, eyes on phone"}, {"a": "Turning against", "b": "\"Can't you see I'm busy?\""}]}
### The six-year follow-up
In a study of newlyweds observed in an apartment-style lab, Gottman followed up six years later. Couples still married had turned towards each other's bids about 86% of the time; those who had divorced, about 33%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of bids turned towards", "items": [{"l": "Still married", "v": 86.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Divorced", "v": 33.0}]}
### Why tiny moments matter
Each response is a small deposit or withdrawal in trust. Over thousands of moments, they build a sense that "you are there for me". Couples with lots of positive deposits handle conflict better because they give each other the benefit of the doubt.
### Phones get in the way
Researchers use the word **phubbing** (phone snubbing) for ignoring someone in favour of a phone. Studies link it to lower relationship satisfaction, because each glance down can be a turning away.
• Notice bids: they are often casual or indirect
• Respond, even briefly: "Hang on, let me finish this, then tell me"
> Love is built less in grand gestures than in how you answer "look at that".
!! Big romantic gestures are what matter most => Everyday small responses predict lasting relationships better

# Proximity and familiarity
In 1950 Leon Festinger and colleagues studied friendships in Westgate, a student housing complex at MIT where rooms were assigned randomly. People were far more likely to befriend next-door neighbours than people a few doors away. People living near stairways and mailboxes made more friends.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% naming as friend (Westgate, 1950)", "items": [{"l": "Next door", "v": 41.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Two doors", "v": 22.0}, {"l": "Four doors", "v": 10.0}]}
### The mere exposure effect
In the 1960s Robert Zajonc showed that people come to prefer things they have seen more often: words, shapes, faces. In a 1992 study, four women attended a university lecture course 0, 5, 10 or 15 times, without talking to anyone. At the end, students rated the women who had attended more often more favourably.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["See someone often", "They feel familiar", "Familiar feels safe", "Liking grows"]}
### Why it works
Familiar things are easier to process, and the brain reads that ease as pleasant and safe. Seeing someone often also gives countless small chances to start talking.
### Limits
• Exposure intensifies first impressions: if you dislike someone, more contact can increase it
• Too much repetition leads to boredom
• Online life has weakened physical proximity, but shared online spaces can work similarly
> Friendship often starts not with deep compatibility, but with simply being around.
!? In a police academy where seats were assigned alphabetically, recruits' closest friends often had surnames starting with nearby letters.

# How many friends?
In the 1990s British anthropologist Robin Dunbar compared the brain size of primates with their group sizes. Extrapolating to humans, he predicted a natural social group of about 150 people, now called **Dunbar's number**.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["5: closest support", "15: good friends", "50: friends", "150: meaningful contacts"]}
### Circles of closeness
Dunbar proposed layers that grow by about three times each: around 5, 15, 50 and 150, then 500 acquaintances and 1,500 faces you can put names to. Inner circles need much more time: we spend roughly 40% of our social time on the closest five.
### Supporting evidence
Dunbar pointed to Neolithic villages, Roman army units and Christmas card lists, which often came close to 150.
### Is 150 real?
The number is contested. A 2021 reanalysis by Swedish researchers using more modern methods found the data could support group sizes anywhere from a handful to over 500. Critics also point out that culture and personality vary a lot. The layered idea is better supported than the exact figure.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Supported", "items": ["limited time and energy", "inner circles are small"]}, "r": {"t": "Contested", "items": ["exactly 150", "fixed by brain size"]}}
> We have room for only a few truly close people, so who we invest time in really matters.
!! Social media lets you have 1,000 real friends => Online lists grow, but the circles of close friends stay about as small

# Making friends as an adult
In 2018 Jeffrey Hall at the University of Kansas surveyed adults who had moved to a new city and students starting university. He asked how long they had spent with new acquaintances and how close they felt.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. hours spent together", "items": [{"l": "Casual friend", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "Friend", "v": 90.0}, {"l": "Close friend", "v": 200.0, "hi": true}]}
ex: 200 hours to a close friend | 3 hours a week together | about 67 weeks: well over a year
### Why adults find it harder
As children and students we have three ingredients that sociologists say make friendships: proximity, repeated unplanned interaction and settings where people let their guard down. Adult life with work, children and commuting removes them.
### What counts
Hall found that time spent hanging out, joking and talking deeply built closeness more than time spent working side by side.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Join a regular group or class", "Say yes to repeat invitations", "Take the initiative to suggest plans", "Share a bit more each time"]}
### Initiative matters
Studies suggest people underestimate how much others like them after a conversation, a pattern called the "liking gap". So people wait for others to call. Assuming others would welcome contact makes adults more likely to reach out and build friendships.
> Friendships are built by hours together, so regular, repeated contact beats a single great evening.
!? People consistently underestimate how much a stranger enjoyed talking to them, so the other person probably liked you more than you think.

# Active listening
The term **active listening** was coined by psychologists Carl Rogers and Richard Farson in 1957. Rogers believed people feel understood, and change more easily, when the listener shows genuine empathy and does not judge.
### The core skills
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Give full attention", "Reflect back the gist", "Ask open questions", "Hold off on advice"]}
• **Reflect**: "So you felt left out when they didn't invite you"
• **Open questions** start with what, how or tell me: "What was that like?"
• **Name feelings** tentatively: "That sounds frustrating"
### Why advice backfires
When someone shares a problem, jumping in with a fix can signal "your feelings aren't important" or "it's easy". Often the person wants to be heard first. Once they feel understood, they are much more open to suggestions.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "You should just quit", "r": "That sounds exhausting. How are you?"}]}
### Does it work?
Studies find that people who are listened to well feel less defensive, more understood and think more clearly about their own views. However, mechanically repeating words back without real interest can feel fake or annoying. Genuine curiosity is the key ingredient.
### Body language counts too
Facing the person, putting the phone away and nodding all show attention.
> The aim of listening is to understand the other person, not to win the conversation or fix it.
!? Studies suggest people who ask more follow-up questions in conversation are rated as more likeable.

# Repairing after conflict
Gottman found that about two-thirds of couples' recurring conflicts are "perpetual": they come from lasting differences in personality or values and are never fully solved. What separated happy couples was not the absence of fights but the ability to **repair** during and after them.
### What a repair looks like
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["\"I'm sorry, that came out wrong\"", "\"Can we start again?\"", "A joke or a silly face", "A touch on the arm"]}
### Example
ex: Argument about who forgot to pay a bill | voices rising | one says "OK, we're both stressed, let's pause" | the other laughs and agrees | the topic is revisited calmly later
### Why repairs sometimes fail
A repair only works if the other person accepts it. In relationships full of resentment, even a kind gesture may be read as manipulation. That's why the everyday positive "bank account" matters: it makes repairs more likely to land.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Conflict starts", "Repair attempt", "Partner accepts", "Calmer talk, trust grows"]}
### After the fight
• Revisit when calm: what did each of you feel and need?
• Own your part, even if small
• Agree on one thing to try differently next time
These skills help with friends, family and colleagues too, not just couples.
> Strong relationships are not those that never break, but those that are good at fixing the breaks.
!! Happy couples don't argue => They argue too, but they repair quickly and keep warmth during conflict

== ps-perception

# The brain predicts
In the 1860s German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz suggested perception involves "unconscious inference": the brain guesses what is out there from incomplete clues. Modern neuroscience has turned this into **predictive processing**.
### How it works
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Brain predicts the scene", "Senses send data", "Compare: any error?", "Update the prediction"]}
The brain constantly generates predictions about incoming signals. Senses mostly report the "prediction errors", the parts that don't fit. If your guess was right, little needs to change; if not, the model updates.
### Why build things this way?
• **Speed**: nerve signals take time; predicting lets you act without waiting
• **Efficiency**: sending only surprises saves energy
• **Gaps**: the world is noisy, dark or partly hidden, and guesses fill in the missing parts
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Camera idea", "items": ["records everything", "passive"]}, "r": {"t": "Prediction idea", "items": ["guesses, then checks", "active"]}}
### Everyday evidence
You can't tickle yourself well because your brain predicts the sensation. A step that isn't there jolts you because your foot expected ground. You "hear" your phone vibrate when it didn't.
British neuroscientist Anil Seth describes perception as a "controlled hallucination": your brain's best guess, kept in check by the senses.
> What you see is your brain's best guess about the world, constantly corrected by your senses.
!? Many people feel "phantom" phone vibrations; surveys of students and doctors found most had experienced them.

# The blind spot
Light-sensitive cells cover the retina at the back of each eye, except in one place: where the optic nerve and blood vessels leave the eye, called the optic disc. There are no receptors there at all, so that patch of your visual field is blind.
### How big is it?
The blind spot covers about 5–7 degrees of your view, off to the side of where you look. That's large enough to make a person's head disappear at a few metres.
ex: Draw a cross and, 8 cm to its right, a dot | close your left eye and stare at the cross with your right eye | move the page slowly towards you | at about 25–30 cm the dot vanishes
### Why you don't notice
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Two eyes cover each other's gap", "Eyes move constantly", "Brain fills in the pattern", "Gap is away from the centre"]}
When you test with one eye on a patterned background, you don't see a black hole: you see the pattern continue. The brain fills in with what is most likely to be there.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["No signal from optic disc", "Brain uses surroundings", "Fills in a best guess", "No hole is seen"]}
### A 17th-century party trick
French scientist Edme Mariotte discovered the blind spot in the 1660s. It soon became a parlour game: lining up a person's head with the blind spot so it seemed to vanish.
> Your vision has a hole in it all the time, and your brain quietly paints over it.
!? Octopuses have no blind spot: in their eyes the nerves run behind the retina, so nothing has to pass through it.

# Optical illusions
German psychiatrist Franz Carl Müller-Lyer published his famous illusion in 1889. Two equal lines, one with arrowheads pointing in, the other with "fins" pointing out, look unequal. The fins version looks clearly longer to most people.
### The corner explanation
British psychologist Richard Gregory argued the shapes resemble 3D corners. Arrows pointing in look like the outside corner of a building, near you. Fins pointing out look like the inside corner of a room, further away. The brain enlarges things it thinks are further away, to keep size constant.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Arrows in", "items": ["outside corner", "seems closer", "looks shorter"]}, "r": {"t": "Fins out", "items": ["inside corner", "seems further", "looks longer"]}}
### Does culture matter?
In the 1960s, researchers found some groups living in rounded huts with few straight lines, such as San foragers in the Kalahari, were much less fooled. This "carpentered world" idea is debated, and other explanations exist, but it shows perception can be shaped by experience.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Müller-Lyer", "b": "equal lines look unequal"}, {"a": "Ponzo", "b": "converging lines change size"}, {"a": "Ebbinghaus", "b": "circles shrink among big ones"}]}
### Knowing doesn't help
Even after measuring the lines, they still look different. Your conscious knowledge cannot override early visual processing, a sign that perception is partly automatic.
> Illusions reveal the shortcuts your brain normally uses correctly.
!! Illusions show the brain is bad at seeing => They show smart rules for a 3D world being applied in the wrong place

# The dress
In February 2015 a photo of a dress, taken at a wedding in Scotland, went viral. Millions argued over whether it was blue and black or white and gold. The real dress was blue and black.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% seeing each (2015 survey, ~1,400 people)", "items": [{"l": "Blue and black", "v": 57.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "White and gold", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Blue and brown", "v": 11.0}]}
### Why it divided people
Your brain normally corrects colours for the light: a white shirt looks white in yellow lamplight and in bluish shade. This is called **colour constancy**. The photo was overexposed, with ambiguous lighting, so brains had to guess.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Assume bluish shade", "items": ["remove the blue", "see white and gold"]}, "r": {"t": "Assume yellow light", "items": ["remove the yellow", "see blue and black"]}}
### Who saw what
Studies suggested people who assumed the dress was in shadow saw white and gold. Some research found that early risers, used to daylight, and older people were slightly more likely to see white and gold, though these effects were modest.
### Why it mattered to science
For vision scientists, the dress was a gift: a rare image that people saw in completely different ways, with no right answer felt from the inside. It showed that two people can look at the same thing and have genuinely different experiences.
• Similar later viral images: "Yanny or Laurel" (sound, 2018) and grey or pink trainers
> Colour is not in the object alone; it is the brain's interpretation of light, and interpretations can differ.
!? The company that sold the dress made a one-off white and gold version for charity after the photo went viral.

# Seeing faces
Seeing faces in electrical sockets, clouds or the front of a car is called **pareidolia**. It is extremely common and harmless.
### A face detector on a hair trigger
The brain has regions specialised for faces, including the fusiform face area. They respond within about a sixth of a second. For our ancestors, missing a real face, a friend or a threat, was costlier than seeing a face in a bush.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "False alarm", "items": ["face in a cloud", "harmless"]}, "r": {"t": "Missed face", "items": ["missed a predator", "costly"]}}
### Even monkeys do it
A 2017 study showed rhesus monkeys looked longer at objects with illusory faces, just like humans. This suggests pareidolia is an old feature of primate brains, not something learned from cartoons.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Two dots and a line = a face", "Brain checks: eyes, nose, mouth?", "Emotion read in a split second", "Most often seen as male"]}
### Faces with feelings
People not only see faces; they read emotions in them, a "happy" plug, an "angry" car. A 2022 study found people tend to see illusory faces as male more often than female.
### Famous examples
• The "Face on Mars", photographed by NASA's Viking 1 in 1976, turned out to be an ordinary hill in later, sharper images
• Images of religious figures seen in toast or stains sometimes sell at auction
> Your brain would rather see a face that isn't there than miss one that is.
!! Seeing faces in objects is a sign of something wrong => Pareidolia is normal and shared by nearly everyone, even monkeys

# The McGurk effect
In 1976 British psychologists Harry McGurk and John MacDonald published a paper titled "Hearing lips and seeing voices". They had dubbed a video of a person saying "ga" with the sound "ba". Many viewers heard "da", a blend that matched neither.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ear hears \"ba\"", "Eye sees \"ga\"", "Brain combines them", "You hear \"da\""]}
### Why it happens
Speech perception is multisensory. Lip movements carry information, especially in noise. When sound and sight disagree, the brain merges them into the most plausible single syllable.
### Knowing doesn't stop it
If you close your eyes, you hear "ba" again. Open them, and for many people "da" returns. Like optical illusions, it works even when you know the trick.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sound \"ba\" + lips \"ga\"", "b": "often heard as \"da\""}, {"a": "Eyes closed", "b": "heard as \"ba\""}, {"a": "Ventriloquist", "b": "voice seems to come from puppet"}]}
### People differ a lot
The effect is not universal. Studies find some people experience it almost every time and others almost never. It also varies with language and culture; it seems weaker in some studies with Japanese and Chinese speakers.
### In daily life
This is why video calls with poor lip-sync are tiring and why people with hearing loss rely heavily on lip-reading. Face masks during the COVID-19 pandemic made many conversations harder for the same reason.
> Hearing is not just the ears: what your eyes see changes the sounds your brain reports.
!? The ventriloquist's trick uses a related effect: your brain links the voice to the moving mouth, even a puppet's.

# Change blindness
In a 1998 experiment by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin, a researcher asked passers-by on a university campus for directions. Mid-conversation, two people carrying a door walked between them, and a different researcher took the first one's place. About half the people kept giving directions without noticing the swap.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Stranger asks directions", "Door passes between them", "Stranger is swapped", "~Half don't notice"]}
### Why it happens
We don't store a detailed picture of a scene. We store the gist: "a man asking for directions". If the gist stays the same, the brain doesn't flag the change. Changes are especially easy to miss during a blink, an eye movement or a cut in a film, because those hide the "motion signal" that would grab attention.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "What we think", "items": ["full detailed picture", "notice any change"]}, "r": {"t": "What happens", "items": ["store the gist", "miss unflagged changes"]}}
### In the lab
In the "flicker" method, two nearly identical photos alternate with a brief blank between them. People can take many seconds to spot a large change, like a missing engine on a plane.
### Real-life consequences
• Film continuity errors go unnoticed by most viewers
• Drivers may miss changes in traffic after glancing at a phone
• Eyewitnesses may not notice a different person
> The brain keeps the gist of a scene, not a photograph, so big changes can slip through.
!! I'd notice if someone I was talking to changed => Many people don't, especially if the gist stays the same

# Top-down processing
Psychologists distinguish **bottom-up processing**, building perception from raw sensory details, and **top-down processing**, where knowledge and expectations shape what you perceive. Both work at once.
### The B and 13
In a 1955 study by Jerome Bruner and Leigh Minturn, the same ambiguous symbol was read as "B" between letters (A, B, C) and as "13" between numbers (12, 13, 14). Context decided what people saw.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Bottom-up", "items": ["from the senses", "details first"]}, "r": {"t": "Top-down", "items": ["from expectations", "context first"]}}
### Reading relies on it
You can read a word faster than a single letter, which is called the **word superiority effect**. It's also why you can miss a typo in your own writing: your brain reads what you meant to write.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ambiguous symbol", "b": "B among letters, 13 among numbers"}, {"a": "Familiar word", "b": "read faster than single letters"}, {"a": "Song lyrics", "b": "misheard until you read them"}]}
### When it misleads
Expectation can make you hear words in noise, see what you fear in the dark, or misread unclear handwriting. Once you know a misheard lyric's "real" words, you often can't unhear them.
### A popular myth
A viral text claims "research at Cambridge" shows we can read words with scrambled middle letters. There was no such study, though jumbled words can often be read, thanks to top-down processing, especially short and predictable ones.
> What you perceive depends not only on what's out there, but on what you expect to find.
!? Proofreading your own text is hard for this reason; reading it aloud or changing the font helps.

# Taste and expectation
In 2001 researchers at the University of Bordeaux, led by Gil Morrot and Frédéric Brochet, gave 54 wine students a white wine and the same white wine coloured red with an odourless dye. Describing the "red", the students used red-wine words like cherry and tobacco.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["See red colour", "Expect red wine", "Notice red flavours", "Describe it as red"]}
### Price changes pleasure
In a 2008 study at Caltech, people tasted the same wine labelled $10 and $90. They rated the "expensive" one more pleasant, and brain scans showed more activity in a region linked to pleasure. The label didn't just change opinions, it changed the experience.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "pleasantness rating (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Labelled $10", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Labelled $90", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}]}
### Many senses at once
• Colour: deeper-coloured drinks are often judged sweeter or stronger
• Sound: crisps taste fresher when the crunch is louder
• Weight: heavier bowls and bottles make food seem more luxurious
• Words: a "succulent Italian seafood fillet" sells better than "fish"
### Experts aren't immune
Blind tastings of wine often show surprising disagreements, even among experts. That doesn't mean wine knowledge is fake, but that flavour is built partly from expectation.
> Flavour is made in the brain from taste, smell, sight, sound and expectation together.
!? Researcher Charles Spence at Oxford has shown that high-pitched music can make food taste sweeter and low-pitched music more bitter.

# Time perception
There's no single "clock organ" in the brain. Our sense of time comes from attention, memory and emotion working together, which is why it's so stretchy.
### Fear seems to slow time
In 2007 neuroscientist David Eagleman had volunteers fall about 30 metres backwards into a net. They estimated their fall took about a third longer than others' falls. But a flickering number display on their wrists showed they did not see faster during the fall. The slow-motion feeling comes from denser memories being laid down, not from seeing in slow motion.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "In the moment", "items": ["absorbed = time flies", "bored = time drags"]}, "r": {"t": "Looking back", "items": ["new = feels long", "routine = feels short"]}}
### The holiday paradox
A new holiday flies by, yet in memory it feels long because it is packed with new memories. A routine fortnight at work drags, then seems to have vanished. Writer Claudia Hammond called this the **holiday paradox**.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fear or danger", "b": "seems slower afterwards"}, {"a": "Absorbing task", "b": "flies by"}, {"a": "Waiting, bored", "b": "drags"}, {"a": "Novel trip", "b": "feels long in memory"}]}
### Why years speed up with age
Childhood is full of firsts; adult life has more routine. Fewer new memories may make years feel shorter looking back. This is a popular explanation, but the evidence is mostly indirect.
> Time doesn't pass at a fixed speed in your mind: novelty stretches it, routine shrinks it.
!? To make a year feel longer in memory, fill it with new places, people and experiences.

== ps-creativity

# What creativity is
Most researchers use what's called the "standard definition": a creative idea is both **original** and **effective**. An idea that is new but useless is just odd; one that works but isn't new is just competent.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Novel only", "items": ["a random word salad", "a car with square wheels"]}, "r": {"t": "Novel and useful", "items": ["the first wheelie bag", "a new recipe that works"]}}
"Useful" is broad: a joke that makes people laugh, a moving poem and a clever spreadsheet formula all count. What matters is that the idea fits a purpose.
### Little c and Big C
Psychologists James Kaufman and Ronald Beghetto proposed a ladder of creativity in 2009.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Big-C: changes a field", "Pro-c: expert, professional", "little-c: everyday creativity", "mini-c: personal insight"]}
• **mini-c**: a child realising a stick can be a sword
• **little-c**: improvising dinner from leftovers
• **Pro-c**: a professional designer's new logo
• **Big-C**: Darwin's theory or Picasso's cubism
### Not just for artists
Creativity is not a special talent owned by a few. It shows up in engineering, cooking, teaching, business and parenting. Studies of creative achievement find it depends on knowledge, practice, motivation and environment as much as on raw talent.
> Creativity means ideas that are both new and useful, and most of it happens in everyday life, not in museums.
!! Creativity is a gift you either have or don't => Everyone has creative ability, and it grows with practice and knowledge

# Divergent and convergent
American psychologist J. P. Guilford launched modern creativity research in a 1950 speech, complaining psychology had ignored it. He distinguished two kinds of thinking.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Divergent", "items": ["many possible answers", "quantity, variety", "no judging yet"]}, "r": {"t": "Convergent", "items": ["one best answer", "select and refine", "judge carefully"]}}
### Measuring them
• **Divergent**: the Alternative Uses Task asks for as many uses of a brick as possible in a few minutes. Answers are scored for number, variety and originality
• **Convergent**: the Remote Associates Test gives three words, such as "cottage, Swiss, cake", and asks for the one word linking them (cheese)
### Both modes, in order
Good creative work alternates between them. Generate widely, then narrow down, then generate again. Mixing them at the same time, criticising each idea as it appears, kills the flow of ideas.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Diverge: many ideas", "Converge: pick a few", "Diverge: develop them", "Converge: choose one"]}
ex: Task: name a new café | Diverge: 40 names in 10 minutes | Converge: shortlist 5 | Test with friends: pick 1
### Design thinking
This pattern is built into many design methods. The UK Design Council's "Double Diamond" model, from 2005, draws two diamonds: widen then narrow to define the problem, then widen and narrow again to find the solution.
> Creativity needs a wide net and a sharp filter, used one after the other.
!? Standard IQ tests mostly measure convergent thinking, which is one reason they say little about creativity.

# Quantity breeds quality
Psychologist Dean Keith Simonton studied the careers of thousands of scientists, composers and artists. He found the **equal-odds rule**: each work a creator produces has roughly the same chance of being a hit. So the people with the most hits are usually those who produce the most, flops included.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~50,000", "l": "works by Picasso (est.)"}, {"v": "1,093", "l": "US patents by Edison"}, {"v": "1,000+", "l": "compositions by Bach"}]}
### Why it works
• You can't predict in advance which idea will be the good one
• Early ideas are usually the obvious ones; original ones come later
• Producing a lot builds skill and judgement
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "originality of ideas (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Ideas 1–5", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Ideas 6–15", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Ideas 16–30", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}]}
### The serial-order effect
In brainstorming tasks, later ideas tend to be rated more original than early ones. The first ideas come from the obvious, well-worn paths in memory; you have to exhaust them before finding unusual ones.
### Even geniuses miss
Simonton's work shows even great creators are poor judges of their own work. Beethoven sometimes favoured pieces later judged weaker. Edison had many failed inventions, such as a concrete house and an electric pen that sold poorly.
> To have great ideas, have lots of ideas, and don't stop at the first good one.
!! Great creators mostly produce masterpieces => They produce many ordinary works too; hits come from volume

# Incubation
In 1926 English social psychologist Graham Wallas described four stages of creative thinking, drawing on accounts from scientists like Henri Poincaré.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Preparation: hard work", "Incubation: step away", "Illumination: insight", "Verification: test it"]}
### Poincaré's bus
The French mathematician described struggling for weeks with a problem, then giving up for a geology trip. As he stepped onto a bus, the solution came to him with "perfect certainty", though he had not been thinking about it.
### What the research says
A 2009 review of 117 studies by Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod found incubation does help, especially for tasks with many possible answers. Breaks spent on light, undemanding activities helped more than complete rest or hard mental work.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Helps incubation", "items": ["walking, showering", "easy chores"]}, "r": {"t": "Helps less", "items": ["hard mental tasks", "switching to emails"]}}
### Why it might work
• You forget unhelpful approaches you were stuck on
• The mind keeps making loose associations in the background
• You return with fresh eyes and notice new clues
### Preparation first
Incubation only works after real effort. The shower idea comes to someone who has already filled their head with the problem.
> When you're stuck after real effort, stepping away can be the most productive thing you do.
!? The famous story of chemist August Kekulé dreaming of a snake biting its tail, giving the benzene ring, was told 25 years later and may be embellished.

# Walking boosts ideas
In 2014 Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz at Stanford had people do creativity tasks while sitting or walking. Walking, even on a treadmill facing a blank wall, increased the number of creative uses people came up with for everyday objects, by around 60% on average.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "creative ideas (relative, sitting = 100)", "items": [{"l": "Sitting", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Walking", "v": 160.0, "hi": true}]}
### What walking helped, and what it didn't
• It boosted **divergent** thinking: generating many ideas
• It did not help, and may slightly hurt, **convergent** thinking: finding one right answer
• The boost lingered briefly after people sat down again
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Walking", "items": ["more ideas", "freer associations"]}, "r": {"t": "Sitting", "items": ["better for focus", "finding one answer"]}}
### Why it might work
Walking is automatic and mildly pleasant. It raises arousal and mood a little and frees the mind to wander, but not so far that you lose the thread.
### Famous walkers
Charles Darwin walked a gravel path he called the Sandwalk several times a day. Beethoven took long afternoon walks with a notebook. Many leaders hold walking meetings for the same reason.
• Try a 10–15 minute walk when brainstorming
• Carry a phone or notebook to capture ideas
• Walk outside if you can, combining the benefits of movement and nature
> When you need ideas, get up and walk; when you need the one right answer, sit down.
!? In the Stanford study, about 80% of participants were more creative walking than sitting.

# Constraints help
In 1960 publisher Bennett Cerf bet Dr Seuss 50 dollars he couldn't write a book using only 50 different words. Seuss won with *Green Eggs and Ham*, one of the best-selling children's books ever.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "50", "l": "different words in the book"}, {"v": "$50", "l": "the bet"}, {"v": "236", "l": "words in The Cat in the Hat"}]}
### Why limits can help
A blank page offers endless options, which can freeze you. Constraints reduce the options and force you off the obvious path: if you can't use the usual solution, you must find another.
### Famous examples
• Haiku and sonnets have strict forms
• French writer Georges Perec wrote a whole novel in 1969 without the letter "e"
• Twitter's original 140-character limit created a new writing style
• Budget films with tiny budgets have produced inventive hits
### Not too much, not too little
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "No constraints", "b": "too many options, freeze"}, {"a": "Some constraints", "b": "focused, inventive"}, {"a": "Too many constraints", "b": "no room to create"}]}
A 2019 review of studies found creativity tends to peak at moderate levels of constraint. Too many rules, such as micromanagement, or no time at all, crush ideas.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Limit the materials", "Limit the time", "Limit the words", "Ban the obvious solution"]}
> A well-chosen limit narrows the options and forces you to find a new path.
!? Seuss's earlier *The Cat in the Hat* (1957) was written using a list of 348 simple words for early readers; he used 236 of them.

# Combining ideas
In 1964 Hungarian-British writer Arthur Koestler called creativity **bisociation**: linking two frames of thought that don't usually meet. Many inventions are combinations rather than creations from nothing.
### Gutenberg's combination
Around 1440–1450 in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg combined existing technologies: a screw press like those used for wine and olives, metal type cast in reusable moulds, and oil-based ink. None was new on its own; together they transformed Europe.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Printing press", "items": ["Wine screw press", "Movable metal type", "Oil-based ink", "Paper from mills"]}
### More combinations
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Velcro (1940s)", "b": "burrs on a dog + fabric"}, {"a": "Caravel (15th c.)", "b": "light hull + lateen sails"}, {"a": "Smartphone", "b": "phone + computer + camera"}]}
Portuguese caravels combined a light, manoeuvrable hull with triangular lateen sails used in the Mediterranean, helping sailors explore the African coast.
### Feeding the combinations
• Read and learn outside your field
• Talk to people with different backgrounds
• Travel or live abroad: studies link deep multicultural experience to more creative problem solving, though the effects are correlational
• Keep a notebook of interesting ideas to combine later
> New ideas are often old ideas that meet for the first time in one person's head.
!? The more varied the "ingredients" you carry, from hobbies to jobs to countries, the more combinations become possible.

# Fear of judgement
Advertising executive Alex Osborn popularised group brainstorming in 1953, claiming groups produced about twice as many ideas. Decades of research disagree. In 1987 German psychologists Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe compared real groups with the same number of people working alone and pooling their ideas.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "ideas produced (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Group of 4 talking", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "4 alone, pooled", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why groups underperform
• **Production blocking**: only one person can talk at a time, and you forget ideas while waiting. This is the biggest factor
• **Evaluation apprehension**: fear of looking silly in front of others
• **Social loafing**: letting others do the work
• Conformity: the group drifts towards the first ideas mentioned
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Write ideas silently", "Share all of them", "Build on each other", "Discuss and choose"]}
### Brainwriting
In **brainwriting**, everyone writes ideas on paper or sticky notes first, then shares. It avoids blocking and judgement. Online tools where ideas are submitted anonymously work similarly.
### Groups still have a role
Groups are useful for evaluating, combining and developing ideas, and people enjoy group sessions and feel more productive in them, even though they generate fewer ideas.
> Generate ideas alone, then share and build on them together.
!! Group brainstorming produces the most ideas => Individuals working separately, then pooling, usually produce more and better ideas

# Mood matters
In the 1980s American psychologist Alice Isen found that people who had just watched a short comedy film were more likely to solve a classic creativity puzzle, fixing a candle to a wall using only a box of drawing pins and matches.
### Broaden and build
Psychologist Barbara Fredrickson proposed that positive emotions **broaden** attention and thinking, making people more open to unusual connections, while negative emotions narrow focus on a threat.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Positive mood", "items": ["broad attention", "more ideas", "playful"]}, "r": {"t": "Mildly negative", "items": ["narrow focus", "spots flaws", "careful"]}}
### What a meta-analysis says
A 2008 review of 102 studies found positive moods slightly increased creativity, especially energetic ones like happiness, rather than very relaxed states. Effects were modest, and some negative moods, like anger, also boosted idea generation in some studies.
### Use moods strategically
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Good mood: generate", "Neutral: develop", "Critical mood: evaluate", "Choose and refine"]}
• Start brainstorming after a short break, music or a laugh
• Do the critical editing when you're feeling more serious
• Don't critique ideas in the same session where you generate them
> Cheerfulness helps you find ideas; a slightly critical mood helps you judge them.
!? The candle problem's solution: empty the box, pin it to the wall as a shelf and stand the candle in it.

# The 30 circles test
This exercise was popularised by Stanford's design school and the design firm IDEO, founded by David Kelley. It's similar to the figural tasks in the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking, created by E. Paul Torrance in 1966.
### How to do it
ex: Draw 30 circles on a sheet | set a timer for 3 minutes | turn each into something: clock, ball, planet, face | count how many you finished
### What it measures
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fluency", "b": "how many circles you filled"}, {"a": "Flexibility", "b": "how many categories used"}, {"a": "Originality", "b": "how unusual the ideas are"}]}
Flexibility tells you if you got stuck in one category, such as twenty different sports balls. Originality is how rare an idea is compared with others.
### What people usually notice
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Slow, careful start", "Judging drops away", "Speed picks up", "Rule-bending appears"]}
Some people start breaking hidden "rules": combining several circles into one drawing, a traffic light or a bicycle. No one said that wasn't allowed. This is a lesson in noticing assumptions you never stated.
### Try it with others
Compare drawings in a group. You will see both overlaps and surprising ideas. It's a good warm-up before meetings because it gets people generating without fear.
> Creativity speeds up once you stop judging each idea and start playing.
!? Most people finish fewer than 20 circles in three minutes, mainly because they pause to judge their ideas.

== ps-experiments

# The Stanford prison experiment
In August 1971 Philip Zimbardo turned a Stanford University basement into a mock prison. He randomly assigned about 24 male students, paid 15 dollars a day, to be guards or prisoners. The study was planned for two weeks but stopped after six days as guards humiliated prisoners.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1971", "l": "Experiment held"}, {"y": "2002", "l": "BBC prison study"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "Recordings re-examined"}, {"y": "2024", "l": "Zimbardo dies"}]}
### The famous conclusion
Zimbardo argued that the situation, not personality, turned ordinary young men into abusers. The study became a staple of textbooks and was cited to explain abuses such as those at Abu Ghraib prison in 2004.
### Why it is now heavily questioned
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Guards were coached to be tough", "Zimbardo ran the prison himself", "Some role-playing, not real distress", "Never properly peer-reviewed"]}
In 2018 French researcher Thibault Le Texier examined the archives. Recordings showed the "warden" encouraging guards to be harsh. One prisoner who had a famous breakdown later said he was partly acting. Most guards did not behave cruelly; a few did.
### Trying to repeat it
In 2002 psychologists Alex Haslam and Stephen Reicher ran a similar study with the BBC. Guards didn't spontaneously become tyrannical; instead they struggled to take charge, and group identity and leadership shaped what happened.
> The Stanford prison experiment is better seen as a demonstration of what leaders can encourage than as proof that situations make anyone cruel.
!! The study proves anyone becomes cruel in the right role => Most guards weren't cruel, and the guards were pushed by the researchers

# The marshmallow test, revisited
In the late 1960s Walter Mischel offered preschool children at Stanford's nursery school a choice: one treat now, or two if they waited alone for up to about 15–20 minutes. Follow-ups in 1988 and 1990 suggested those who waited longer had higher test scores and coped better as teenagers.
### The 2018 replication
Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan studied about 900 children from a much more diverse US sample. The link between waiting and age-15 achievement was about half as strong as before. After accounting for family background, home environment and early ability, it shrank by about two-thirds more and was no longer reliable.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "link with later achievement (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Original studies", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "2018, raw", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "2018, controlled", "v": 0.7, "hi": true}]}
### Waiting is rational sometimes
In a 2013 study, children who had just seen an adult break a promise waited far less than those whose adult kept promises: about 3 minutes against about 12. Waiting depends partly on trust.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Reliable adult", "items": ["promises kept", "waited ~12 min"]}, "r": {"t": "Unreliable adult", "items": ["promises broken", "waited ~3 min"]}}
### What's left
Self-control does matter, but the test captures it only partly. A child's waiting reflects their environment: in a home where food or promises are uncertain, taking the treat now is sensible.
> Waiting for a second marshmallow says as much about a child's world as about their willpower.
!? The original Stanford sample was very small and drawn mostly from children of university staff, not a typical population.

# Little Albert
In 1920 American psychologist John B. Watson and his student Rosalie Rayner at Johns Hopkins University tested whether fear could be learned. "Albert B.", aged about 9 months, first showed no fear of a white rat.
### The procedure
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rat: no fear", "Rat + loud bang", "Repeat pairing", "Rat alone: crying"]}
When Albert reached for the rat, a researcher struck a steel bar with a hammer behind him. After several pairings, Albert cried and crawled away at the sight of the rat. His fear generalised to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat and a Santa Claus mask.
This is **classical conditioning**, first described by Ivan Pavlov with dogs: a neutral thing becomes linked to an unpleasant one.
### Serious problems
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Only one baby", "Fear never removed", "No consent by today's rules", "Fear judged by eye, not measured"]}
Albert left the hospital before the fear could be removed. Researchers have since argued over who he was: one candidate died aged six; another lived until 2007. The identity remains disputed.
### What came next
In 1924 Mary Cover Jones showed the reverse: a boy called Peter lost his fear of rabbits through gradual, pleasant exposure. Her work is an ancestor of today's exposure therapy.
> Little Albert showed fears can be learned, and the study's failings helped shape modern research ethics.
!? Watson later left academia after a scandal and became a successful advertising executive.

# Harlow's monkeys
In the 1950s many experts, including behaviourists like Watson, believed babies love their mothers mainly because they provide food. Some parenting advice even warned against too much cuddling.
### The experiment
At the University of Wisconsin, Harry Harlow raised infant rhesus monkeys with two artificial "mothers": one of bare wire with a feeding bottle, the other covered in soft terry cloth with no milk.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "hours per day on each mother (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Cloth mother", "v": 17.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Wire mother", "v": 1.0}]}
The monkeys went to the wire mother to feed, then returned to cling to the cloth one. When frightened, they ran to the cloth mother.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Wire mother", "items": ["gave milk", "visited briefly"]}, "r": {"t": "Cloth mother", "items": ["gave comfort", "safe base when scared"]}}
### Why it mattered
Harlow called it "contact comfort". His work supported John Bowlby's attachment theory and helped change hospital and orphanage practices, for example allowing parents to visit sick children and encouraging physical contact.
### The dark side
Harlow's later experiments isolated monkeys for months, causing severe, lasting harm. Many people, including some colleagues, found them cruel. The work fed into the animal-welfare movement and to stricter rules on animal research.
> Babies need comfort and touch, not just food, a lesson learned at a terrible cost to the animals.
!? Harlow titled his famous 1958 lecture "The Nature of Love".

# The Robbers Cave
In summer 1954 Turkish-American psychologist Muzafer Sherif ran an experiment at a camp in Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma. Twenty-two 11-year-old boys, similar in background, were split into two groups who didn't know the other existed.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Groups bond", "Groups compete", "Hostility grows", "Shared goals reduce it"]}
### The phases
The groups named themselves the Rattlers and the Eagles. A tournament with prizes led to name-calling, flag burning and raids on cabins. Simply bringing them together for meals didn't help: food fights broke out.
### Superordinate goals
Then the researchers created problems that needed both groups: a "broken" water supply, a stuck food truck pulled with a rope, and money pooled to rent a film. Hostility fell and friendships formed across groups.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Competition", "items": ["one side wins", "hostility, insults"]}, "r": {"t": "Shared goals", "items": ["both need each other", "friendships form"]}}
### Critiques
Australian writer Gina Perry examined the archives in 2018. An earlier 1953 attempt had failed: the boys united against the staff instead. Researchers also nudged the conflict along more than the published account admits. The core idea, that competition for scarce resources fuels conflict, is supported by other research, but the camp study is less clean than often told.
> Groups turn hostile when they compete, and come together when they need each other.
!? This work helped inspire "contact" approaches, such as cooperative learning in mixed classrooms.

# The Pygmalion effect
In 1965 Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson gave pupils at a San Francisco school a test, telling teachers it identified "bloomers" who would soon make big gains. In fact about 20% of pupils were chosen at random. Eight months later, the "bloomers" in the youngest classes had gained more IQ points than others.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Teacher expects more", "More warmth and attention", "Pupil tries harder", "Better results"]}
### How expectations might work
Later research suggested teachers who expect more give more warmth, more feedback, more chances to answer and harder material. Low expectations can do the reverse, sometimes called the **golem effect**.
### How strong is it really?
The original study had problems: some IQ scores were implausible, and the gains appeared mostly in the first two school years. Reviews by Lee Jussim and others found that teacher expectation effects are real but usually small, and mostly reflect teachers accurately predicting pupils.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Belief transforms any pupil", "r": "Small but real effects"}]}
### Who is most affected
Effects appear larger for pupils from stigmatised or disadvantaged groups, and when teachers know little about pupils beforehand.
> What others expect of us can nudge our performance, but less dramatically than the legend suggests.
!? The name comes from the Greek myth of Pygmalion, a sculptor whose statue came to life, and from George Bernard Shaw's play.

# The good Samaritan study
In 1973 John Darley and Daniel Batson at Princeton recruited trainee priests at a theological seminary. Each was asked to walk to another building to give a short talk: some on jobs, some on the parable of the Good Samaritan. Some were told they were late, some that they had a few minutes, some that they had time to spare.
On the way, they passed a man slumped in a doorway, coughing and groaning.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% who offered help", "items": [{"l": "Plenty of time", "v": 63.0}, {"l": "Some hurry", "v": 45.0}, {"l": "Very late", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}]}
### What mattered
Being in a hurry strongly affected helping. The topic of the talk did not make a noticeable difference: thinking about the Good Samaritan did not make people more helpful. Some hurrying students literally stepped over the man.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Made a difference", "items": ["time pressure"]}, "r": {"t": "Little difference", "items": ["topic of talk", "religious type"]}}
### Cautions
The study had only about 40 participants, so the percentages are rough. It has rarely been directly replicated. Still, the broad idea that rushing reduces helping fits other research on attention: hurried people may simply not notice someone needs help.
• In busy cities, people walk faster and help strangers less often, on average
> When we rush, we stop noticing others; slowing down is part of being kind.
!? Several hurried participants seemed anxious afterwards, suggesting conflict between helping and keeping the researchers waiting.

# The replication crisis
In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration, led by Brian Nosek, published attempts by 270 researchers to repeat 100 studies from three major psychology journals of 2008. 97% of the originals had reported significant results; only about 36% of the repeats did.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% with significant results", "items": [{"l": "Original studies", "v": 97.0}, {"l": "Replications", "v": 36.0, "hi": true}]}
Effects in the repeats were, on average, about half as large as in the originals.
### Famous casualties
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Ego depletion", "b": "23-lab test in 2016 found ~0"}, {"a": "Power posing", "b": "hormone effects not repeated"}, {"a": "Elderly priming", "b": "slow walking not repeated"}]}
• **Ego depletion**, the idea that willpower runs out like fuel, largely failed a large multi-lab test
• Some findings, like the anchoring effect and the Stroop effect, replicated strongly
### Why it happened
• Small samples that produce flukes
• Journals favouring surprising, positive results
• "Flexible" analyses: trying many tests until one works
• A few cases of fraud, such as Diederik Stapel in the Netherlands in 2011
### Not unique to psychology
Medicine, economics and cancer biology have found similar problems. Some researchers argued the 2015 project underestimated success because some repeats differed from the originals. Debate continues, but the field now takes replication seriously.
> Failed replications were painful, but they made psychology more honest and more reliable.
!! Science that fails to replicate proves science is useless => Checking and correcting itself is exactly how science is meant to work

# Better science
After the replication crisis, psychology reformed. Many of the changes are now standard in good journals.
### Key reforms
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Preregistration", "b": "plan published before data"}, {"a": "Registered reports", "b": "accepted before results known"}, {"a": "Large samples", "b": "fewer flukes"}, {"a": "Open data", "b": "others can check analyses"}]}
### Registered reports
In this format, journals review and accept a study based on its question and method, before data exist. Publication is guaranteed whatever the result. A 2021 analysis found only about 44% of registered reports found support for their main hypothesis, compared with about 96% of standard papers, showing how much the usual system favoured positive results.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% supporting main hypothesis", "items": [{"l": "Standard papers", "v": 96.0}, {"l": "Registered reports", "v": 44.0, "hi": true}]}
### Big team science
Projects like Many Labs and the Psychological Science Accelerator run the same study in dozens of labs worldwide at once, including labs in Europe, giving huge samples and testing whether effects hold across cultures.
### What this means for you
When you read about a psychology finding, ask:
• How large was the sample?
• Has it been replicated?
• Was it preregistered?
> Good science states its plans in advance, shares its data and gets checked by others.
!? Preregistration became standard for clinical drug trials earlier, partly after evidence that negative trials were going unpublished.

# Ethics today
Many classic psychology studies would not be approved today: Little Albert, Harlow's isolated monkeys, Stanford prison, and Stanley Milgram's 1960s obedience studies, where people believed they were giving painful electric shocks.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1947", "l": "Nuremberg Code"}, {"y": "1964", "l": "Declaration of Helsinki"}, {"y": "1979", "l": "Belmont Report (US)"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "GDPR protects data"}]}
### Where the rules came from
The Nuremberg Code of 1947 responded to Nazi medical experiments. The World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki followed in 1964. In the US, scandals like the Tuskegee syphilis study led to the Belmont Report in 1979.
### Core principles now
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Informed consent", "Right to withdraw", "Minimise harm", "Confidential data", "Full debriefing"]}
### How it works in practice
Researchers submit plans to an ethics committee before starting. Deception is allowed only if necessary and harmless, and participants must be told the truth afterwards. In Europe, the GDPR adds strict rules on personal data. In Portugal, psychologists also follow the ethics code of the Ordem dos Psicólogos Portugueses.
### Trade-offs
Some argue ethics reviews can be slow or overcautious. But the rules protect participants and public trust in research.
> Research must protect the people who take part; no discovery is worth deceiving or harming them without care.
!? Modern partial replications of Milgram's study stop at a lower, safer shock level and screen out vulnerable participants.

== ps-communication

# Most meaning isn't in words
The famous "7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language" figure comes from two small studies by Albert Mehrabian in 1967. Listeners heard single words like "dear" or "terrible" said in a liked, neutral or disliked tone, sometimes with a photo of a face. They then judged how the speaker felt about them.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% weight on feelings (1967 lab task)", "items": [{"l": "Words", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Tone of voice", "v": 38.0}, {"l": "Facial expression", "v": 55.0, "hi": true}]}
### What the study did not show
• It only concerned feelings and attitudes, not facts or ideas
• It used single words, not real conversations
• It mattered most when words and tone **contradicted** each other
• Mehrabian himself has said the figures don't apply to ordinary communication
If 93% of meaning were non-verbal, you could follow a lecture in a foreign language. You can't.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "93% of all meaning is non-verbal", "r": "Non-verbal cues matter most for feelings"}]}
### When non-verbal cues win
When someone says "I'm fine" in a flat voice with crossed arms, most people believe the tone, not the words. That is the real lesson: when channels clash, we trust the harder-to-fake ones.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Non-verbal channels", "items": ["tone and pace", "face", "posture and gestures", "distance and touch"]}
### Text messages
Written messages strip out tone and face, so they are easily misread. A 2005 study by Justin Kruger and colleagues found people were far more confident that their email sarcasm would be recognised than it actually was.
> Words carry the content; tone and body carry the feeling, and when they clash, people believe the feeling.
!! 93% of communication is body language => That figure comes from a narrow lab test about liking, not everyday speech

# "I" statements
The idea comes from American psychologist Thomas Gordon, who taught "I-messages" in his Parent Effectiveness Training courses from the 1960s. It is now standard in couples therapy and workplace training.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "You statement", "items": ["\"You never listen!\"", "sounds like blame", "invites defence"]}, "r": {"t": "I statement", "items": ["\"I feel unheard...\"", "describes your view", "invites a fix"]}}
### Why "you" triggers defence
"You" statements, especially with "always" or "never", sound like a verdict on someone's character. The listener's attention turns to defending themselves and finding the exception: "I listened last Tuesday!" The original problem is lost.
### A simple formula
= I feel [emotion] when [specific behaviour] because [effect on me]
ex: You: "You're always late!" | I: "I feel stressed when we arrive after the start" | Add: "because I miss the briefing. Could we leave ten minutes earlier?"
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["I feel...", "when (behaviour)...", "because (effect)...", "Could we...?"]}
### What the research says
Studies of couples and roommates find "I" statements are usually rated as less hostile than "you" statements, but the effect is modest. Tone, timing and the relationship matter a lot. "I feel that you are selfish" is still an accusation dressed as an I-statement.
• Name a feeling, not a judgement
• Describe one specific behaviour
• Keep it short
> Describe your experience instead of judging their character, and you leave room for a solution.
!? "I feel that you..." usually introduces a judgement, not a feeling; real feelings are words like worried, hurt or frustrated.

# Nonviolent communication
Marshall Rosenberg, an American psychologist who trained with Carl Rogers, developed Nonviolent Communication (NVC) from the 1960s. His book of the same name has been translated into many languages, including Portuguese, and NVC is used in schools, mediation and peace work.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Observation", "Feeling", "Need", "Request"]}
### The four steps in practice
ex: Observation: "The dishes have been in the sink since Monday" | Feeling: "I feel frustrated" | Need: "I need the kitchen to be usable" | Request: "Would you wash them tonight?"
### What makes each step hard
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Observation", "b": "facts a camera would record"}, {"a": "Feeling", "b": "emotion, not \"I feel ignored\""}, {"a": "Need", "b": "universal: rest, respect"}, {"a": "Request", "b": "specific, doable, refusable"}]}
• "You're lazy" is an evaluation, not an observation
• "Ignored" or "betrayed" hide a judgement of the other person
• A request is only a request if "no" is an acceptable answer; otherwise it is a demand
### The evidence
NVC is popular, but research on it is thin. Reviews find mostly small studies without proper control groups, with promising but uncertain results on empathy. It is best seen as a well-structured framework that combines ideas with stronger support, such as active listening and specific requests.
> Separate what happened from what you think about it, then ask clearly for what you need.
!! NVC means always being soft and polite => It aims at honesty; you can say hard things clearly without blame

# Listening to understand
Psychologist Carl Rogers and Richard Farson described **active listening** in 1957. They argued that feeling truly understood is what allows people to calm down and change.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Attend: put the phone away", "Ask open questions", "Paraphrase what you heard", "Reflect the feeling"]}
### Why we listen badly
• We think faster than people speak, so attention drifts
• We prepare our reply while the other person is talking
• We jump to advice or our own similar story
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Listen to reply", "items": ["planning a rebuttal", "interrupting", "quick advice"]}, "r": {"t": "Listen to understand", "items": ["curious questions", "summarising", "checking meaning"]}}
### What paraphrasing does
A summary like "So you're worried the deadline is unrealistic, and nobody asked you?" does three things. It proves you heard. It lets the speaker correct you. And it slows the conversation down. In a 2014 study by Harry Weger and colleagues, people who received paraphrasing responses felt more understood by their conversation partner.
ex: They: "I'm sick of doing everything around here" | You: "Sounds like you feel the work isn't shared fairly" | They: "Yes, especially the cooking"
### Understanding is not agreeing
You can summarise someone's view accurately and still disagree. Doing it first often makes them more willing to hear your side.
> Before you answer, show you understood; it's the fastest way to lower the temperature.
!? Research on "question-asking" by Karen Huang and colleagues (2017) found that people who asked more follow-up questions on first dates were better liked.

# The curse of knowledge
Economists Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein and Martin Weber named the **curse of knowledge** in 1989. Once you know something, your brain can't easily switch it off to see things as a beginner does.
### The tapping study
In 1990 Stanford student Elizabeth Newton asked people to tap the rhythm of a well-known song, such as "Happy Birthday", on a table. Listeners had to guess the song.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% songs recognised", "items": [{"l": "Tappers predicted", "v": 50.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Listeners got", "v": 2.5}]}
Tappers heard the melody in their heads as they tapped, so the rhythm seemed obvious. Listeners heard only knocks: about 3 songs out of 120.
### Where it shows up
• Teachers skipping steps that feel "obvious"
• Instruction manuals full of jargon
• Doctors explaining a diagnosis too fast
• Experts underestimating how long a task takes a beginner
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["You learn a skill", "Steps become automatic", "You forget they were hard", "You skip them explaining"]}
### Breaking the curse
Test your explanation on a real beginner and watch for confusion. Use concrete examples before abstract rules. Define every term the first time. Ask "what would I have needed to know on day one?"
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "It's obvious, everyone knows", "r": "Check with a real beginner"}]}
> The better you know something, the harder it is to explain it, unless you deliberately test with beginners.
!? Experts in a field can be worse than intermediate learners at predicting how difficult a task is for novices.

# Giving feedback
In 1996 Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi reviewed over 600 feedback studies. On average feedback helped, but in about a third of cases it actually made performance **worse**. The difference was often whether feedback pointed at the task or at the person.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Unhelpful", "items": ["\"You're rude\"", "weeks later", "about the person"]}, "r": {"t": "Useful", "items": ["\"You interrupted Ana\"", "same day", "about the behaviour"]}}
### What makes feedback work
• **Specific**: the exact behaviour, when and where
• **Timely**: soon enough that both remember it
• **Behaviour-focused**: what someone did, not who they are
• **Forward-looking**: what to try next time
### The SBI model
The Center for Creative Leadership teaches Situation–Behaviour–Impact.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Situation: when, where", "Behaviour: what you saw", "Impact: the effect", "Next: ask, suggest"]}
ex: Situation: "In Tuesday's meeting" | Behaviour: "you interrupted Ana twice" | Impact: "she stopped sharing her idea" | Next: "Could you let her finish next time?"
### The "feedback sandwich"
Hiding criticism between two compliments is common, but people often notice the trick, start to distrust praise, or miss the main point entirely. Many coaches now prefer giving praise and criticism separately and honestly.
> Feedback is useful when it describes a specific action and what to do next, not a verdict on the person.
!! Any feedback is better than none => About a third of feedback in studies made performance worse, mostly when it targeted the person

# Receiving feedback
Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen of the Harvard Negotiation Project, in their 2014 book "Thanks for the Feedback", describe three triggers that make criticism hard to hear.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Truth trigger", "b": "\"That's just wrong!\""}, {"a": "Relationship trigger", "b": "\"Who are you to say?\""}, {"a": "Identity trigger", "b": "\"I'm a failure\""}]}
### Why we get defensive
Criticism can register as a social threat, activating the same stress response as physical danger: faster heart, narrowed attention. In that state people argue, justify or shut down, and miss whatever was useful.
### A better sequence
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pause and breathe", "Ask to understand", "Thank them", "Decide later"]}
• **Pause**: a few seconds stops a reflex reply
• **Ask**: "Can you give me an example?" turns a vague comment into something usable
• **Thank**: you're thanking them for the effort, not agreeing with every word
• **Decide later**: you choose what to act on once calm
ex: Comment: "Your reports are confusing" | Ask: "Which part lost you?" | Answer: "the summary is at the end" | Fix: put the conclusion first
### Spot the useful part
Even unfair or clumsy feedback often contains a grain of truth. Asking "what is this person seeing that I can't?" is more useful than judging whether the whole comment is fair.
> Listen first, decide later: understanding feedback is not the same as accepting all of it.
!? People who regularly ask for feedback, rather than waiting for it, tend to be rated more highly by their managers in workplace studies.

# De-escalation
In a heated argument, the body's stress response takes over. Couples researcher John Gottman calls this **flooding**: heart rate jumps, often above about 100 beats a minute, and the ability to think flexibly and listen drops sharply.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Raised voice", "Threat felt", "Defensive reply", "Anger rises"]}
### Breaking the spiral
• **Lower your voice and slow down**: people tend to match the pace and volume of the person they're talking to
• **Acknowledge the feeling**: "I can see you're really angry about this"
• **Avoid** "calm down", sarcasm and listing old grievances
• **Find a small point of agreement**
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Escalates", "items": ["\"Calm down!\"", "louder, faster", "proving them wrong"]}, "r": {"t": "Calms", "items": ["\"I see this matters\"", "slower, quieter", "naming the feeling"]}}
### Taking a break
Gottman's research suggests a break of at least about 20 minutes, spent doing something soothing rather than rehearsing arguments. Say when you will come back, so it isn't heard as walking out.
ex: "I'm too wound up to talk well" | "Can we take 30 minutes?" | Walk, then return at the agreed time
### Professional use
Police, nurses and airline staff in many European countries are trained in de-escalation. The same core ideas apply: space, calm voice, acknowledgement and offering choices.
> When emotions run high, calm the body first; the argument can wait.
!! Venting anger gets it out of your system => Studies, such as Brad Bushman's in 2002, found venting tends to increase anger rather than release it

# Win-win negotiation
The approach was set out by Roger Fisher and William Ury of the Harvard Negotiation Project in "Getting to Yes" (1981). It is often called **principled negotiation**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Positions", "items": ["\"I want the orange\"", "what you demand", "fixed, either-or"]}, "r": {"t": "Interests", "items": ["juice vs peel", "why you want it", "many solutions"]}}
### Four principles
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Separate people from problem", "Focus on interests", "Invent options for mutual gain", "Use objective criteria"]}
### Asking "why?"
Positions are what people say they want. Interests are the reasons behind them. Asking "what would that give you?" often reveals that both sides can get what they really care about.
ex: Position: "I want a €3,000 raise" | Interest: recognition and paying for childcare | Option: €1,500 raise + 1 day remote + training budget
### Know your BATNA
Your **Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement** is what you'll do if talks fail. A strong BATNA, such as another job offer, gives you confidence to walk away from a bad deal and to stay calm.
### Limits
Not every negotiation is win-win. Haggling over the price of a used car is largely a fixed pie. But many real disputes involve several issues, and trading on the ones each side cares less about creates value.
> Ask why people want what they demand; the answer often opens solutions neither side saw.
!? The orange story is a classic teaching parable; versions are often credited to management thinker Mary Parker Follett, whose 1920s example was actually about opening a library window.

# Saying no
Many people say yes to avoid awkwardness, then regret it. Research suggests the fear is often exaggerated: in a 2022 set of studies by Julian Givi and Colleen Kirk, people who declined invitations expected far more disappointment than the inviters actually felt.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Appreciate", "Clear no", "Brief reason (optional)", "Alternative (optional)"]}
### What a good no includes
• **Warmth**: thank them or acknowledge the request
• **Clarity**: "I can't" or "I won't", not "maybe, let me see"
• **Brevity**: long excuses invite negotiation
• **An alternative**, if you genuinely want to offer one
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Vague", "items": ["\"I'll try...\"", "long excuses", "hidden resentment"]}, "r": {"t": "Clear and kind", "items": ["\"I can't this time\"", "short reason", "maybe next month"]}}
ex: Request: lead a new project | Reply: "Thanks for thinking of me" | "I can't take it on with the audit due" | "I could review the plan in March"
### Why it matters
Every yes uses time and energy you can't spend elsewhere. Saying yes to everything leads to overload, lower quality and, eventually, resentment. A clear no early is kinder than a half-hearted yes followed by a late cancellation.
### Pause before answering
"Let me check my calendar and get back to you today" buys time to decide calmly, without leaving the other person waiting for days.
> A kind, clear no protects your time and respects the other person more than a reluctant yes.
!! Saying no damages relationships => Most people react far less negatively than we expect to a polite refusal

== ps-work

# Psychological safety
Harvard professor Amy Edmondson coined **team psychological safety** in 1999. She found it by accident while studying hospital teams in the 1990s: the best-led teams seemed to report more medication errors. They weren't making more mistakes; they were more willing to admit them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Low safety", "items": ["errors hidden", "silent in meetings", "problems found late"]}, "r": {"t": "High safety", "items": ["errors reported", "questions welcome", "problems fixed early"]}}
### Project Aristotle
From 2012 Google studied about 180 of its teams to find out why some performed better. Who was on the team mattered less than how the team worked together.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Impact", "Meaning", "Structure and clarity", "Dependability", "Psychological safety"]}
Psychological safety was the base the other four factors rested on.
### It isn't about being nice
Psychological safety doesn't mean low standards or avoiding conflict. It means people can disagree, ask "stupid" questions and flag problems without being punished or humiliated. High standards plus high safety give the best learning.
### How leaders build it
• Admit their own mistakes and uncertainty
• Ask for input explicitly: "What am I missing?"
• Respond to bad news with thanks, not blame
• Treat failures as learning, where appropriate
> Teams learn fastest when people can admit mistakes without fear of being punished for them.
!? Aviation's move to encourage junior pilots to challenge captains, called crew resource management, is widely credited with helping cut accident rates.

# Autonomy, mastery, purpose
Daniel Pink's 2009 book "Drive" popularised research from **self-determination theory**, developed by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan from the 1970s. Their version names three basic needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Autonomy", "b": "control over how you work"}, {"a": "Mastery", "b": "getting better at things"}, {"a": "Purpose", "b": "work that matters"}]}
### When rewards backfire
In a 1973 study by Mark Lepper and colleagues, children who liked drawing were promised a certificate for it. Later they chose to draw less in free time than children who got no reward. This is the **overjustification effect**: an external reward can crowd out the inner reason for doing something.
### The debate
• Meta-analyses disagree on how strong and general the effect is
• It mainly applies to activities people already enjoy
• For routine tasks, pay and bonuses do raise output
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Routine tasks", "items": ["clear steps", "rewards help", "pay for output"]}, "r": {"t": "Creative tasks", "items": ["open-ended", "autonomy helps", "pay fairly, then step back"]}}
### Money still matters
Pink's point is that pay must be fair enough to take money off the table. Underpaid people are demotivated whatever else you offer. Above that line, autonomy, growth and meaning do more.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fair, adequate pay", "Autonomy", "Growth", "Meaningful purpose"]}
> Pay fairly, then give people control, room to grow and a reason that matters.
!! Bigger bonuses always mean better work => For creative or enjoyable tasks, heavy rewards can reduce motivation and quality

# Burnout
American psychologist Herbert Freudenberger coined "burnout" in 1974 to describe exhausted volunteers at a free clinic. Christina Maslach then created the Maslach Burnout Inventory in 1981, still the most widely used measure.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Burnout (WHO, ICD-11)", "items": ["Exhaustion", "Cynicism, distance", "Reduced effectiveness"]}
### What WHO's definition says
WHO included burnout in its ICD-11 classification, adopted in 2019 and in force from 2022. It is an **occupational phenomenon**, not a medical condition, and it applies only to work. It results from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.
### Causes are mainly in the job
Maslach and Michael Leiter identified six mismatches between people and jobs.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Too much work", "Too little control", "Too little reward", "Unfairness, poor community"]}
The other two are conflicting values and a breakdown of community. This is why yoga classes alone rarely fix burnout if the workload stays the same.
### Burnout and depression
Burnout overlaps heavily with depression, and some researchers argue they are hard to separate. Anyone with persistent low mood, poor sleep or loss of pleasure should speak to a doctor rather than assume it is "only" work stress.
### A Portuguese angle
Since 2021 Portuguese law has restricted employers from contacting staff outside working hours, except in exceptional cases, as part of a "right to disconnect".
> Burnout is mainly a problem of the job, not a personal weakness, so fixes must change the work as well as the person.
!! Burnout just means being very tired => It also includes cynicism about work and a sense of no longer being effective

# The Hawthorne effect
Between 1924 and 1932, researchers at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago tested how lighting, breaks and working hours affected output. Productivity seemed to rise with almost every change, even when lights were dimmed.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1924", "l": "Lighting tests begin"}, {"y": "1927", "l": "Relay assembly room"}, {"y": "1958", "l": "Effect gets its name"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Data reanalysed"}]}
### The classic explanation
Workers were thought to respond to the attention of the researchers, not the changes themselves. Sociologist Henry Landsberger named this the **Hawthorne effect** in 1958.
### Why it's debated
• The original studies had tiny samples, often five or six workers
• Other things changed too: pay schemes, supervision, two workers were replaced
• In 2011 economists Steven Levitt and John List found the original lighting data showed little sign of the famous effect
• A 2014 systematic review found observation can change behaviour, but effects vary and are poorly understood
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Popular story", "items": ["any change helps", "attention alone works"]}, "r": {"t": "Current view", "items": ["original data weak", "effects real but varied"]}}
### Why the idea survives
Being watched really can change behaviour. Hand-washing in hospitals rises when auditors are present. This is why researchers use control groups that also get attention, and why measuring something can disturb what you're measuring.
> Observation can change behaviour, though the famous factory studies are much weaker evidence than the legend suggests.
!? Hospital studies have found hand-hygiene compliance rises sharply when staff know an auditor is watching.

# Social loafing
In the 1880s French agricultural engineer Max Ringelmann had men pull on a rope, alone and in groups. The more people pulled, the less force each one added on average.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of solo effort per person", "items": [{"l": "Alone", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Group of 2", "v": 93.0}, {"l": "Group of 3", "v": 85.0}, {"l": "Group of 8", "v": 49.0, "hi": true}]}
### Effort or coordination?
Part of the drop is coordination: people don't pull at exactly the same moment. In 1979 Bibb Latané and colleagues asked people to clap and shout, alone and in groups. They also used blindfolded people who only believed others were shouting with them. Effort still dropped, so motivation was part of the cause.
### Why it happens
• Individual effort is invisible, so there is little credit or blame
• People expect others to do the work
• Nobody wants to be the "sucker" who works hardest
### How to reduce it
A 1993 meta-analysis by Steven Karau and Kipling Williams of 78 studies found loafing was smaller when:
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Individual work is visible", "Task feels meaningful", "Group is small", "Members value the group"]}
ex: Group report, 5 people | Vague "everyone helps" | Better: each owns one named section, shown in the final version
### Culture matters
The effect tends to be weaker in more collectivist cultures and in close teams. In some cases people even work harder for a group they care about, called social compensation.
> When everyone is responsible, no one feels responsible, so make each person's part visible.
!? Loafing also appears in mental tasks like brainstorming, where groups often produce fewer ideas than the same people working alone.

# Groupthink
Yale psychologist Irving Janis coined **groupthink** in 1972. Studying US foreign-policy disasters like the 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion, he argued that close-knit groups can value agreement above good judgement.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cohesive group", "Pressure to agree", "Doubts kept quiet", "Illusion of consensus"]}
### Warning signs
• Feeling the group can't fail
• Dismissing warnings and outsiders
• Self-censorship: members hide doubts
• "Mindguards" who shield the leader from bad news
### Counter-measures
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Leader speaks last", "Devil's advocate role", "Invite outside experts", "Write views before discussing"]}
John F. Kennedy changed his decision process after the Bay of Pigs. During the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis he sometimes left meetings so advisers would argue more freely.
### How solid is the theory?
Janis built it from historical case studies, which can be cherry-picked. Laboratory tests support parts of it, such as directive leaders and isolation leading to worse decisions, but cohesion on its own is not reliably harmful. Many cohesive teams decide well. Most researchers treat groupthink as a useful warning rather than a precise law.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "No one objected, so we agree", "r": "Ask each person for doubts"}]}
### Pre-mortems
Psychologist Gary Klein suggests a **pre-mortem**: imagine the project has failed a year from now and ask everyone to write down why. It makes raising doubts the task, not disloyalty.
> Silence is not agreement; good groups go looking for dissent.
!! Close, friendly teams make the best decisions => Cohesion helps, but only when doubts can be voiced and outsiders heard

# The planning fallacy
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described the **planning fallacy** in 1979. We picture the task going smoothly from start to finish and neglect the many things that usually go wrong.
### Students and their theses
In a 1994 study by Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross, students predicted when they would finish their thesis. Even their "worst case" was too optimistic.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "days to finish thesis", "items": [{"l": "Best guess", "v": 34.0}, {"l": "Worst case", "v": 49.0}, {"l": "Actual", "v": 56.0, "hi": true}]}
### Megaprojects
Bent Flyvbjerg of Oxford studied thousands of large projects and found the great majority run over budget, schedule or both.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2006", "l": "Berlin airport begins"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Planned opening"}, {"y": "2020", "l": "Actually opens"}]}
Berlin Brandenburg Airport opened about nine years late, at roughly three times its original budget.
### The fix: outside view
Instead of planning bottom-up from the task's details (**inside view**), look at how long similar past projects actually took (**outside view**). This is called **reference class forecasting**.
ex: Your estimate: 4 weeks | Past 5 similar projects: 6, 7, 5, 8, 6 weeks | Better estimate: about 6–7 weeks
### Other tips
• Break the task into small parts and estimate each
• Ask someone outside the project to estimate
• Add a buffer based on your own track record
> Ask not "how long should this take?" but "how long did similar things take last time?"
!? People are often better at predicting how long other people's tasks will take than their own.

# Meetings
Meeting researcher Steven Rogelberg found that many employees rate a large share of their meetings as poor uses of time. A 2017 Harvard Business Review survey by Leslie Perlow and colleagues found senior managers averaged about 23 hours a week in meetings.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~23 h", "l": "weekly meetings, managers"}, {"v": "~7", "l": "people for a decision"}, {"v": "34%", "l": "shorter when standing"}]}
### Why meetings go wrong
• No clear purpose, so discussion wanders
• Too many people, so most watch rather than contribute
• **Parkinson's law**: work expands to fill the time available, so an hour gets used even if 25 minutes would do
• No decision or owner at the end, so the same topic returns next week
### What works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Purpose and agenda", "Only needed people", "Timed discussion", "Decisions and owners"]}
A 1999 study by Allen Bluedorn and colleagues found stand-up meetings were about a third shorter than seated ones, with similar decision quality.
### Is it a meeting at all?
Status updates can often go in a shared document. Meetings are best for decisions, debates and building relationships, which need real-time conversation.
ex: 8 people × 1 hour weekly | = 8 person-hours per week | ≈ 350 person-hours a year
> A good meeting has a clear purpose, only the people it needs, and ends with decisions and named owners.
!! More people in the meeting means a better decision => Beyond about seven or eight, each extra person tends to reduce decision quality

# Recognition
Recognition meets a basic human need to feel valued and competent. It also tells people which behaviours matter, so it shapes what they do next.
### A striking experiment
In a 2010 study by Adam Grant and Francesca Gino, university fundraisers who received a short thank-you visit from a director made about 50% more calls the following week. Fundraisers who weren't thanked made about the same number as before.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "change in calls next week (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Not thanked", "v": 0.0, "t": "0%"}, {"l": "Thanked", "v": 50.0, "t": "+50%", "hi": true}]}
### What makes recognition work
• **Specific**: "Your summary made the board decision easy" beats "good job"
• **Sincere**: generic praise or forced schemes ring hollow
• **Timely**: soon after the work
• **Fitting**: some like public praise, others prefer private thanks
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Weak", "items": ["\"Great job, team\"", "months later", "generic award"]}, "r": {"t": "Strong", "items": ["names what they did", "right away", "says why it mattered"]}}
### Recognition versus money
Small cash bonuses can feel transactional, and can even reduce motivation for work people already enjoy. Thoughtful recognition costs nothing. But recognition doesn't replace fair pay; praise for an underpaid person can feel insulting.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Notice the work", "Name it specifically", "Explain its impact", "Say thanks soon"]}
> Specific, sincere thanks is one of the cheapest and strongest motivators a manager has.
!? Gallup's surveys often find that recognition is one of the things employees say they want most, and that many feel they rarely receive it.

# Deep work
Cal Newport, a computer scientist at Georgetown University, published "Deep Work" in 2016. He contrasts **deep work**, focused effort on demanding tasks, with **shallow work** such as email and admin, which is easy to copy and adds little value.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Deep work", "items": ["demanding thinking", "creates new value", "hard to replace"]}, "r": {"t": "Shallow work", "items": ["email, admin", "easy to copy", "fills the day"]}}
### The cost of switching
• Gloria Mark's research found that after an interruption, people took on average about 23 minutes to get back to the original task
• Her later studies found attention on a screen shifts on average roughly every 47 seconds
• Sophie Leroy described **attention residue**: part of your mind stays on the previous task after you switch
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~23 min", "l": "to return after interruption"}, {"v": "~47 s", "l": "average screen focus"}]}
### How to protect it
• Block 1–3 hours in your calendar and treat it like a meeting
• Turn off notifications and close email
• Batch shallow tasks into set times
• Start with shorter blocks and build up; most people manage about four hours of truly deep work a day
ex: 9:00–11:00 deep work, phone in drawer | 11:00 email and messages | 14:00–15:30 second deep block
### Flow
Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described **flow**, a state of complete absorption, which happens when a task's challenge matches your skill and you can work without interruption. Deep work creates the conditions for it.
> Focused, uninterrupted time is scarce, so guard it like your most valuable resource.
!! Multitasking makes you more productive => Switching between tasks costs time and accuracy; people who multitask most are often worse at filtering distractions

== ps-digital

# The attention economy
In 1971 the economist Herbert Simon pointed out that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. Half a century later, that scarce attention is what many tech companies sell. If you don't pay for an app, the advertisers do, and they pay for your eyes and your time.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Free app attracts users", "Users spend time", "Data and ad space sold", "Revenue funds more hooks"]}
### Following the money
Advertising brings in almost all of Meta's revenue (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp) and around three-quarters of Google's parent company's. The business model rewards anything that increases time on the app, so designers test features constantly to see which ones keep people there longer.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Product", "b": "your attention and data"}, {"a": "Customer", "b": "the advertiser"}, {"a": "Key metric", "b": "daily active users, time spent"}]}
### Why it matters to you
• The app's goal (more minutes) and your goal (a good evening) can quietly diverge
• Design choices, not just personal weakness, shape how long you stay
• Surveys commonly put adult smartphone use at about 3–4 hours a day
Not every app works this way. Paid tools, subscription services and many public-service apps make money in other ways, and their design incentives differ.
### Regulators are paying attention
In the EU, the Digital Services Act (fully in force since 2024) lets the European Commission investigate the largest platforms, including over design that may encourage compulsive use by children.
> When a product is free, ask how it makes money: the answer usually explains its design.
!! "If you're not paying, you're the product" is the whole story => You're more the raw material: your attention is packaged and sold to advertisers

# Infinite scroll
Infinite scroll was invented by designer Aza Raskin around 2006. Instead of clicking to page two, new content loads automatically as you near the bottom. Raskin has since said he regrets how it is used, estimating it wastes vast amounts of human time.
### Stopping cues
Most activities have natural endings: the end of a chapter, an episode, a newspaper. These **stopping cues** prompt a quick decision: carry on, or stop? Infinite scroll removes them, so the default becomes "keep going".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Paged content", "items": ["end of page", "a pause to decide", "easy to stop"]}, "r": {"t": "Infinite feed", "items": ["no bottom", "no pause", "stopping takes effort"]}}
### The same trick elsewhere
• Autoplay of the next video or episode
• "Stories" that slide straight into the next person's
• Feeds that refresh with new posts the moment you return
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Infinite scroll", "Autoplay next episode", "Auto-advancing stories", "Refresh on return"]}
### Why it works
Starting and stopping cost a small amount of willpower. Removing the decision point means you never have to "choose" to continue, so you drift. People often underestimate how long they spent scrolling when asked afterwards.
ex: Plan: "5 minutes" on a feed | No natural end, each post a few seconds | Common result: 20–30 minutes pass
> Without a built-in ending, you have to create your own: a timer, a set number of posts, or a reason to stop.
!? Some apps now add "you're all caught up" messages, a deliberate stopping cue reintroduced after criticism of endless feeds.

# Variable rewards online
In the 1950s B. F. Skinner found that pigeons pecked hardest when food came after an unpredictable number of pecks. He called this a **variable-ratio schedule**. Behaviour trained this way is also the hardest to extinguish: the animal keeps trying long after rewards stop.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Fixed reward", "b": "same every time, easy to stop"}, {"a": "Variable reward", "b": "unpredictable, hard to stop"}, {"a": "Slot machine", "b": "the classic variable schedule"}]}
### Your phone as a slot machine
Each time you open an app you might get a message, a like, or nothing. That uncertainty is the hook. Pull-to-refresh, invented by developer Loren Brichter, even mimics the lever of a fruit machine, and Brichter has said he regrets its addictive side.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Cue: boredom or a buzz", "Check the phone", "Sometimes a reward", "Urge to check again"]}
### The dopamine story, carefully
Dopamine neurons respond strongly to rewards that are better than expected, and to cues that predict them. That helps explain why unpredictable rewards are compelling. But popular talk of a "dopamine hit" from every like oversimplifies: dopamine is about learning and wanting, not pleasure as such, and brain studies of social media are still limited.
### Breaking the loop
• Batch checking into set times instead of responding to every buzz
• Remove badges (the red counters), which act as constant cues
• Notice the trigger: is it boredom, anxiety or habit?
> Rewards that arrive unpredictably keep us checking far more than rewards that arrive reliably.
!! Phone checking is pure weakness of will => It's partly a learned response to a reward schedule that is deliberately unpredictable

# Social comparison
In 1954 psychologist Leon Festinger proposed that we judge our abilities and lives by comparing ourselves with others. Comparison isn't new, but feeds change who we compare with and what we see of them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Upward", "items": ["with people 'better off'", "can inspire", "often lowers mood"]}, "r": {"t": "Downward", "items": ["with people worse off", "boosts mood briefly", "can breed smugness"]}}
### The highlight reel
People post holidays, promotions and good-hair days, not dull Tuesdays. You see your whole life, including the boring and sad parts, and only their edited peaks. Filters and editing apps widen the gap further, especially for appearance.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Their life is better than mine", "r": "I'm comparing my full life with their best bits"}]}
### What the evidence says
This is a hotly debated area. Large studies by Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski (2019) found the average link between teens' screen use and wellbeing was very small. Others, such as Jonathan Haidt, argue social media is a major cause of rising teen anxiety, especially for girls. A 2022 study of Facebook's staggered launch at US universities found it worsened students' mental health, which supports a causal role.
• Effects seem larger for appearance-focused use and for some vulnerable users
• Passive scrolling may be worse than chatting with friends, though this is debated too
• Average effects can hide people who are strongly harmed
> Comparing your ordinary life with others' curated moments is a rigged contest.
!? Even knowing photos are edited doesn't fully protect people: experiments show idealised images can still lower body satisfaction.

# FOMO
"Fear of missing out" was popularised as a research term in 2013, when psychologist Andrew Przybylski and colleagues built a questionnaire to measure it. They found FOMO was higher in people whose basic needs for connection and competence felt unmet, and it predicted more social media use.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Worry about missing out", "Check the feed", "See others' plans", "Feel left out, worry more"]}
### Why notifications matter so much
Each notification is a cue that something might be happening without you. In a 2016 experiment by Kostadin Kushlev and colleagues, people who turned notifications off for a week reported less inattention and hyperactivity than when they turned them on. Other studies found that receiving notifications in a few batches a day improved wellbeing.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Willpower", "items": ["fights each urge", "tiring", "often fails"]}, "r": {"t": "Change settings", "items": ["removes the cue", "decide once", "works automatically"]}}
### Changing the environment
Psychologists call this **situational control**: it's easier to avoid temptation than to resist it. You decide once (turn it off) instead of a hundred times a day.
ex: 80 notifications a day | Each one a small decision: check or not? | Turn off non-essential ones: perhaps 10 left
### A caution
A well-known 2017 study claimed that the mere presence of your phone on the desk reduces available brainpower. Later replication attempts mostly failed to find the effect, so treat that particular claim with caution.
> It's easier to remove the trigger than to resist it every time it fires.
!! You'll miss something important if notifications are off => Messages from real people can stay on; most alerts are from apps seeking attention

# Screens and sleep
Most attention goes to blue light, but the evidence suggests what you're doing on the screen matters more than the light it emits.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "relative effect on sleep (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Engaging content", "v": 3.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Later bedtime", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Screen light", "v": 1.0}]}
### The light effect
Bright, blue-rich light in the evening suppresses **melatonin**, the hormone that signals night to your body. In a 2014 Harvard lab study, people who read on a light-emitting e-reader for about four hours before bed took around 10 minutes longer to fall asleep than when reading a printed book. Real-life phone use is usually shorter and dimmer, so the effect is smaller. A 2021 study found "night mode" settings made no clear difference.
### The engagement effect
• Exciting games, arguments and shows raise arousal, the opposite of winding down
• "One more video" pushes bedtime later: a habit called bedtime procrastination
• Night-time notifications can wake you
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Phone in bed", "Engaging content", "Later bedtime", "Less sleep"]}
### What helps
Charging the phone outside the bedroom removes both the temptation and night-time alerts. A cheap alarm clock replaces the "I need it as an alarm" excuse. Adults generally need 7–9 hours of sleep; teenagers 8–10, and many get much less.
> Late-night screens steal sleep mostly by keeping you up, not by the colour of their light.
!! Night mode makes late scrolling harmless => Warmer colours reduce the light effect slightly, but the content still keeps you awake

# Echo chambers
In 2011 activist Eli Pariser coined the **filter bubble**: the idea that personalised algorithms shield us from views we disagree with. An **echo chamber** is a related idea, a group where similar opinions bounce around and get louder.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["You engage with a post", "Algorithm learns", "Shows more of the same", "You engage again"]}
### What research actually found
The evidence is more reassuring than the popular story, though not entirely.
• A 2015 Facebook study found users' own choices limited exposure to other views more than the algorithm did
• In 2023, large studies with Meta during the 2020 US election found that switching people to a chronological feed for three months did not measurably change their political attitudes
• Most people's news diets are fairly mixed; tight echo chambers mainly involve a small, highly partisan minority
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Popular story", "items": ["algorithms trap everyone", "main cause of division"]}, "r": {"t": "Evidence so far", "items": ["choice matters more", "bubbles in a minority"]}}
### Where concern is justified
Recommendation systems can still pull some users towards more extreme content, and small groups can be very influential. Effects may differ by country, platform and time, and studies relying on company cooperation have limits.
### Burst your own bubble
• Follow a few sources you usually disagree with
• Read the strongest version of the other side's argument
• Notice when content makes you angry; outrage spreads furthest
> Algorithms can narrow what you see, but our own choices usually narrow it more.
!? Studies of Twitter posts found each moral-emotional word, such as "evil" or "shame", raised a political post's chance of being shared, by roughly 10–20% depending on the study.

# Digital amnesia
In 2011 Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner reported in the journal Science that people remembered information less well when they expected it to be saved on a computer. They remembered where it was stored instead. The media called it the **Google effect**.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Expect it deleted", "items": ["remember the fact"]}, "r": {"t": "Expect it saved", "items": ["forget the fact", "remember where it is"]}}
### Transactive memory
The idea builds on Wegner's earlier concept of **transactive memory**: couples and teams split up remembering. One partner remembers birthdays, the other the bank details. Using the internet this way is an extension of a very old human habit, not something entirely new.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Partner", "b": "remembers birthdays"}, {"a": "Colleague", "b": "knows the software"}, {"a": "Phone", "b": "stores numbers, maps, facts"}]}
### Replication status
This finding is shakier than it sounds. In a large 2018 project re-running 21 social-science experiments published in Nature and Science, the Sparrow experiment tested did not replicate. Related ideas, such as taking photos impairing memory of what you photographed, also show mixed results.
### What's still sensible
• Offloading phone numbers is fine; that frees memory for other things
• Deep understanding still needs knowledge in your head to connect new ideas to
• Testing yourself (retrieval practice) strongly boosts memory, while looking things up again doesn't
> Outsourcing memory is an old habit; the risk is only when we stop building knowledge we need to think with.
!! Search engines are making us forget everything => The famous study didn't replicate well; offloading some facts is normal and often useful

# Using it well
Total screen time is a crude measure. An hour video-calling a grandparent, an hour learning guitar and an hour doom-scrolling are all "an hour of screen time", but they're not the same.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Often helpful", "items": ["staying in touch", "learning skills", "finding support"]}, "r": {"t": "Often unhelpful", "items": ["mindless scrolling", "late-night use", "appearance comparison"]}}
### How big are the effects?
In a 2019 analysis of over 350,000 adolescents, Orben and Przybylski found digital technology use explained less than half a percent of the variation in wellbeing. Critics reply that average figures hide stronger harms for some young people and for heavy use. Both points can be true.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Why am I opening it?", "What will I do?", "How do I feel after?", "Adjust the habit"]}
### Differences between people
A 2020 Dutch study tracking teenagers several times a day found most showed little effect of social media on wellbeing, a minority felt better and a minority felt worse. One-size-fits-all rules miss this.
### Policy responses
• Several European countries, including Portugal, restrict smartphones in schools for younger pupils
• Australia banned social media accounts for under-16s from late 2025; others are considering limits
• Evidence on whether these measures work is still coming in
> Ask not "how much screen time?" but "what am I doing, and how do I feel afterwards?"
!? Moderate use of digital media by teenagers is linked to slightly better wellbeing than none at all, in some large studies: the "Goldilocks" pattern.

# A healthier phone
Small changes to settings and layout can cut use more reliably than good intentions, because they add **friction** to habits and remove cues.
ex: Settings → Notifications | Keep calls and messages from people | Turn off apps, news, shopping, games
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Notifications: people only", "Social apps off home screen", "Grayscale in the evening", "Daily app timers", "Charge outside the bedroom"]}
### Why each one works
• **People-only notifications**: removes most interrupting cues
• **Moving apps**: breaks the automatic thumb-to-icon habit; you must choose to search
• **Grayscale**: colour makes icons and feeds more attractive; a 2020 study found grayscale cut daily phone use by about 40 minutes
• **App timers**: a pause and a reminder at the limit, a stopping cue
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~40 min", "l": "less daily use, grayscale"}, {"v": "1 week", "l": "notifications-off trial"}]}
### Where to find the settings
On iPhones, look in Screen Time and in Accessibility for colour filters. On Android, look for Digital Wellbeing and bedtime mode. Many phones can switch to grayscale automatically at a set hour.
### Make it a trial
Try one change for a week and notice how you feel. Keep what helps. The aim is not a phone-free life but a phone that serves your plans rather than the app's.
> Design your phone so the easy option is the one you'd choose on reflection.
!! You need an app-free detox to fix your habits => Small permanent tweaks to settings usually beat dramatic short breaks

== ps-intelligence

# Defining intelligence
In 1921 a journal asked leading experts to define intelligence and got almost as many answers as experts. In 1923 psychologist Edwin Boring quipped that intelligence is simply what intelligence tests test. The debate hasn't fully ended, but there is broad agreement on the core.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Intelligence", "items": ["Learn from experience", "Reason and plan", "Solve new problems", "Adapt to surroundings"]}
### A common working definition
In 1994, 52 researchers signed a statement describing intelligence as a very general mental capability involving reasoning, planning, problem-solving, abstract thinking, understanding complex ideas and learning quickly. They stressed it is not just book learning or test-taking skill.
### Points of dispute
• **One or many?** Is it a single ability or several separate ones?
• **Culture**: some cultures include social responsibility or wisdom in their idea of intelligence
• **Practical vs academic**: Robert Sternberg argues for analytical, creative and practical intelligence
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Narrow view", "items": ["reasoning, memory", "measured by tests"]}, "r": {"t": "Broad view", "items": ["adds creativity", "adds practical sense"]}}
### Animals and machines
The question returns with AI. A system can beat humans at chess or exams while lacking common sense in everyday situations. That has pushed psychologists and computer scientists to ask which abilities really generalise.
> Psychologists mostly agree intelligence is about learning, reasoning and adapting; they disagree on whether it's one thing or many.
!? The word "intelligence" comes from the Latin intellegere, to understand, literally "to choose between".

# IQ tests
In 1905, in Paris, Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon made the first practical intelligence test. The French government wanted to identify children who needed extra help at school. Binet warned that his scale should not be seen as measuring a fixed, inborn quantity.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1905", "l": "Binet-Simon test"}, {"y": "1912", "l": "Stern coins 'IQ'"}, {"y": "1916", "l": "Stanford-Binet, USA"}, {"y": "1955", "l": "Wechsler adult scale"}]}
### How scores work
Early IQ was mental age divided by real age, times 100. Modern tests compare you with a large sample of people your age. Scores are scaled so the average is 100 and the standard deviation (typical spread) is 15.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of people in each IQ band", "items": [{"l": "Below 70", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "70–85", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "85–115", "v": 68.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "115–130", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "Above 130", "v": 2.0}]}
### What modern tests include
The Wechsler adult test groups its subtests into four areas:
• **Verbal comprehension**: vocabulary, general knowledge
• **Perceptual reasoning**: puzzles, patterns, block designs
• **Working memory**: repeating digits, mental arithmetic
• **Processing speed**: fast, simple visual tasks
### How good are they?
IQ scores are among the most reliable measurements in psychology: retest a person and the score is usually close. They predict school grades fairly well (a correlation of about 0.5) and job performance more modestly. Tests must be updated and re-normed, and language and culture can bias results.
> An IQ score tells you how someone did compared with others their age on a set of reasoning and knowledge tasks, not their worth.
!! IQ tests were designed to rank people by fixed innate ability => Binet built them to find children who needed help, and warned against that view

# The g factor
In 1904 English psychologist Charles Spearman noticed that children who did well in one school subject tended to do well in others. He proposed a general factor, **g**, behind this pattern. Researchers call the pattern the **positive manifold**: scores on all kinds of mental tests correlate positively.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["g: general ability", "Broad: fluid, verbal, memory, speed", "Narrow: vocabulary, digit span…"]}
### The modern picture
The most widely used model today, the Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory, is a hierarchy. John Carroll built it after reanalysing more than 460 data sets in 1993. g sits at the top, with broad abilities below and specific skills at the base.
ex: Vocabulary test score | Partly g (general) | Partly verbal ability | Partly specific to that test
### What g predicts
g usually accounts for around 40–50% of the variation across a set of test scores. It is the main part of IQ that predicts school and work outcomes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "g view", "items": ["one core ability", "explains correlations"]}, "r": {"t": "Mutualism view", "items": ["abilities boost each other", "g emerges over time"]}}
### What g is, physically
Nobody has found a single "g" spot in the brain. It may reflect overall efficiency of brain networks, especially connections between frontal and parietal regions. Another view, **mutualism**, says g is not one thing at all but arises because separate abilities help each other develop.
> Mental abilities hang together; g is the name for what they share, though what it is in the brain remains open.
!? The positive manifold is one of the most replicated findings in all of psychology, found across countries, ages and test types.

# Multiple intelligences
In his 1983 book Frames of Mind, Harvard psychologist Howard Gardner argued intelligence isn't one thing but several relatively independent abilities.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Linguistic, logical-mathematical", "Spatial, musical", "Bodily-kinaesthetic", "Interpersonal, intrapersonal", "Naturalist (added 1990s)"]}
### Why it caught on
The theory appealed to teachers. It values children who shine in music, sport or people skills, not just maths and language. Schools around the world, including in Portugal and Brazil, adopted lesson plans built around it.
### The scientific problems
• Gardner never produced validated tests for each intelligence, so it's hard to test the theory
• When measured, several "intelligences" correlate with each other and with g, rather than being independent
• Critics say some are better called talents or skills, such as musical or athletic ability
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gardner", "items": ["8 separate abilities", "each its own type"]}, "r": {"t": "Critics", "items": ["abilities overlap", "some are talents"]}}
### Not learning styles
The theory is often confused with **learning styles**, the idea that students learn best when taught in their preferred mode, such as visual or auditory. Reviews have found little evidence that matching teaching to a style improves learning. Gardner himself has said the two ideas are different.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Teach each child in their style", "r": "Match teaching to the content"}]}
> Recognising many talents is a good value; treating them as separate intelligences lacks strong evidence.
!! Each child has one dominant learning style => Most people learn best from a mix of methods suited to the material

# The Flynn effect
In the 1980s New Zealand political scientist James Flynn showed that raw scores on IQ tests had risen across the 20th century in many countries. Test makers had hidden this by regularly re-norming the tests so the average stayed 100.
ex: Gain of ~3 points per decade | Over 1920–2000 that's about 8 decades | ~24 points on old norms
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "IQ on 1900 norms (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "1900", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "1950", "v": 115.0}, {"l": "2000", "v": 130.0, "hi": true}]}
### What caused it?
The rise was too fast to be genetic. Likely contributors:
• Better nutrition and health, fewer childhood infections
• More and longer schooling
• Smaller families and more stimulating environments
• Daily life full of abstract categories, symbols and hypotheticals
### Biggest on abstract reasoning
Gains were largest on puzzle-like tests such as Raven's matrices, and smallest on vocabulary and arithmetic. Flynn argued our grandparents weren't less clever; they used their minds for concrete, practical problems rather than abstract classification.
### The reverse Flynn effect
Since people born around the 1970s, scores have fallen slightly in Norway, Denmark, Finland and some other countries. A Norwegian study comparing brothers showed the decline happens within families, so it's environmental, not a change in who has children. The causes are still debated.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1900s", "l": "Steady rises begin"}, {"y": "1984", "l": "Flynn's US paper"}, {"y": "1990s", "l": "Nordic gains stall"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "Brother study: declines"}]}
> IQ scores can change a lot across generations, showing that environment shapes measured intelligence.
!? Because of the Flynn effect, an out-of-date test can inflate scores, which has mattered in court cases where an IQ cutoff decided legal outcomes.

# Fluid and crystallised
Psychologist Raymond Cattell proposed this split in the 1940s, later developed with John Horn. **Fluid intelligence** (Gf) is reasoning with new problems. **Crystallised intelligence** (Gc) is accumulated knowledge, vocabulary and skills.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fluid", "items": ["new puzzles", "speed, working memory", "peaks around 20s"]}, "r": {"t": "Crystallised", "items": ["facts, vocabulary", "expertise", "grows into 60s+"]}}
### Different peaks
A large 2015 study by Joshua Hartshorne and Laura Germine, using tens of thousands of online participants, found abilities peak at very different ages.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. age of peak performance", "items": [{"l": "Processing speed", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "Working memory", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Reading emotions", "v": 45.0}, {"l": "Vocabulary", "v": 67.0, "hi": true}]}
### What it means in practice
• Young adults learn new software and games faster
• Older adults often outperform on tasks drawing on experience and knowledge
• Many jobs need both; experience can compensate for slower processing
### Slowing the decline
The decline in fluid ability is gradual and varies widely. Physical activity, education, social engagement and managing blood pressure are linked to better cognitive ageing, though cause and effect are hard to untangle. Long-term studies such as the Seattle Longitudinal Study found many abilities hold up well until the 60s.
> Some abilities decline with age while others keep growing, so there's no single peak of intelligence.
!! Your mind starts declining at 25 => Only some abilities fall early; knowledge and vocabulary often grow for decades

# Emotional intelligence
Psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer introduced emotional intelligence (EI) in 1990. Journalist Daniel Goleman's 1995 bestseller made it famous, with the claim that it could matter more than IQ.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Perceive emotions", "Use them in thinking", "Understand emotions", "Manage emotions"]}
### Two kinds of measure
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ability EI", "items": ["tested like IQ", "right/wrong answers"]}, "r": {"t": "Trait EI", "items": ["self-report survey", "overlaps personality"]}}
Ability tests, such as the MSCEIT, ask people to identify emotions in faces or choose the best way to handle a situation. Trait measures ask how emotionally skilled you think you are, and they overlap a lot with personality traits such as extraversion and low neuroticism.
### What the evidence shows
• EI predicts job performance modestly, especially in emotionally demanding jobs such as nursing or customer service
• Once IQ and personality are accounted for, EI adds only a little extra prediction
• Some EI skills can be improved with training, though effects are typically modest
ex: Colleague snaps at you | Perceive: they look stressed | Understand: deadline pressure | Manage: reply calmly, talk later
### The hype
Claims that EI accounts for most of success, or "matters twice as much as IQ", are not supported by research. EI is real and useful, but it is one ingredient among many.
> Reading and handling emotions is a genuine skill, but its power has been oversold.
!! EQ matters more than IQ for success => Studies show EI adds modestly to IQ and personality, not more than them

# Can you get smarter?
Brain-training apps promise a sharper mind in minutes a day. The research mostly finds **near transfer**, improvement on the trained task and very similar ones, but little **far transfer** to everyday thinking.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Near transfer", "items": ["better at the game", "similar tasks"]}, "r": {"t": "Far transfer", "items": ["better reasoning", "rarely found"]}}
### A cautionary tale
A 2008 study suggested training on a memory task called the n-back raised fluid intelligence. Later replications and meta-analyses largely failed to confirm it. In 2016 the US Federal Trade Commission fined the maker of Lumosity 2 million dollars for unsupported advertising claims.
### What does work
• **Education**: a 2018 meta-analysis found each extra year of schooling raises IQ by about 1 to 5 points
• **Reading and learning**: builds crystallised knowledge and vocabulary
• **Sleep**: consolidates learning and restores attention
• **Exercise**: linked to better brain health and slower decline, especially in older adults
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "strength of evidence (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Schooling", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Good sleep", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Exercise", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Brain games", "v": 1.0}]}
### Mozart and other myths
A 1993 study reported that listening to Mozart briefly improved spatial reasoning. Later studies found the effect small, short-lived and probably due to mood and alertness. Any enjoyable, arousing music worked about as well.
> The surest ways to get smarter are the old ones: learning, reading, sleeping well and staying active.
!! We only use 10% of our brains => Brain scans show activity across virtually all regions over a day

# Intelligence isn't everything
IQ predicts school grades with a correlation of about 0.5. That's strong for psychology, yet it means IQ statistically accounts for only about a quarter of the differences in grades. The rest comes from other factors.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "variance in grades explained (rough)", "items": [{"l": "IQ", "v": 25.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Everything else", "v": 75.0}]}
### What else matters
• **Conscientiousness**: being organised and diligent predicts grades and job performance
• **Curiosity**: interest drives learning beyond what's required
• **Health and sleep**
• **Opportunity**: money, schools, family support, networks
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Life outcomes", "items": ["Cognitive ability", "Personality, effort", "Health", "Opportunity, luck"]}
### Contested favourites
Popular ideas here have had mixed replication. **Grit** (passion plus perseverance) turned out to overlap heavily with conscientiousness and predict modestly. The famous **marshmallow test** link between childhood self-control and later success shrank sharply in a 2018 replication once family background was taken into account.
### The Termites
In 1921 Lewis Terman began following about 1,500 high-IQ Californian children. Many did well, but most were not extraordinary, and achievement among them depended heavily on motivation and family background. Two boys tested but not selected, William Shockley and Luis Alvarez, later won Nobel Prizes in physics.
> High intelligence opens doors, but persistence, health, relationships and luck decide much of what lies behind them.
!? Above a certain level, extra IQ points seem to add less to creative achievement than training, motivation and opportunity.

# Wisdom
Psychologists treat wisdom as distinct from intelligence. The Berlin Wisdom Project, led by Paul Baltes from the 1980s, described it as expert knowledge about the conduct and meaning of life. Newer work by Igor Grossmann focuses on **wise reasoning**.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Intellectual humility", "Seeing other viewpoints", "Accepting uncertainty and change", "Seeking compromise"]}
### Smart but foolish
Psychologist Keith Stanovich uses the term **dysrationalia** for the gap between intelligence and rational thinking. IQ correlates only weakly with avoiding many reasoning biases. Clever people can also be better at building arguments for what they already believe.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Intelligence", "items": ["solve the problem", "speed and power"]}, "r": {"t": "Wisdom", "items": ["pick the right problem", "judgement and care"]}}
### Solomon's paradox
King Solomon was famed for wisdom, yet his own life was full of poor choices. Grossmann and Ethan Kross found people reason more wisely about a friend's problem than their own. Taking a distanced view, such as thinking about yourself in the third person, made reasoning wiser.
ex: Your problem: "I was treated unfairly" | Ask: what would I advise a friend? | Consider their view, what I don't know | Choose a fairer response
### Does it come with age?
Not automatically. Some older adults show more wise reasoning about social conflicts, but the link with age is weak, and experience only helps if you reflect on it.
> Wisdom is knowing how to use what you know, with humility about what you don't.
!! Wisdom comes naturally with age => Age helps only a little; reflection and perspective-taking matter more
