== sp-solar

# Our star
The Sun is an ordinary star: a medium-sized yellow dwarf, one of perhaps 100–400 billion in the Milky Way. What makes it special is simply that it's ours. It is 1.39 million km wide, about 109 times Earth's diameter, and you could fit roughly 1.3 million Earths inside it.
In the core, the temperature is about 15 million °C and the pressure is about 250 billion times the air pressure at sea level. Under those conditions, hydrogen nuclei collide hard enough to fuse into helium. Every second the Sun turns about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium, and around 4 million tonnes of mass disappear as pure energy (E = mc²).
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hydrogen nuclei collide", "Fuse into helium", "Mass lost as energy", "Light and heat"]}
### Why it doesn't explode or collapse
The Sun is a balancing act. Gravity squeezes inwards; the heat from fusion pushes outwards. If fusion speeds up, the core expands, cools slightly and fusion slows again. This self-regulation has kept the Sun stable for about 4.6 billion years.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gravity", "items": ["pulls inwards", "squeezes the core"]}, "r": {"t": "Fusion pressure", "items": ["pushes outwards", "heats and expands"]}}
### What it's made of
• About 73% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass
• The remaining 2% is "metals" to astronomers: oxygen, carbon, iron and more
• It rotates faster at its equator (about 25 days) than at its poles (about 35 days), because it isn't solid
> The light reaching you now left the Sun's surface about 8 minutes ago, but the energy behind it took tens of thousands of years to work its way out from the core.
!? The Sun loses about 4 million tonnes of mass every second, yet it's so big that over its whole life it will lose less than 0.1% of its mass this way.

# Eight planets
The planets formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a flat, spinning disc of gas and dust around the young Sun. Close to the Sun it was too hot for ice, so only rock and metal could clump together: that's why the inner planets are small and rocky. Beyond the "snow line", between Mars and Jupiter, ice was plentiful, so cores grew big enough to grab huge amounts of gas.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Spinning disc of gas and dust", "Dust sticks into pebbles", "Pebbles into planetesimals", "Collisions build planets"]}
### Why they all orbit the same way
All eight planets orbit in the same direction and roughly in the same plane, because they formed from the same spinning disc. It's a fossil of the Solar System's birth.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Same direction of orbit", "Nearly the same flat plane", "Most also spin the same way"]}
### Quick profiles
• **Mercury**: smallest, no real atmosphere, days scorching and nights freezing
• **Venus**: Earth-sized, buried under thick clouds of sulphuric acid
• **Earth**: the only place known to have life and liquid surface oceans
• **Mars**: cold desert with the tallest volcano and a deep canyon system
• **Jupiter**: largest, a gas giant with a storm bigger than Earth
• **Saturn**: famous rings, lighter than water on average
• **Uranus**: tilted on its side, an icy blue-green giant
• **Neptune**: farthest, darkest and windiest

!! The planets line up in a neat row => They're spread around their orbits; true alignments are rare and loose
> The mnemonic "My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming" gives the order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

# Rocky vs giant
The Solar System splits neatly in two. The four inner planets are dense balls of rock around iron cores. The four outer planets are giants: mostly gas and fluids, with no surface you could stand on.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Terrestrial", "items": ["small", "dense", "solid surface", "few moons"]}, "r": {"t": "Giants", "items": ["huge", "low density", "no surface", "rings and many moons"]}}
### Gas giants vs ice giants
Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium, like the Sun. Uranus and Neptune contain much more water, ammonia and methane, compressed into hot, dense fluids. Astronomers call those "ices" even though they aren't frozen.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Atmosphere: hydrogen and helium", "Ocean of hot \"ices\"", "Rocky core"]}
### What's inside Jupiter
Going down into Jupiter, the gas gets denser and hotter until hydrogen is squeezed into a liquid metal that conducts electricity. Swirling metallic hydrogen generates Jupiter's magnetic field, the strongest of any planet.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Cloud tops: ammonia clouds", "Liquid hydrogen", "Metallic hydrogen", "Dense core"]}
### Why the split?
• Near the Sun, only rock and metal could stay solid
• Farther out, ice added extra building material, so cores grew faster
• Big cores (about 10 Earth masses) could pull in surrounding gas before the young Sun blew it away
!? Jupiter's magnetic field is about 20,000 times stronger than Earth's, and its radiation belts would be lethal to an unshielded astronaut.

# Venus is the hottest
Venus shows what a runaway greenhouse effect can do. Its atmosphere is about 96% carbon dioxide and so thick that the surface pressure is 92 times Earth's, like being 900 metres under the sea.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "465 °C", "l": "surface"}, {"v": "92×", "l": "Earth's pressure"}, {"v": "96%", "l": "CO₂ in the air"}]}
### How the trap works
Sunlight that gets through the clouds warms the ground. The ground gives off infrared heat, but CO₂ absorbs it and sends much of it back down. With so much CO₂, almost no heat escapes, and the whole planet sits at around 465 °C, day and night, poles and equator.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sunlight warms the ground", "Ground radiates infrared", "Thick CO₂ traps it", "Surface hot enough to melt lead"]}
### Earth's twin gone wrong
Venus is almost Earth's size and was probably once much more like it, possibly with oceans. Being closer to the Sun, its water evaporated, strengthening the greenhouse effect until the oceans boiled away and the hydrogen leaked into space.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth", "items": ["liquid oceans", "CO₂ locked in rocks", "15 °C average"]}, "r": {"t": "Venus", "items": ["no water", "CO₂ in the air", "465 °C average"]}}
### Other oddities
• Venus spins backwards compared with most planets, so the Sun rises in the west
• A day on Venus (243 Earth days) is longer than its year (225 Earth days)
• Clouds of sulphuric acid race around the planet in about four days
• Soviet Venera landers survived on the surface for only up to about two hours
!! Mercury must be hottest because it's closest to the Sun => Venus is hotter, thanks to its thick CO₂ atmosphere
> Distance from the Sun isn't everything: an atmosphere can matter more.

# Jupiter, the giant
Jupiter has 318 times Earth's mass, more than twice the mass of all the other planets combined. If it had been about 80 times more massive, it could have become a small star.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mass, Earth = 1", "items": [{"l": "Earth", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Neptune", "v": 17.0}, {"l": "Saturn", "v": 95.0}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 318.0, "hi": true}]}
### The Great Red Spot
The Great Red Spot is a giant anticyclone, a high-pressure storm, wider than Earth. It has been observed since at least the 1830s, maybe since 1665, but it's shrinking: it was about three Earths wide in the 1800s.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1665", "l": "possible first sighting"}, {"y": "1830s", "l": "continuous observations"}, {"y": "1979", "l": "Voyager close-ups"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "about 1.3 Earths wide"}]}
### A fast spinner
Despite its size, Jupiter spins once in under 10 hours, the fastest of any planet. The spin flattens it visibly at the poles and stretches its clouds into the striped bands we see.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Light bands", "b": "zones, rising gas"}, {"a": "Dark bands", "b": "belts, sinking gas"}, {"a": "Fast spin", "b": "stretches them into stripes"}]}
### Moons and missions
• About 95 known moons, including the four large Galilean moons Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto
• Galileo discovered those four in 1610, proving not everything orbits Earth
• NASA's Juno has orbited Jupiter since 2016, mapping its interior and poles
• ESA's JUICE, launched 2023, will study the icy moons in the 2030s
!? Jupiter doesn't strictly orbit the Sun's centre: the Sun and Jupiter both orbit a shared balance point just outside the Sun's surface.

# Saturn's rings
Saturn's rings are mostly water ice, from tiny grains to chunks the size of a house. They stretch about 280,000 km across, almost three-quarters of the distance from Earth to the Moon, yet are often only about 10 metres thick. If the rings were a sheet of paper, they'd be far thinner than paper relative to their width.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["A ring: outer", "Cassini Division: gap", "B ring: brightest", "C ring: faint", "D ring: innermost"]}
### How old are they?
Data from the Cassini spacecraft suggested the rings could be young, perhaps only a few hundred million years old, because they look clean and bright. Other scientists argue rings can stay clean for billions of years. It's still debated.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Young rings idea", "items": ["clean ice", "few hundred million years"]}, "r": {"t": "Old rings idea", "items": ["self-cleaning", "billions of years"]}}
### Where rings come from
Rings likely formed when a moon or comet came too close and was torn apart by Saturn's gravity. Inside a zone called the Roche limit, tidal forces stop material clumping into a moon.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Moon strays too close", "Tidal forces pull it apart", "Debris spreads into a disc", "Rings"]}
### Rings everywhere
• All four giant planets have rings; Saturn's are simply the brightest
• Gaps are cleared by small "shepherd moons" orbiting within the rings
• The rings are slowly raining onto Saturn and may vanish in a few hundred million years
!? Every 13–15 years Earth passes through the plane of Saturn's rings, and they almost disappear from view because they're so thin.

# Pluto, demoted
For 76 years Pluto was the ninth planet. Then astronomers began finding similar icy worlds beyond Neptune, in the Kuiper Belt. In 2005 they found Eris, about the same size as Pluto. Either it was a tenth planet, or the definition had to change.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1930", "l": "Clyde Tombaugh finds Pluto"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "first Kuiper Belt object"}, {"y": "2005", "l": "Eris discovered"}, {"y": "2006", "l": "IAU new definition"}]}
### The three rules
In 2006 the International Astronomical Union said a planet must meet three criteria. Pluto passes the first two but fails the third.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Orbits the Sun", "Round, shaped by its own gravity", "Has cleared its orbital neighbourhood"]}
### What New Horizons found
In 2015 NASA's New Horizons flew past Pluto and revealed a surprisingly active world: a heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice, mountains of water ice, a thin hazy atmosphere and signs of a possible underground ocean.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sputnik Planitia", "b": "nitrogen ice plain, the \"heart\""}, {"a": "Mountains", "b": "water ice, up to ~3.5 km"}, {"a": "Atmosphere", "b": "thin nitrogen haze"}, {"a": "Charon", "b": "moon half Pluto's size"}]}
### Still controversial
• Some planetary scientists prefer a geology-based definition: any round world is a planet
• Under that rule, the Solar System would have dozens of planets
• Officially, there are five recognised dwarf planets: Ceres, Pluto, Eris, Haumea and Makemake
!! Pluto was demoted because it's too small => It hasn't cleared its orbit of other objects
> Science reclassifies things when new evidence shows the old categories no longer fit.

# Asteroids and comets
Asteroids and comets are leftovers from the formation of the planets, 4.6 billion years old. Studying them is like reading the Solar System's original building materials.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Asteroids", "items": ["rock and metal", "inner Solar System", "no tail"]}, "r": {"t": "Comets", "items": ["ice and dust", "outer Solar System", "tails near the Sun"]}}
### Asteroid types
• **C-type**: dark, carbon-rich, the most common
• **S-type**: stony, made of silicate minerals
• **M-type**: metallic, possibly cores of shattered protoplanets
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of known asteroids (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "C-type", "v": 75.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "S-type", "v": 17.0}, {"l": "Others", "v": 8.0}]}
### A comet's journey
Most of the time a comet is a dark, frozen lump far from the Sun. As it approaches, sunlight turns ice directly into gas, which drags dust out with it. That creates a glowing coma and two tails: a straight blue ion tail pushed by the solar wind, and a curved, yellowish dust tail.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Frozen far away", "Warms near the Sun", "Gas and dust released", "Coma and tails form"]}
### Samples brought home
Missions have collected pieces of asteroids and returned them to Earth: Japan's Hayabusa2 from Ryugu (2020) and NASA's OSIRIS-REx from Bennu (2023). Bennu's sample contained water-bearing minerals and organic molecules, ingredients for life.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2010", "l": "Hayabusa returns grains"}, {"y": "2020", "l": "Hayabusa2 returns Ryugu samples"}, {"y": "2023", "l": "OSIRIS-REx returns Bennu samples"}]}
!? Comets and water-rich asteroids may have delivered some of Earth's water and organic molecules early in its history.

# Distances
Space distances are so large that kilometres become clumsy. Astronomers use the astronomical unit (AU), the average Earth–Sun distance of about 149.6 million km, for the Solar System, and light-years for the stars.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "1 AU", "b": "149.6 million km"}, {"a": "1 light-minute", "b": "18 million km"}, {"a": "1 light-year", "b": "9.46 trillion km"}, {"a": "1 light-year", "b": "about 63,000 AU"}]}
### Light travel times
• Moon: 1.3 seconds
• Sun: 8 minutes 20 seconds
• Jupiter: 35–52 minutes, depending on positions
• Neptune: about 4 hours
• Voyager 1: over 23 hours
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "light travel time from the Sun, minutes", "items": [{"l": "Earth", "v": 8.3}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 12.7}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 43.0}, {"l": "Saturn", "v": 79.0}, {"l": "Neptune", "v": 250.0, "hi": true}]}
### How we measure it
Astronomers bounce radar off Venus and asteroids and time the echo, giving the AU to within metres. For nearby stars, they use parallax: the tiny shift in a star's position as Earth moves around the Sun.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Observe star in January", "Observe in July", "Measure the tiny shift", "Calculate distance"]}
> Communicating with distant spacecraft is slow: commands to a Mars rover can take up to 24 minutes to arrive, so rovers must drive partly by themselves.
!? Travelling at motorway speed, 120 km/h, it would take about 140 years to drive to the Sun.

== sp-stars

# A star is born
Stars are born inside giant molecular clouds: cold (around −260 °C), dark clouds of hydrogen and dust that can be hundreds of light-years across. They sit quietly until something disturbs them, such as the shock wave of a nearby supernova or a collision between clouds.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cold cloud disturbed", "Clumps collapse under gravity", "Spinning disc forms", "Core reaches ~10 million °C", "Fusion ignites"]}
### From clump to star
As a clump collapses, it spins faster, like a skater pulling in their arms, and flattens into a disc. The centre heats up into a protostar that glows from the energy of collapse, not yet from fusion. For a Sun-like star this stage lasts tens of millions of years.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Main-sequence star: fusion", "Protostar: glowing from collapse", "Collapsing clump", "Molecular cloud"]}
### Stellar nurseries
• The Orion Nebula, 1,300 light-years away, is the nearest big star-forming region, visible with binoculars
• Webb's infrared images show "pillars" of dust with newborn stars hidden inside
• Stars form in clusters; the Sun's birth siblings have since drifted apart
> The leftover disc around a newborn star is where planets form, so star birth and planet birth are one process.
!? The Milky Way makes only a few new stars a year on average, a few Suns' worth of mass, but over billions of years that adds up to hundreds of billions.

# Fusion
In a star's core, hydrogen nuclei (protons) are moving so fast that they overcome their electric repulsion and stick together. Through a chain of steps, four protons end up as one helium nucleus. The helium weighs about 0.7% less than the four protons that made it; that missing mass becomes energy.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["4 protons", "Several fusion steps", "1 helium nucleus", "0.7% of the mass released as energy"]}
### Why it takes a star
On Earth, protons repel each other far too strongly. Only the crushing gravity of a whole star creates the temperature (~15 million °C) and density needed. Even then, quantum "tunnelling" is required: protons sneak through the repulsion barrier by chance.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sun's core", "items": ["gravity confines", "15 million °C", "slow and steady"]}, "r": {"t": "Fusion reactor", "items": ["magnets confine", "100+ million °C", "still experimental"]}}
### Heavier fuels
• Bigger stars also fuse helium into carbon and oxygen, then heavier elements
• Each stage burns faster: a massive star may fuse silicon into iron in about a day
• Iron is the end of the line: fusing it consumes energy instead of releasing it
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy released per kg, relative", "items": [{"l": "Burning coal", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Fission, uranium", "v": 3000000.0, "t": "3000000"}, {"l": "Fusion, hydrogen", "v": 20000000.0, "t": "20000000", "hi": true}]}
!! The Sun is burning like a fire => It's nuclear fusion, not chemical burning; there's no oxygen fire
> Fusion is why the Sun can shine for 10 billion years: a chemical fire the size of the Sun would burn out in a few thousand.

# Star colours
Heat anything enough and it glows: first red, then orange, yellow, white and finally blue-white. A blacksmith judges iron's temperature by colour; astronomers judge stars the same way. The peak colour of a star's light tells us its surface temperature.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 2000.0, "hi": 40000.0, "unit": "°C", "items": [{"l": "Red dwarf", "v": 3000.0}, {"l": "Sun", "v": 5500.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Sirius", "v": 9700.0}, {"l": "Rigel", "v": 11000.0}, {"l": "Blue giant", "v": 30000.0}]}
### Spectral classes
Astronomers sort stars into classes O, B, A, F, G, K and M, from hottest to coolest. The Sun is a G2 star. Students remember the order with "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "O and B", "b": "blue, hottest, rare"}, {"a": "A and F", "b": "white"}, {"a": "G", "b": "yellow-white, like the Sun"}, {"a": "K and M", "b": "orange and red, cool, most common"}]}
### Colour, size and life
• Blue stars are massive and burn fuel fast: they live only millions of years
• Red dwarfs are small and frugal: they can shine for trillions of years
• About three-quarters of all stars are red dwarfs, too faint to see with the naked eye
!! The Sun is yellow => Seen from space it's white; our atmosphere makes it look yellow
> Hot, heavy stars live fast and die young; cool, small stars live almost forever.

# Life of a star like the Sun
A star spends most of its life on the "main sequence", steadily fusing hydrogen in its core. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years into a roughly 10-billion-year main-sequence life, and it's slowly getting brighter, about 10% every billion years.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Now", "l": "4.6 billion years old"}, {"y": "+1 billion", "l": "too hot for Earth's oceans"}, {"y": "+5 billion", "l": "red giant"}, {"y": "+7 billion", "l": "white dwarf"}]}
### The red giant phase
When the core runs out of hydrogen, it contracts and heats up while the outer layers swell enormously. The Sun will grow about 200 times wider, swallowing Mercury and Venus and possibly Earth. It will then fuse helium into carbon in its core.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Core hydrogen runs out", "Core shrinks, heats", "Outer layers swell", "Red giant"]}
### The gentle ending
Eventually the outer layers drift away as a glowing shell called a planetary nebula, lit by the exposed core. That core, a white dwarf about Earth's size but with half the Sun's mass, slowly cools for billions of years.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Planetary nebula: expanding gas shell", "White dwarf: hot, Earth-sized core"]}
• A teaspoon of white-dwarf material would weigh several tonnes on Earth
• "Planetary nebula" is a misleading old name: they have nothing to do with planets
!? Earth will become uninhabitable long before the red giant stage, as the slowly brightening Sun boils away the oceans in about a billion years.

# Death of a giant star
Stars more than about 8 times the Sun's mass end violently. They fuse heavier and heavier elements in layers like an onion, until the core is iron. Iron fusion produces no energy, so the core suddenly loses its support.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Hydrogen shell", "Helium shell", "Carbon and oxygen", "Neon and magnesium", "Silicon", "Iron core"]}
### Collapse and explosion
The iron core, about the mass of the Sun but the size of Earth, collapses in under a second to a ball about 20 km wide. Infalling layers bounce off this ultra-dense core and, driven by a flood of neutrinos, the star blows itself apart in a supernova.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Iron core forms", "Core collapses in < 1 second", "Shock wave and neutrinos", "Supernova"]}
### What's left
• **Neutron star**: a city-sized ball of neutrons; a teaspoon would weigh about a billion tonnes
• **Pulsar**: a spinning neutron star sweeping beams of radio waves, like a lighthouse
• **Black hole**: if the remaining core is heavy enough, nothing stops the collapse
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Neutron star", "items": ["~20 km wide", "1.4–2 Suns", "can be a pulsar"]}, "r": {"t": "Black hole", "items": ["no surface", "heavier remnant", "traps light"]}}
!? In 1054 Chinese astronomers recorded a "guest star" visible in daylight for weeks. Its remains are the Crab Nebula, with a pulsar spinning 30 times a second at its heart.
> Betelgeuse, Orion's red shoulder, will explode as a supernova sometime in the next 100,000 years, bright enough to cast shadows at night.

# We are stardust
The Big Bang made almost only hydrogen and helium, plus a trace of lithium. Every heavier atom, the carbon in your DNA, the oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood, was forged later inside stars and scattered into space when they died.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Where atoms come from", "items": ["Big Bang: hydrogen, helium", "Ordinary stars: carbon, nitrogen", "Massive stars and supernovae: oxygen to iron", "Neutron star collisions: gold, platinum"]}
### The cosmic recycling loop
Dying stars blow enriched gas into space. That gas mixes into new clouds, which form new stars and planets with more heavy elements than before. The Sun is at least a third-generation star.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Stars forge elements", "Die and scatter them", "Gas clouds enriched", "New stars and planets"]}
### Gold from collisions
In 2017, detectors caught gravitational waves from two neutron stars colliding 130 million light-years away. Telescopes then saw the explosion's light, full of freshly made heavy elements such as gold and platinum.
• The hydrogen in your body is about 13.8 billion years old
• About 99% of your body's atoms by count are hydrogen, oxygen, carbon or nitrogen
• Every heavier element in your body passed through at least one star
> Carl Sagan put it simply: "We are made of star-stuff."

# Black holes
A black hole forms when a lot of mass is squeezed into a small space, so that the escape velocity, the speed needed to get away, exceeds the speed of light. Since nothing can travel faster than light, nothing escapes from inside the boundary called the event horizon.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mass squeezed", "Escape speed exceeds light speed", "Event horizon forms", "Nothing escapes"]}
### Not cosmic vacuum cleaners
From a distance, a black hole's gravity is no stronger than any object of the same mass. If the Sun were replaced by a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting exactly as now, just in darkness.
!! Black holes suck everything in => Far away, their gravity is like any object of the same mass
### The 2019 image
The Event Horizon Telescope linked radio dishes across the planet into an Earth-sized telescope. It imaged the glowing ring of hot gas around M87*, a black hole 6.5 billion times the Sun's mass, 55 million light-years away. The dark centre is the black hole's "shadow".
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "55 million ly", "l": "distance to M87*"}, {"v": "6.5 billion", "l": "Suns of mass"}, {"v": "8", "l": "observatories linked in 2017"}]}
### Types of black holes
• **Stellar**: a few to tens of Suns, left by dying massive stars
• **Supermassive**: millions to billions of Suns, at the centres of galaxies
• **Intermediate**: hundreds to thousands of Suns, rarer and harder to find
!? Inside the event horizon, the direction "towards the centre" becomes like the direction "towards the future": unavoidable.

# Light-years
A light-year is a distance, not a time: how far light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion km. Light moves at 299,792 km per second, fast enough to circle Earth seven and a half times in a second.
ex: 299,792 km/s × 31.6 million s | ≈ 9.46 trillion km | = 1 light-year

Because light takes time to arrive, looking far away means looking into the past. We see Proxima Centauri as it was 4.2 years ago and the most distant galaxies as they were over 13 billion years ago.
!! A light-year is a unit of time => It measures distance

# The Milky Way
The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy: a central bar of old stars, a bulge, a thin disc with spiral arms full of gas and young stars, and a faint halo of old stars and globular clusters. Surrounding it all is an invisible halo of dark matter that outweighs the stars many times over.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Dark matter halo", "Stellar halo and globular clusters", "Thin disc with spiral arms", "Central bar and bulge", "Supermassive black hole"]}
### Our address
The Sun lies in the Orion Spur, a minor arm between the Sagittarius and Perseus arms, about 26,000 light-years from the centre. It orbits the centre at about 230 km/s, once every ~230 million years. The last time the Sun was here, the first dinosaurs were appearing.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Galaxy", "b": "Milky Way"}, {"a": "Arm", "b": "Orion Spur"}, {"a": "Distance from centre", "b": "~26,000 ly"}, {"a": "Orbit", "b": "~230 million years"}]}
### The centre
At the very centre lies Sagittarius A*, a black hole of about 4 million Suns. Astronomers proved it by tracking stars orbiting an invisible object at thousands of km/s, work that won the 2020 Nobel Prize.
• Thick dust blocks our visible view of the centre; infrared and radio see through
• Gaia, an ESA spacecraft, has mapped over 1.8 billion stars in 3D
!? Within the next few billion years the Milky Way may merge with the Andromeda galaxy. Stars are so far apart that direct star collisions will be extremely rare.

# Billions of galaxies
In 1995 the Hubble Space Telescope stared at a tiny, apparently empty patch of sky for ten days. The Hubble Deep Field revealed about 3,000 galaxies in a speck the size of a tennis ball seen from 100 metres away. Scaled to the whole sky, the observable universe holds hundreds of billions of galaxies, perhaps up to 2 trillion including faint dwarfs.

### Groups and clusters
Galaxies gather in groups and clusters. The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group of about 80 galaxies, dominated by Andromeda and us. The Local Group is part of the Virgo Supercluster and the larger Laniakea supercluster.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Laniakea supercluster", "Virgo Supercluster", "Local Group", "Milky Way"]}
• Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away and contains around a trillion stars
• It's approaching us at about 110 km/s
• On a dark night it's a faint smudge, the most distant object easily seen by eye
> Every star you see with the naked eye is in our own galaxy; Andromeda is the one galaxy beyond it you can easily spot.

# The Space Race
The Space Race grew out of Cold War rivalry. Rockets built to carry nuclear warheads could also carry satellites and people, and each superpower wanted to prove its system was superior. Sputnik 1, a 58 cm metal ball beeping radio signals, shocked the West in October 1957.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Oct 1957", "l": "Sputnik 1"}, {"y": "Nov 1957", "l": "Laika in orbit"}, {"y": "Apr 1961", "l": "Gagarin"}, {"y": "May 1961", "l": "Kennedy's Moon goal"}, {"y": "Jul 1969", "l": "Apollo 11"}]}
### Soviet firsts
• First satellite (Sputnik 1, 1957) and first animal in orbit (Laika, 1957)
• First human in space (Yuri Gagarin, 1961) and first woman (Valentina Tereshkova, 1963)
• First spacewalk (Alexei Leonov, 1965) and first landing on another planet (Venera 7, Venus, 1970)
### The American response
The USA created NASA in 1958 and, in 1961, President Kennedy set the goal of landing a man on the Moon before the decade was out. At its peak, Apollo employed about 400,000 people and took over 4% of the US federal budget.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of US federal budget for NASA (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1960", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "1966", "v": 4.4, "hi": true}, {"l": "1975", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Today", "v": 0.4}]}
### From rivals to partners
The race ended in cooperation: the Apollo–Soyuz handshake in orbit in 1975, then the International Space Station, built by the USA, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada.
> Competition drove astonishing speed: only 12 years separated Sputnik from footprints on the Moon.

# How rockets work
A rocket is a controlled explosion pointed in one direction. Fuel and an oxidiser burn in a chamber; the hot gas expands and rushes out through a bell-shaped nozzle at several kilometres per second. Pushing gas backwards pushes the rocket forwards: Newton's third law.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Fuel + oxidiser burn", "Hot gas expands", "Nozzle accelerates gas backwards", "Rocket pushed forwards"]}
!! Rockets push against the air => They push against their own exhaust, so they work best in a vacuum
### Fuel choices
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Kerosene + oxygen", "b": "Falcon 9, Saturn V first stage"}, {"a": "Liquid hydrogen + oxygen", "b": "very efficient, space shuttle"}, {"a": "Methane + oxygen", "b": "Starship"}, {"a": "Solid fuel", "b": "simple boosters, can't be switched off"}]}
### The tyranny of the rocket equation
Most of a rocket is fuel: to reach orbit, roughly 85–90% of the launch mass is propellant. Carrying more fuel means carrying fuel to lift that fuel. That's why rockets drop empty stages on the way up.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of launch mass (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Propellant", "v": 88.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Rocket structure", "v": 9.0}, {"l": "Payload", "v": 3.0}]}
### Staging
• The first stage lifts the heavy rocket through the thick lower atmosphere
• It drops away when empty, and the lighter upper stage continues
• Each stage sheds dead weight so the rest can go faster
!? The Saturn V that launched Apollo 11 was 111 m tall and weighed about 2,900 tonnes at launch, nearly all of it propellant.

# Getting to orbit
Imagine Newton's cannon on a very tall mountain. Fire slowly and the ball lands nearby. Fire faster and it lands farther away. At about 7.8 km/s, the ground curves away as fast as the ball falls: it never lands. That's an orbit.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Slow: lands nearby", "Faster: lands farther", "7.8 km/s: falls around Earth", "11.2 km/s: escapes"]}
### Up is easy, sideways is hard
Reaching 100 km, the edge of space, takes a few minutes and modest energy; suborbital tourist flights do it. Staying there requires going sideways at 28,000 km/h, which needs about 30 times more energy. That's why rockets tilt over soon after launch.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Suborbital", "items": ["goes up", "falls back down", "minutes of weightlessness"]}, "r": {"t": "Orbital", "items": ["goes sideways fast", "keeps falling around Earth"]}}
### Helpful tricks
• Launching eastward borrows Earth's spin, up to 465 m/s at the equator
• That's why launch sites are often near the equator, like Kourou in French Guiana
• Launching over the sea keeps falling stages away from people
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "free speed from Earth's spin, m/s", "items": [{"l": "Baikonur, 46°N", "v": 322.0}, {"l": "Cape Canaveral, 28°N", "v": 408.0}, {"l": "Kourou, 5°N", "v": 463.0, "hi": true}]}
> Getting to low orbit is "halfway to anywhere": most of the energy of a deep-space trip is spent just reaching orbit.

# Apollo 11
Apollo 11 launched from Florida on 16 July 1969 on a Saturn V. After three days it entered lunar orbit. Armstrong and Aldrin descended in the lunar module *Eagle* while Collins stayed in the command module *Columbia*.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Launch on Saturn V", "3 days to the Moon", "Eagle lands", "2.5 hours on the surface", "Return and splashdown"]}
### A tense landing
During descent, the guidance computer flashed "1202" and "1201" alarms: it was overloaded. Mission Control judged it safe to continue. Armstrong then flew manually past a boulder field and landed with about 25 seconds of fuel left before an abort decision.

### What they did
• Planted a flag and a plaque: "We came in peace for all mankind"
• Set up a seismometer and a laser reflector still used to measure the Moon's distance
• Collected rock and soil samples showing the Moon is about 4.5 billion years old
### Six landings in all

# The International Space Station
The ISS is the largest structure humans have built in space: about 109 m across and around 420 tonnes. It was assembled from 1998 in more than 40 missions by the USA, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada, and has been continuously occupied since 2 November 2000.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1998", "l": "first modules"}, {"y": "2000", "l": "first crew arrives"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "assembly complete"}, {"y": "~2030", "l": "planned retirement"}]}
### Life aboard
Crews of about seven live and work for around six months. Each day they see 16 sunrises and sunsets as they orbit at about 28,000 km/h, 400 km up. The station needs regular engine burns to stay up, because thin air at that height slowly drags it down.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~400 km", "l": "altitude"}, {"v": "92 min", "l": "one orbit"}, {"v": "~7", "l": "crew"}, {"v": "270+", "l": "people from 20+ countries have visited"}]}
### What it's for
• Medical research on how bodies cope with weightlessness, for future Mars trips
• Experiments in materials, fluids and plants that behave differently without gravity
• Testing life-support and recycling systems
• A place for international cooperation
### What comes next
The ISS is due to be de-orbited around 2030, guided into a remote part of the Pacific. Commercial stations are planned to replace it, and China operates its own station, Tiangong.
!? You can see the ISS with the naked eye: it looks like a bright, steady star crossing the sky in a few minutes. Apps tell you when it passes overhead.

# Space telescopes
Earth's atmosphere blurs starlight (that's why stars twinkle) and blocks most ultraviolet, X-rays and much of the infrared. Telescopes in space escape both problems.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hubble", "items": ["launched 1990", "2.4 m mirror", "visible and UV", "orbits 540 km up"]}, "r": {"t": "James Webb", "items": ["launched 2021", "6.5 m mirror", "infrared", "1.5 million km away"]}}
### Hubble's rescue
Hubble's mirror was ground slightly wrong: off by about 1/50th of a hair's width, enough to blur its images. In 1993 astronauts fitted corrective optics, like glasses. Five servicing missions kept it working for over three decades.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1990", "l": "launch, blurry images"}, {"y": "1993", "l": "corrective optics fitted"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "Hubble Deep Field"}, {"y": "2009", "l": "last servicing mission"}]}
### Webb's tricks
• Its mirror, made of 18 gold-coated segments, folded up to fit in the rocket
• A five-layer sunshield the size of a tennis court keeps it below −220 °C
• Cold matters because warm objects glow in infrared, which would swamp faint signals
### What they've shown us
Hubble pinned down the age of the universe and showed that its expansion is accelerating. Webb sees galaxies from just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang and analyses the air of exoplanets.
!? Webb can detect the heat of a bumblebee at the distance of the Moon.

Rovers are the only way to explore Mars' surface so far. Each generation has been bigger and smarter: from the microwave-sized Sojourner to car-sized Curiosity and Perseverance, powered by nuclear batteries.

# Voyager
Voyager 1 and 2 launched in 1977, using a rare planetary alignment that happens about every 176 years to swing from planet to planet. Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus and Neptune.

# Why we have seasons
Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° relative to its orbit, and it stays pointing the same way in space (towards Polaris) all year. So for half the orbit the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun, and for the other half it leans away.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "June, north leans in", "items": ["Sun high", "long days", "summer"]}, "r": {"t": "December, north leans away", "items": ["Sun low", "short days", "winter"]}}
### Two effects of the tilt
• **Angle**: high Sun concentrates energy on a small area; low Sun spreads the same energy thin
• **Day length**: in Lisbon, the longest day has about 15 hours of daylight; the shortest about 9.5
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "hours of daylight in Lisbon", "items": [{"l": "21 Dec", "v": 9.5}, {"l": "21 Mar", "v": 12.1}, {"l": "21 Jun", "v": 14.9, "hi": true}]}
### The distance myth
Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical, but we're actually closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter. The difference in distance is only about 3%, too small to drive seasons.
!! Summer happens when Earth is closest to the Sun => We're closest in January; the tilt causes seasons
### Opposite hemispheres
Because the hemispheres take turns, Christmas is in summer in Brazil and Australia. At the equator, seasons are weak; near the poles, they're extreme, with midnight Sun in summer and polar night in winter.
> If Earth had no tilt, there would be no seasons at all, just permanent climate bands from equator to poles.

# Day and night
Earth spins once on its axis every 23 hours 56 minutes relative to the stars, the "sidereal day". Because Earth also moves along its orbit, it needs an extra ~4 minutes to bring the Sun back to the same position: the 24-hour solar day.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sidereal day", "b": "23 h 56 min, relative to stars"}, {"a": "Solar day", "b": "24 h, relative to the Sun"}, {"a": "Difference", "b": "~4 min, orbit motion"}]}
### How fast are we moving?
At the equator, Earth's surface spins at about 1,670 km/h. In Portugal, at about 40°N, it's around 1,280 km/h. We don't feel it because the motion is smooth and everything, including the air, moves with us.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "spin speed, km/h", "items": [{"l": "Equator", "v": 1670.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Lisbon, 39°N", "v": 1290.0}, {"l": "Oslo, 60°N", "v": 835.0}, {"l": "North Pole", "v": 0.0}]}
### Time zones
Since noon moves westward with the Sun, the world is divided into 24 time zones of roughly 15° each. Portugal's mainland uses Western European Time (UTC+0, +1 in summer), the Azores are an hour behind.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earth turns 15° per hour", "Noon shifts westward", "Time zones every ~15°", "Clocks match local daylight"]}
### Twilight
After sunset the sky stays light because the atmosphere scatters sunlight from below the horizon. Twilight is shorter near the equator, where the Sun sets steeply, and lasts for hours in summer near the poles.
!? Earth's spin is slowly slowing because of the Moon's tides: days get about 2 milliseconds longer per century. 400 million years ago a year had about 400 days.

# Moon phases
Half the Moon is always lit by the Sun. Phases depend on how much of that lit half faces us. As the Moon orbits Earth every 29.5 days, the angle between Sun, Earth and Moon changes.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["New Moon: lit side faces away", "Waxing crescent", "First quarter: half lit", "Waxing gibbous", "Full Moon: lit side faces us", "Waning gibbous", "Last quarter", "Waning crescent"]}
### Waxing or waning?
In the Northern Hemisphere, a Moon lit on the right is growing (waxing); lit on the left, it's shrinking (waning). A Portuguese saying: "a Lua é mentirosa", the Moon lies. When it's shaped like a C it's actually shrinking (decrescente), and when it's shaped like a D it's growing (crescente), the opposite of what the letters suggest.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Waxing", "items": ["lit on the right", "seen in the evening"]}, "r": {"t": "Waning", "items": ["lit on the left", "seen in the morning"]}}
### When to see it
• A full Moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise
• A first-quarter Moon is high at sunset
• A waning crescent is only visible before dawn
!! Phases are caused by Earth's shadow => They're caused by our changing view of the sunlit half; Earth's shadow only matters in eclipses
### 27 or 29.5 days?
The Moon orbits in 27.3 days relative to the stars, but because Earth moves around the Sun meanwhile, it takes 29.5 days to return to the same phase.
> The word "month" comes from "Moon": calendars across the world were based on its phases.

# The far side
The Moon rotates once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth, so the same face always points towards us. This "tidal locking" happened because Earth's gravity raised bulges on the young Moon and braked its spin until the two periods matched.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Young Moon spins fast", "Earth's gravity raises bulges", "Friction slows spin", "Spin = orbit: locked"]}
### Seeing a bit more
Thanks to slight wobbles called libration, we actually see about 59% of the Moon's surface over time, not just 50%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of the Moon's surface", "items": [{"l": "Seen from Earth over time", "v": 59.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Never seen from Earth", "v": 41.0}]}
### A different face
The far side, first photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 in 1959, looks very different: heavily cratered, with almost none of the dark lava plains ("maria") that make the "face" on the near side. Its crust is thicker, which may have stopped lava reaching the surface.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Near side", "items": ["dark lava plains", "thinner crust"]}, "r": {"t": "Far side", "items": ["mostly craters", "thicker crust"]}}
### Landing there
In 2019 China's Chang'e 4 made the first soft landing on the far side, using a relay satellite to talk to Earth. Chang'e 6 brought the first far-side samples home in 2024.
!! The far side is always dark => It gets day and night like the near side; it's just hidden from us
> Pink Floyd's "Dark Side of the Moon" is poetic but wrong: "far side" is the correct term.

# Solar eclipses
A solar eclipse happens at new Moon, when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth. It doesn't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's; usually the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["New Moon", "Lines up exactly with the Sun", "Moon's shadow touches Earth", "Eclipse along a narrow path"]}
### Types
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Solar eclipse", "items": ["Total: Moon covers the Sun", "Annular: Moon too far, ring of fire", "Partial: only part covered"]}
### Totality
During a total eclipse, the sky darkens in minutes, temperatures drop, birds go quiet and the Sun's pearly corona appears. Totality lasts at most about 7.5 minutes and is visible only along a path ~100–200 km wide.
### Iberia's eclipses
A total eclipse crosses northern Spain on 12 August 2026, with a partial eclipse across Portugal. Another total eclipse crosses southern Spain on 2 August 2027, with the Algarve close to the path.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "12 Aug 2026", "l": "total in northern Spain"}, {"y": "2 Aug 2027", "l": "total in southern Spain"}, {"y": "26 Jan 2028", "l": "annular in Iberia"}]}
!? The Moon is slowly moving away from Earth by about 3.8 cm a year. In roughly 600 million years it will look too small to cause total eclipses.
> Never look at the Sun without certified eclipse glasses, except during the brief moment of totality itself.

# Lunar eclipses
A lunar eclipse happens at full Moon, when Earth passes directly between the Sun and the Moon. Unlike solar eclipses, it can be seen from the whole night side of Earth, and it's perfectly safe to watch.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Full Moon", "Earth between Sun and Moon", "Moon enters Earth's shadow", "Turns coppery red"]}
### Why red?
Earth's atmosphere scatters blue light (that's why the sky is blue) and lets red light through. Sunlight passing through the atmosphere around Earth's edge is bent into the shadow, painting the Moon with the red glow of all the world's sunrises and sunsets at once.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Blue light", "b": "scattered away"}, {"a": "Red light", "b": "bent into the shadow"}, {"a": "Result", "b": "a \"blood Moon\""}]}
### Shadow parts
• **Penumbra**: the faint outer shadow; the Moon dims only slightly
• **Umbra**: the dark inner shadow; this is where the Moon turns red
• Totality can last over an hour and a half
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Penumbra: slight dimming", "Umbra: red totality"]}
### A clue to Earth's shape
Aristotle noticed that Earth's shadow on the Moon is always round, whatever the angle: evidence that Earth is a sphere, more than 2,300 years ago.
!? After big volcanic eruptions, dust in the atmosphere makes lunar eclipses darker, sometimes making the Moon almost vanish.

# Tides
The Moon's gravity pulls more strongly on the side of Earth facing it than on Earth's centre, and more strongly on the centre than on the far side. These differences stretch the oceans into two bulges: one towards the Moon and one away from it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Moon's pull strongest on near side", "Weaker on Earth's centre", "Weakest on far side", "Two bulges of water"]}
### Spring and neap tides
The Sun also raises tides, about half as strong as the Moon's. When Sun and Moon line up (new and full Moon), their effects add up: spring tides, with the biggest range. At quarter Moons they partly cancel: neap tides.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Spring tides", "items": ["Sun and Moon aligned", "biggest range", "new and full Moon"]}, "r": {"t": "Neap tides", "items": ["at right angles", "smallest range", "quarter Moons"]}}
### Why the timing shifts
The Moon moves along its orbit as Earth turns, so it takes about 24 h 50 min to return overhead. That's why high tide is about 50 minutes later each day.
### Local differences
• Coastline shape amplifies tides: the Bay of Fundy in Canada reaches about 16 m
• Portugal's Atlantic coast has a range of up to about 3.5–4 m at spring tides
• The Mediterranean has tiny tides, often under 30 cm

# Where the Moon came from
The giant-impact theory says that about 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized protoplanet, named Theia, struck the young Earth a glancing blow. Molten rock and vapour blasted into orbit and clumped together, perhaps within years, into the Moon.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Theia strikes young Earth", "Debris disc forms", "Debris clumps together", "The Moon"]}
### The evidence
• Apollo rocks have almost the same oxygen isotopes as Earth's mantle
• The Moon has a small iron core, as expected if it formed from outer layers
• Lunar rocks are depleted in easily evaporated elements, as if heated intensely
• Computer simulations reproduce the Earth–Moon system
### Open questions
The Moon's composition is almost too similar to Earth's: if Theia was a different body, why aren't its traces more obvious? Some models suggest a more violent collision that thoroughly mixed both bodies.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Classic impact", "items": ["glancing blow", "Moon mostly from Theia"]}, "r": {"t": "Newer models", "items": ["stronger impact", "mixed Earth and Theia"]}}
### The Moon's importance
The Moon stabilises Earth's tilt, keeping seasons and climate relatively steady over millions of years. Without it, the tilt could wobble wildly.
!? When it formed, the Moon was perhaps 15 times closer than today, appearing enormous in the sky.

# Shooting stars
A meteor is a flash of light made by a grain of dust or rock, often smaller than a grain of sand, hitting the atmosphere at 11–72 km/s. It's not burning like fire: compressing air in front of it heats it, and it vaporises, making the air glow.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Meteoroid", "b": "rock in space"}, {"a": "Meteor", "b": "streak of light"}, {"a": "Meteorite", "b": "piece that reaches the ground"}, {"a": "Fireball", "b": "very bright meteor"}]}
### Meteor showers
Comets leave trails of dust along their orbits. When Earth crosses one, many meteors appear to come from one point in the sky, the radiant, which names the shower.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Early Jan", "l": "Quadrantids"}, {"y": "Mid Aug", "l": "Perseids, comet Swift–Tuttle"}, {"y": "Late Oct", "l": "Orionids, Halley"}, {"y": "Mid Dec", "l": "Geminids"}]}
### Watching tips
• Go somewhere dark, lie back and let your eyes adapt for 20 minutes
• The best hours are after midnight, when your side of Earth faces "forward" into the debris
• No telescope needed: they streak across the whole sky
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "meteors per hour at peak, dark sky", "items": [{"l": "Orionids", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Quadrantids", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Perseids", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Geminids", "v": 120.0, "hi": true}]}
### Meteorites
About 50 tonnes of space dust and rock fall on Earth every day, mostly as dust. Meteorites that survive are prized: some are 4.6 billion years old, older than any Earth rock.
!? Swift–Tuttle, the comet behind the Perseids, is about 26 km wide, more than twice the size of the asteroid that ended the dinosaurs. Its orbit is being watched.

# Spotting planets
Stars twinkle because they're points of light, easily jostled by moving air. Planets are tiny discs, so their light is steadier. Planets also wander against the background stars over days and weeks, always staying near the Sun's path, the ecliptic.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Stars", "items": ["twinkle", "fixed patterns", "any part of sky"]}, "r": {"t": "Planets", "items": ["steady light", "move over weeks", "near the ecliptic"]}}
### The five naked-eye planets
• **Venus**: the brightest, never far from the Sun; "evening star" or "morning star"
• **Jupiter**: very bright, visible much of the night when well placed
• **Mars**: distinctly orange-red; very bright every ~26 months
• **Saturn**: steady yellowish; its rings need a small telescope
• **Mercury**: hard to catch, low in twilight

### With binoculars
Steady binoculars show Jupiter's four big moons as tiny dots that move from night to night, just as Galileo saw them in 1610. Venus shows phases like the Moon through a small telescope.

# Exoplanets
For centuries, people wondered whether other stars had planets. The first confirmed exoplanets, found in 1992, orbited a pulsar, a dead star. In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found 51 Pegasi b, a giant planet orbiting a Sun-like star every 4 days, winning the 2019 Nobel Prize.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1992", "l": "pulsar planets"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "51 Pegasi b"}, {"y": "2009–18", "l": "Kepler finds thousands"}, {"y": "2018", "l": "TESS launched"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "over 5,000 confirmed"}]}
### A zoo of worlds
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Exoplanet types", "items": ["Hot Jupiters: giants close to their star", "Super-Earths: rocky, bigger than Earth", "Mini-Neptunes: the most common type", "Rogue planets: no star at all"]}
### Statistics
Kepler's survey suggests most stars have planets, and there are likely more planets than stars in the Milky Way. Super-Earths and mini-Neptunes, which don't exist in our Solar System, are the most common types.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of known exoplanets, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Neptune-like", "v": 35.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Gas giants", "v": 31.0}, {"l": "Super-Earths", "v": 31.0}, {"l": "Earth-sized rocky", "v": 3.0}]}
### Famous systems
• **TRAPPIST-1**: seven Earth-sized planets around a red dwarf 40 light-years away
• **Proxima b**: a planet in the habitable zone of the nearest star
• **Kepler-16b**: orbits two suns, like Tatooine
> Our Solar System, with small rocky planets inside and giants outside, may not be typical at all.

# Finding them
Planets are tiny and faint next to their stars, so most are found indirectly, by their effect on the star.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Detection methods", "items": ["Transit: star dims", "Radial velocity: star wobbles", "Direct imaging: photo of the planet", "Microlensing: gravity magnifies a background star"]}
### Transits
When a planet crosses in front of its star, it blocks a small fraction of the light. Jupiter would dim the Sun by about 1%; Earth by only 0.008%. Repeated, regular dips reveal the planet's size and orbit.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "dip in starlight, %", "items": [{"l": "Earth-size", "v": 0.008}, {"l": "Neptune-size", "v": 0.13}, {"l": "Jupiter-size", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}]}
### Radial velocity
A planet and its star orbit a shared centre, so the star wobbles. Its light shifts slightly blue as it approaches and red as it recedes. Earth makes the Sun wobble by only about 9 cm/s, walking pace for a snail.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Planet pulls star", "Star wobbles", "Light shifts blue and red", "Planet's mass found"]}
### Combining methods
Transits give a planet's size; radial velocity gives its mass. Together they give density, telling us whether it's rocky, watery or gassy.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "density = mass ÷ volume", "items": [{"s": "mass", "m": "from the wobble"}, {"s": "volume", "m": "from the transit size"}]}
!? Webb can analyse starlight filtering through a transiting planet's atmosphere and has detected water vapour, CO₂ and methane on distant worlds.

# The Goldilocks zone
The habitable zone is the range of orbits where a planet with an atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface. Its location depends on the star: close in for cool red dwarfs, farther out for hot stars.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Red dwarf", "items": ["zone very close", "planet may be tidally locked", "flares"]}, "r": {"t": "Sun-like star", "items": ["zone at ~1 AU", "stable light"]}}
### Our Solar System's zone
The Sun's habitable zone spans roughly from just outside Venus to around Mars. Earth sits comfortably inside; Mars is near the outer edge but too small to hold a thick atmosphere.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.3, "hi": 2.0, "unit": "AU", "items": [{"l": "Venus", "v": 0.72}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 1.52}]}
### It's not that simple
• Atmosphere matters: Venus would be habitable distance-wise if it lacked its greenhouse
• Moons outside the zone, like Europa, may have oceans heated by tides
• Planets need a magnetic field and time for life to develop
!! Habitable zone means a planet has life => It only means liquid water is possible on the surface
### The zone moves
As stars age, they brighten, and the habitable zone moves outwards. In about a billion years, Earth will drift out of the inner edge.
> Many scientists prefer "temperate zone": "habitable" promises more than we know.

# What life needs
Every living thing we know needs liquid water as a solvent, a source of energy (sunlight or chemical reactions) and chemistry built mainly from carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulphur, often remembered as CHNOPS.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Water", "b": "solvent where chemistry happens"}, {"a": "Energy", "b": "sunlight or chemicals"}, {"a": "Carbon", "b": "backbone of complex molecules"}, {"a": "CHNOPS", "b": "the six key elements"}]}
### Why carbon and water?
Carbon forms four bonds and long, stable chains: perfect for complex molecules like DNA. Water stays liquid over a wide temperature range, dissolves many substances and floats when frozen, insulating lakes below.
### Life at the edges
Earth's extremophiles expand the possibilities: microbes thrive in boiling vents, Antarctic ice, acid pools, nuclear reactor water and kilometres down in solid rock.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": -25.0, "hi": 125.0, "unit": "°C", "items": [{"l": "Coldest active microbes", "v": -20.0}, {"l": "Human body", "v": 37.0}, {"l": "Boiling water", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Hottest microbes", "v": 122.0, "hi": true}]}
### Biosignatures
To find life elsewhere, scientists look for chemical signs that are hard to explain without biology, such as oxygen and methane together in an atmosphere, which react and would vanish unless constantly replenished.
!? Life on Earth appeared within a few hundred million years of the planet cooling: a hint that it may arise easily where conditions allow.

# Places to look nearby
Our best chances to find life beyond Earth may be in our own Solar System, where spacecraft can visit and sample.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Candidates", "items": ["Mars: past habitable lakes", "Europa: ocean under ice", "Enceladus: ocean spraying into space", "Titan: methane lakes and an inner ocean"]}
### Mars
Mars had lakes, rivers and a thicker atmosphere over 3 billion years ago. Perseverance is collecting samples from an ancient lake bed; any life today would likely be microbial, underground.
### Enceladus
Cassini flew through plumes of water erupting from cracks at Enceladus' south pole and found salts, silica and organic molecules: signs of a salty ocean in contact with hot rock, much like Earth's hydrothermal vents.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ocean under the ice", "Hot rock beneath", "Water sprays through cracks", "Cassini samples it"]}
### Europa and Titan
• Europa Clipper (launched 2024) will study Europa's ocean from orbit from 2030
• Dragonfly, a nuclear-powered drone, is planned to fly across Titan in the 2030s
• Titan's lakes of methane could host very different chemistry
!? Enceladus is only about 500 km wide, about the length of mainland Portugal, yet it may have one of the most promising oceans for life.

# The Drake equation
Frank Drake wrote his equation for the first SETI meeting in 1961. It isn't meant to be solved exactly but to organise our ignorance: it breaks a big question into smaller ones we can study.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "N = R* · fp · ne · fl · fi · fc · L", "items": [{"s": "R*", "m": "stars formed per year"}, {"s": "fp", "m": "share with planets"}, {"s": "ne", "m": "habitable planets per system"}, {"s": "fl", "m": "share where life starts"}, {"s": "fi", "m": "share developing intelligence"}, {"s": "fc", "m": "share that signal"}, {"s": "L", "m": "years they keep signalling"}]}
### What we've learnt
Since 1961, astronomers have measured the first factors: stars form at a few per year, and nearly all have planets, many in habitable zones. The biological and social factors remain complete guesses.

### Wildly different answers
Plug in optimistic guesses and you get millions of civilisations; pessimistic ones give less than one. The lifetime L dominates: if civilisations broadcast for only a century, we'd have little overlap with any.

> The equation's real value is showing which questions matter: how often life starts, and how long technological civilisations last.

# The Fermi paradox
In 1950, over lunch at Los Alamos, physicist Enrico Fermi asked, "Where is everybody?" Even with slow rockets, a civilisation could spread across the galaxy in a few million years, tiny compared with its 13-billion-year age. So why no sign of anyone?
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Galaxy is old", "Colonising is fast by comparison", "We should see someone", "Silence"]}
### Proposed answers
• **Rare Earth**: complex life needs a rare combination of conditions
• **Great Filter**: some step, early or late, almost always fails
• **Short-lived civilisations**: they destroy themselves or fade quickly
• **Too far, too slow**: signals are weak and travel takes too long
• **Zoo hypothesis**: they know we're here but stay hidden
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "The Great Filter", "items": ["Behind us: life is rare", "Ahead of us: civilisations self-destruct"]}
### Why it matters
If the filter is behind us, life may be rare and we're lucky. If it's ahead, civilisations like ours may tend to end early: a sobering reason to manage risks wisely.
!! Silence proves no one is out there => We've searched a tiny fraction of stars and frequencies
!? Astronomer Jill Tarter compared our search so far to scooping one glass of water from the ocean and concluding there are no fish.

# SETI
SETI looks for "technosignatures": signs of technology rather than biology. Radio is the favourite medium because it crosses the galaxy easily, and a narrow-band signal, concentrated at one frequency, would stand out from natural noise.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Point radio telescope", "Record many frequencies", "Filter out Earth interference", "Look for narrow, repeating signals"]}
### The Wow! signal
On 15 August 1977, the Big Ear telescope in Ohio detected a strong narrow-band signal lasting 72 seconds. Astronomer Jerry Ehman circled it and wrote "Wow!" in the margin. It was never seen again, and its origin is still debated.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "72 s", "l": "duration"}, {"v": "1977", "l": "date"}, {"v": "0", "l": "repeat detections"}]}
### Modern searches
• **Breakthrough Listen** (from 2016) surveys a million nearby stars and 100 galaxies
• Searches now include laser flashes and infrared heat from huge structures
• Citizen scientists once lent their home computers to SETI@home (1999–2020)
### False alarms
Pulsars were first nicknamed "LGM" (little green men) in 1967 because their pulses were so regular. In 2019 a promising signal turned out to be human-made interference.
> The odds are long, but the payoff would be the most important discovery in history.

# Messages we sent
Humanity has sent a few deliberate messages into space, designed to be understood without a shared language: through mathematics, physics and pictures.

### The Arecibo message
Written by Frank Drake and Carl Sagan, it was 1,679 bits long, a product of two primes (23 × 73) so it could be arranged into a grid. The picture shows numbers 1–10, DNA's elements, a human figure, the Solar System and the telescope.

Pioneer 10 and 11 carry plaques showing a man and woman, the Solar System and the Sun's position relative to 14 pulsars, which work like a cosmic map.

Some scientists argue against "messaging extraterrestrial intelligence" (METI) until we know more: if others exist, announcing ourselves may be unwise. Others say our radio leakage has been spreading for a century anyway.

On 14 February 1990, just before its cameras were switched off, Voyager 1 turned around and took a "family portrait" of the planets from about 6 billion km away. Earth appeared as a pale blue speck, 0.12 pixels wide, caught in a sunbeam scattered in the camera.

### Sagan's reflection
Carl Sagan had pushed NASA to take the photo. In his 1994 book he wrote that every human who ever lived, "every saint and sinner", lived on that mote of dust, and that it underscores our responsibility to treat each other kindly and preserve our only home.

# Why it's red
Mars' surface is covered in fine dust rich in iron oxide, the same compound as rust. Over billions of years, iron in volcanic rocks reacted with small amounts of oxygen and water, and the planet literally rusted. Below the top layer, many rocks are darker grey or black basalt.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Iron-rich volcanic rock", "Reacts with traces of oxygen and water", "Iron oxide dust", "Wind spreads it everywhere"]}
### A butterscotch sky
Dust suspended in the thin air scatters red light, so the daytime sky looks pinkish-tan. At sunset the opposite happens: fine dust scatters blue light forwards, and Martian sunsets glow blue.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth", "items": ["blue day sky", "red sunsets"]}, "r": {"t": "Mars", "items": ["butterscotch day sky", "blue sunsets"]}}
### The dust itself
• Particles are tiny, around 3 micrometres, finer than flour
• The dust is electrostatic and sticks to solar panels and spacesuits
• It contains perchlorates, salts that are toxic to humans: a challenge for future crews
!? The ancient Romans named Mars after their god of war because of its blood-red colour; the Greeks called it Ares.

# A small, cold world
Mars is 6,779 km wide, about 53% of Earth's diameter, with only about 11% of Earth's mass. Its gravity is 38% of ours: a 70 kg person would feel like 27 kg.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Diameter", "b": "53% of Earth's"}, {"a": "Mass", "b": "11% of Earth's"}, {"a": "Gravity", "b": "38% of Earth's"}, {"a": "Air pressure", "b": "less than 1% of Earth's"}]}
### Thin air
The atmosphere is 95% carbon dioxide, but so thin, about 0.6% of Earth's sea-level pressure, that water can't stay liquid on the surface: it boils or freezes. Unprotected, a human's blood would boil at body temperature.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "air pressure, % of Earth sea level", "items": [{"l": "Mars", "v": 0.6, "hi": true}, {"l": "Top of Everest", "v": 33.0}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 100.0}]}
### Wild temperature swings
With thin air holding little heat, temperatures swing widely: near the equator a summer day can reach about +20 °C at noon and plunge to −70 °C at night. Polar winters drop below −120 °C, cold enough to freeze CO₂ into dry-ice snow.
### Why Mars lost its air
Mars has no global magnetic field. Without that shield, the solar wind gradually stripped its atmosphere over billions of years, measured directly by NASA's MAVEN orbiter.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Core cooled", "Magnetic field faded", "Solar wind strips the air", "Thin, cold planet"]}
> Small planets cool faster: Mars' smaller size is probably why its core and magnetic field died early.

# Day and year
A Martian solar day, called a sol, lasts 24 hours 39 minutes. Mission controllers living on "Mars time" shift their schedule by about 40 minutes every day, which is famously exhausting.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "24 h 39 min", "l": "one sol"}, {"v": "687 Earth days", "l": "one year"}, {"v": "668", "l": "sols in a Martian year"}]}
### Seasons
Mars' axis is tilted 25°, similar to Earth's 23.4°, so it has seasons too. But its orbit is more elliptical, so seasons are uneven: southern summers are shorter and hotter, and they often trigger dust storms.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth", "items": ["tilt 23.4°", "near-circular orbit", "even seasons"]}, "r": {"t": "Mars", "items": ["tilt 25°", "elliptical orbit", "uneven seasons"]}}
### Seasons in numbers
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "length, sols (northern hemisphere)", "items": [{"l": "Spring", "v": 194.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Summer", "v": 178.0}, {"l": "Autumn", "v": 142.0}, {"l": "Winter", "v": 154.0}]}
• Polar ice caps grow and shrink with the seasons as CO₂ freezes and evaporates
• About a quarter of Mars' atmosphere freezes onto the poles in winter
!? Mars' tilt isn't stabilised by a large moon, so over millions of years it wobbles between roughly 15° and 45°, causing big climate shifts.

# Olympus Mons
Olympus Mons is a shield volcano, built by countless eruptions of runny lava, like Hawaii's volcanoes but vastly bigger. It rises about 22 km above the surrounding plains and covers an area roughly the size of Poland.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "height above surroundings, km", "items": [{"l": "Mont Blanc", "v": 4.8}, {"l": "Everest", "v": 8.8}, {"l": "Mauna Kea, from seabed", "v": 10.2}, {"l": "Olympus Mons", "v": 22.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why so big?
• **No plate tectonics**: on Earth, plates move over hotspots, creating chains of smaller volcanoes; on Mars, the crust stays put, so lava piles up in one place
• **Lower gravity**: allows taller structures before they collapse
• **Long life**: eruptions may have continued for billions of years
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth, Hawaii", "items": ["plate moves", "chain of islands"]}, "r": {"t": "Mars, Olympus", "items": ["plate fixed", "one giant volcano"]}}
### Standing on it
Its slopes are so gentle, about 5°, that standing on the summit you couldn't see it was a mountain: the horizon on Mars is close, and the volcano's edges would be far beyond it. Its summit caldera is about 80 km wide.
!? Olympus Mons is ringed by cliffs up to 8 km high, taller than Everest.

# Valles Marineris
Valles Marineris is a system of canyons about 4,000 km long, up to 200 km wide and 7 km deep. It would span the continental United States or stretch roughly from Lisbon to Moscow.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "depth, km", "items": [{"l": "Grand Canyon", "v": 1.8}, {"l": "Colca Canyon, Peru", "v": 3.3}, {"l": "Valles Marineris", "v": 7.0, "hi": true}]}
### Not carved by a river
Unlike the Grand Canyon, Valles Marineris formed mainly as the crust cracked and pulled apart, probably when the nearby Tharsis volcanic region bulged upwards. Later, landslides and possibly water widened it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tharsis bulges up", "Crust stretches and cracks", "Rift valleys form", "Landslides widen them"]}
### Named after a spacecraft
It's named after Mariner 9, the first spacecraft to orbit another planet, in 1971. When it arrived, a global dust storm hid everything except the tops of the giant volcanoes.
• Layered rocks on its walls record billions of years of Martian history
• Some areas show minerals that formed in water
> On Earth the closest comparison is the East African Rift, also formed by the crust pulling apart.

# Water on Mars
The evidence that Mars was once wet is overwhelming: dry river valleys, deltas, lake beds, and minerals like clays and sulphates that only form in water. Curiosity found ancient lake sediments in Gale Crater; Perseverance landed on a river delta in Jezero Crater.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "4.1–3.7 billion years ago", "l": "wet era, lakes and rivers"}, {"y": "3.5 billion", "l": "drying"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "cold desert with ice"}]}
### Where's the water now?
• Frozen in the polar ice caps; the south cap alone could cover Mars in water about 11 m deep
• Buried as ice just below the surface across large regions
• Some lost to space as the atmosphere thinned
• Possibly locked in minerals or deep underground
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Mars' water", "items": ["Polar ice caps", "Buried ice", "Lost to space", "Locked in rocks"]}
### Liquid water today?
Pure liquid water can't last on the surface, but salty brines may form briefly. In 2024 seismic data from the InSight lander suggested a deep reservoir of liquid water 10–20 km down, too deep to reach easily.
!! Mars has always been a dry desert => It once had lakes, rivers and perhaps a northern ocean
> If life ever started on Mars, the wet era over 3.5 billion years ago is when it most likely happened.

# Two tiny moons
Phobos (fear) and Deimos (dread), named after the sons of the Greek war god, were discovered in 1877 by Asaph Hall. They're small, dark and potato-shaped, more like asteroids than our round Moon.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Phobos", "items": ["~22 km", "orbits in 7.7 h", "rises in the west"]}, "r": {"t": "Deimos", "items": ["~12 km", "orbits in 30 h", "slowly drifts away"]}}
### Phobos' doom
Phobos orbits only about 6,000 km above Mars' surface, closer than any other moon to its planet, and it's spiralling inwards by about 2 m per century. In 30–50 million years it will either crash into Mars or be torn apart into a ring.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Phobos orbits fast and low", "Tides drag it inwards", "Breaks apart or crashes", "Possible ring around Mars"]}
### Where did they come from?
They may be captured asteroids, but their orbits are neat circles above the equator, which fits better with formation from debris after a giant impact, like our Moon. Japan's MMX mission aims to bring samples from Phobos to settle the question.
!? From Mars, Phobos rises in the west and sets in the east twice a day, because it orbits faster than Mars spins.

# Dust storms
Mars' dust storms are driven by sunlight heating dust, which warms the air, which lifts more dust: a feedback loop. Local storms are common; every few Martian years, they merge into planet-wide storms lasting months.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sun heats dusty air", "Air rises and winds pick up", "More dust lifted", "Storm spreads"]}
### Not like the film
Martian winds can be fast, but the air is so thin that a 100 km/h gust would feel like a light breeze. The dramatic storm that strands the astronaut in *The Martian* couldn't really knock over equipment.
!! Mars storms can blow over rockets => Thin air means even fast winds push very gently
### Real dangers
• Dust blocks sunlight, starving solar-powered machines (Opportunity, 2018)
• Fine dust gets into seals, joints and lungs
• Storms change temperatures and the atmosphere's structure

### Dust devils
Whirlwinds of dust, some kilometres tall, sweep across the plains, leaving dark tracks. They sometimes helpfully cleaned rovers' solar panels.
> Future crews will likely prefer nuclear power, which works day and night through any storm.

# Getting there
Earth and Mars orbit at different speeds, so the distance between them varies from about 55 million km to 400 million km. Spacecraft use a "Hohmann transfer": an efficient half-ellipse that leaves Earth and meets Mars on the far side of the Sun.

### Launch windows
Efficient alignments come every 26 months, so missions cluster: in 2020, NASA, China and the UAE all launched to Mars within weeks.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Jul 2020", "l": "three missions launch"}, {"y": "Feb 2021", "l": "all arrive"}, {"y": "Late 2026", "l": "next window"}, {"y": "Early 2029", "l": "following window"}]}
### Landing is hard
The "seven minutes of terror": a lander enters at about 20,000 km/h and must stop within minutes, using a heat shield, parachute and rockets. The thin air is too thin for parachutes alone but thick enough to burn up. About half of all Mars missions have failed.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Heat shield: from 20,000 km/h", "Parachute: to ~300 km/h", "Rockets: final descent", "Touchdown"]}
!? Because of the radio delay of up to 24 minutes, landings are fully automatic: by the time engineers hear the craft has entered the atmosphere, it has already landed or crashed.

# Living on Mars?
Mars is the most Earth-like planet nearby, but it's still extremely hostile. Every human necessity, air, water, food, warmth and protection, would have to be engineered.

### Radiation
Without a magnetic field or thick air, Mars' surface gets about 40–50 times Earth's background radiation. Habitats would likely be covered by metres of soil or built in lava tubes.

### Living off the land
NASA's MOXIE experiment on Perseverance proved oxygen can be extracted from Martian CO₂. The same chemistry could produce rocket fuel for the trip home, a key idea called in-situ resource use.

### Health and psychology
• Muscle and bone loss in low gravity, though 38% may help compared with zero

# Gas and ice giants
The giant planets formed beyond the "frost line", where ice was abundant. Jupiter and Saturn grew cores big enough fast enough to capture vast hydrogen envelopes. Uranus and Neptune formed later or farther out, where material was sparser, so they captured less gas.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Hydrogen-helium atmosphere", "Fluid layers", "Dense core of rock and ice"]}
### Inside the two kinds
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gas giants", "items": ["mostly hydrogen and helium", "metallic hydrogen inside", "huge"]}, "r": {"t": "Ice giants", "items": ["mostly water, ammonia, methane", "hot dense \"ocean\"", "smaller"]}}
### Colours
Jupiter's bands come from ammonia clouds and unknown colouring compounds. Uranus and Neptune look blue-green because methane in their air absorbs red light.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Jupiter", "b": "bands of white and red-brown"}, {"a": "Saturn", "b": "pale gold"}, {"a": "Uranus", "b": "cyan"}, {"a": "Neptune", "b": "deeper blue"}]}
### Diamond rain?
Inside Uranus and Neptune, extreme pressure may break methane apart and squeeze the carbon into diamonds that sink slowly towards the core. Lab experiments with lasers have produced tiny diamonds under similar conditions.
!? Many known exoplanets are "mini-Neptunes", so understanding our own ice giants helps explain worlds across the galaxy.

# Jupiter's protection?
The popular idea is that Jupiter acts as a cosmic shield, catching or flinging away comets that would otherwise hit Earth. Reality is more mixed: simulations show Jupiter also nudges asteroids and comets into the inner Solar System.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Shield", "items": ["absorbs or ejects comets"]}, "r": {"t": "Slingshot", "items": ["deflects some towards us"]}}
### Impacts on Jupiter
In 1994 comet Shoemaker–Levy 9, torn into 21 pieces by Jupiter's gravity, crashed into the planet over six days. The impacts left dark scars larger than Earth, watched by telescopes worldwide. Amateur astronomers still catch small flashes of impacts.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1992", "l": "comet torn apart"}, {"y": "Jul 1994", "l": "21 fragments hit"}, {"y": "Weeks later", "l": "Earth-sized dark scars visible"}]}
### Shaping the Solar System
• Jupiter's gravity stopped a planet forming in the asteroid belt
• It may have migrated inwards and back out early on (the "Grand Tack"), keeping Mars small
• Its resonances create gaps in the asteroid belt (Kirkwood gaps)
> Jupiter is less a bodyguard than a traffic controller: it shapes where small bodies go, for better and worse.

# Io, the volcanic moon
Io, slightly bigger than our Moon, is the most volcanically active body known, with over 400 active volcanoes. Some fountain sulphur plumes up to 400 km high; lava lakes are hotter than Earth's lavas.

### Tidal heating
Io's orbit is slightly oval because Europa and Ganymede tug on it rhythmically. As Io moves closer and farther from Jupiter, its rocky body flexes by up to about 100 m, generating heat by friction like bending a paperclip back and forth.

### The resonance

### A colourful world
Sulphur compounds paint Io yellow, orange, red and white, giving it the look of a pizza. It has almost no impact craters, because lava resurfaces it constantly.

# Europa's ocean
Europa, slightly smaller than our Moon, has a smooth icy crust criss-crossed by cracks and ridges. Beneath an ice shell perhaps 15–25 km thick lies a global salty ocean around 60–150 km deep, kept liquid by tidal heating.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Ice shell: 15–25 km", "Liquid ocean: 60–150 km", "Rocky mantle", "Metal core"]}
### Evidence for an ocean
• Galileo detected a magnetic signal consistent with a salty, conductive ocean
• Few craters: the surface is young and constantly renewed
• "Chaos terrain" looks like icebergs that broke, shifted and refroze
• Hubble and Webb spotted possible water plumes and surface CO₂
### Why it's exciting
The ocean is in contact with rock, which could supply chemical energy like Earth's deep-sea vents. Water, energy and chemistry are the three ingredients for life.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ocean meets rocky seafloor", "Chemical reactions", "Possible energy for microbes"]}
### Europa Clipper
Launched in October 2024, Europa Clipper will make about 50 flybys from 2030, measuring the ice thickness, ocean saltiness and surface chemistry. It's the largest planetary spacecraft NASA has built, with solar panels over 30 m across.
!? Europa probably holds about twice as much liquid water as all of Earth's oceans combined.

# Ganymede
Ganymede is 5,268 km wide, bigger than Mercury, though only half its mass because it's made largely of ice. It's the only moon known to generate its own magnetic field, which creates small auroras around it.

### A layered world
Ganymede likely has an iron core, a rocky mantle, and thick ice layers sandwiching a salty ocean that may hold more water than Earth's oceans.

### Two kinds of terrain
• Dark, ancient regions heavily cratered
• Lighter, grooved terrain where the ice was cracked and resurfaced
### ESA's JUICE
The JUpiter ICy moons Explorer, launched in 2023, will arrive in 2031 and later become the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than ours: Ganymede.

# Titan
Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is the only moon with a thick atmosphere, about 1.5 times Earth's surface pressure, mostly nitrogen. At −179 °C, methane plays the role water plays on Earth: it forms clouds, rain, rivers, lakes and seas.

### Huygens' landing
In January 2005 ESA's Huygens probe parachuted through the orange haze for 2.5 hours and landed on a damp plain strewn with rounded "pebbles" of water ice: the most distant landing ever made.

# Saturn floats
Saturn's average density is about 0.69 g/cm³, less than water's 1 g/cm³. It's mostly hydrogen and helium, the lightest elements. Of course, no bathtub is big enough, and Saturn would dissolve rather than bob, but the comparison shows how light it is.

### Flattened by spin
Saturn spins once every 10.7 hours. Combined with its low density, that makes it the most flattened planet: its equator is about 10% wider than its pole-to-pole diameter.

### The hexagon
At Saturn's north pole, a jet stream forms a six-sided pattern, each side wider than Earth. It's a stable wave pattern in the atmosphere and has lasted at least since Voyager saw it in 1981.
### Many moons
Saturn has the most known moons of any planet, over 270, mostly small irregular ones captured long ago.

• Enceladus: water plumes
• Mimas: a giant crater that makes it look like the Death Star
• Iapetus: one side dark, one side bright
!? Saturn radiates more heat than it receives from the Sun, partly because helium "rain" sinks inside it and releases energy.

# Uranus on its side
Uranus is tilted about 98°, so its axis lies almost in the plane of its orbit. It likely suffered one or more huge collisions early in its history that knocked it over.

### Extreme seasons
During one part of its orbit, a pole points almost straight at the Sun; half an orbit later, it points away. Each pole gets about 42 years of daylight followed by 42 years of darkness.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1986", "l": "Voyager 2 sees the south pole facing the Sun"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "equinox"}, {"y": "2028", "l": "north pole faces the Sun"}]}
### Discovery
William Herschel found Uranus in 1781 with a homemade telescope, the first planet discovered since antiquity. He wanted to name it after King George III ("Georgium Sidus"); astronomers chose Uranus, father of Saturn.
### Mysteries
• Uranus barely emits more heat than it receives, unlike the other giants
• Its magnetic field is tilted 59° and offset from the centre
• It has 13 faint rings and 28 known moons named after Shakespeare and Pope characters
> Scientists have ranked a dedicated Uranus orbiter as a top priority for the coming decades.

# Neptune's winds
Neptune has the fastest winds measured in the Solar System, up to about 2,100 km/h, despite receiving only 1/900th of the sunlight Earth does. The energy likely comes from heat escaping its interior.

### Discovered with maths
Astronomers noticed Uranus drifting from its predicted path. Urbain Le Verrier calculated where an unseen planet must be pulling it. On 23 September 1846, Johann Galle pointed a telescope there and found Neptune within 1° of the prediction.

### Triton
Neptune's largest moon, Triton, orbits backwards, a sign it was captured, probably from the Kuiper Belt. Voyager 2 saw geysers of nitrogen erupting from its frozen surface. Tides are slowly dragging it inwards.

== sp-sun

# Layers of the Sun
The Sun has no solid surface. Its structure is defined by how energy moves outward from the core.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Corona: million-degree outer atmosphere", "Chromosphere: thin pink layer", "Photosphere: visible surface, 5,500 °C", "Convective zone: boiling motion", "Radiative zone: light bouncing outwards", "Core: fusion, 15 million °C"]}
### The slow escape
In the radiative zone, a photon of light is absorbed and re-emitted countless times in random directions. This "random walk" means energy takes from about 10,000 to 170,000 years to cross it. Then, in the convective zone, hot plasma rises like boiling soup, carrying energy to the surface in weeks.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Core makes energy", "Random walk through radiative zone, ~100,000 years", "Convection to surface", "8 minutes to Earth"]}
### Granulation
The photosphere looks grainy in close-up: granules around 1,000 km wide, about twice the length of Portugal, the tops of convection cells, each lasting about 8–20 minutes before being replaced.
• Core: about a quarter of the Sun's radius, where almost all fusion happens
• Density at the core is about 150 times that of water
!? We can "see" inside the Sun with helioseismology: tracking sound waves that make its surface ripple, much as earthquakes reveal Earth's interior.

# Hot and hotter
Moving away from a heater, it gets colder. But above the Sun's 5,500 °C surface, the temperature rises dramatically: the thin corona reaches 1–3 million °C. This "coronal heating problem" has puzzled scientists since the 1940s.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "temperature, °C", "items": [{"l": "Photosphere", "v": 5500.0}, {"l": "Chromosphere", "v": 20000.0}, {"l": "Corona", "v": 2000000.0, "t": "2000000", "hi": true}]}
### Leading explanations
• **Nanoflares**: countless tiny magnetic explosions continuously heating the corona
• **Waves**: magnetic waves carrying energy up from below and dissipating
• Parker Solar Probe and Solar Orbiter are testing both by flying close
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Coronal heating", "items": ["Nanoflares", "Magnetic waves", "Probably both"]}
### Hot but not dense
The corona is extremely thin, so even at millions of degrees it holds little heat energy. You'd freeze before you'd cook, if the Sun's light didn't get you first.
### Seeing the corona
The corona is normally hidden by the bright surface. It appears during a total solar eclipse as pearly streamers, or with coronagraphs, telescopes that block the Sun's disc.
> Temperature measures how fast particles move, not how much heat they carry; the corona's particles are fast but few.

# Sunspots
Sunspots appear where intense magnetic fields poke through the surface and block the upward flow of heat. They're about 1,500 °C cooler than their surroundings, so they look dark, although on their own they'd glow brightly.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Sunspot", "items": ["~3,800 °C", "strong magnetic field", "looks dark"]}, "r": {"t": "Surrounding surface", "items": ["~5,500 °C", "normal convection", "bright"]}}
### The 11-year cycle
Sunspot numbers rise and fall roughly every 11 years. At each solar maximum, the Sun's magnetic poles flip. Solar Cycle 25 peaked around 2024–25, producing strong auroras.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "average sunspot number (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "2008, minimum", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "2014, max", "v": 114.0}, {"l": "2019, minimum", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "2024, max", "v": 155.0, "hi": true}]}
### Size
Big sunspots can be several times wider than Earth. Groups often come in pairs with opposite magnetic polarity, like the ends of a bar magnet poking out.
### The Maunder Minimum
From about 1645 to 1715, sunspots almost vanished. This coincided with part of the "Little Ice Age", though volcanic eruptions and other factors also played a role.
!? Galileo used sunspots to show that the Sun rotates and is not a perfect, unchanging sphere.
> Never look at sunspots directly; use projection or a certified solar filter.

# Solar flares
A flare happens when twisted magnetic field lines above sunspots suddenly snap and reconnect, releasing energy equal to millions of hydrogen bombs in minutes. Flares emit X-rays and ultraviolet light that reach Earth in about 8 minutes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Magnetic field twists", "Field lines snap and reconnect", "Burst of radiation", "Reaches Earth in 8 minutes"]}
### Flare classes
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "A and B", "b": "background level"}, {"a": "C", "b": "small"}, {"a": "M", "b": "medium, radio blackouts at poles"}, {"a": "X", "b": "largest, global radio blackouts"}]}
### Coronal mass ejections
Flares often come with coronal mass ejections (CMEs): billions of tonnes of plasma hurled into space at hundreds to thousands of km/s. A CME takes 1–3 days to reach Earth, and it's the CME, not the flare, that drives big geomagnetic storms.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Flare", "items": ["flash of light and X-rays", "arrives in 8 min", "radio blackouts"]}, "r": {"t": "CME", "items": ["cloud of plasma", "arrives in 1–3 days", "geomagnetic storms"]}}
### Effects on Earth
• Radio blackouts for aircraft and ships
• GPS errors and satellite damage
• Extra drag that can bring satellites down early: in 2022, about 38 new satellites were lost after a storm
• Higher radiation on polar flights
!? The strongest flare ever recorded, in November 2003, saturated the sensors measuring it; it was later estimated at around X28 to X45.

# Solar wind
The corona is so hot that the Sun's gravity can't hold it: it streams away as the solar wind, a constant flow of electrons and protons. The slow wind travels at about 400 km/s; the fast wind, from coronal holes, at about 750 km/s.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Slow wind", "items": ["~400 km/s", "from near the equator"]}, "r": {"t": "Fast wind", "items": ["~750 km/s", "from coronal holes"]}}
### The heliosphere
The solar wind inflates a vast bubble, the heliosphere, that protects the Solar System from much galactic radiation. Voyager 1 crossed its edge, the heliopause, in 2012 at about 121 AU.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Interstellar space", "Heliopause: edge of the bubble", "Heliosphere: solar wind dominates", "Planets"]}
### Earth's shield
Earth's magnetic field deflects most of the solar wind, forming the magnetosphere, which is squashed on the Sun side and stretched into a long tail on the night side.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Solar wind arrives", "Magnetosphere deflects it", "Some particles funnel to the poles", "Auroras"]}
• The Sun loses about 1.5 million tonnes per second to the solar wind
• Comet ion tails point straight away from the Sun because the wind pushes them
> Mars, without a global magnetic field, had much of its atmosphere stripped by the solar wind.

# Auroras
Charged particles from the solar wind and CMEs are funnelled along Earth's magnetic field lines towards the poles. At 100–300 km altitude they collide with oxygen and nitrogen, exciting them to glow, like a neon sign.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Particles arrive", "Guided by magnetic field", "Hit gas high up", "Gas glows"]}
### Colours and heights
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Red: oxygen, above 200 km", "Green: oxygen, 100–200 km", "Blue and purple: nitrogen, lower"]}
### The auroral ovals
Auroras normally form in rings around the magnetic poles: northern Norway, Iceland, Canada and Alaska. During strong storms the ovals expand towards the equator. In May 2024 and October 2024, auroras were photographed from Portugal.

### Tips
• Phone cameras often capture colour the eye can't see in faint auroras
• Watch the Kp index and space weather forecasts
• Look north, away from city lights
!? Jupiter and Saturn have permanent auroras far more powerful than Earth's, powered partly by their own moons.

# The Carrington Event
On 1 September 1859, British astronomer Richard Carrington saw a brilliant white flare on the Sun. Seventeen hours later, a massive CME hit Earth. Auroras lit up skies as far south as Cuba and Hawaii; people in the Rocky Mountains woke thinking it was dawn.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1 Sep 1859", "l": "flare seen"}, {"y": "17 h later", "l": "CME hits Earth"}, {"y": "2 Sep", "l": "auroras near the tropics"}, {"y": "Days after", "l": "telegraphs disrupted"}]}
### Telegraph chaos
Telegraph lines sparked and some operators got shocks. Remarkably, some operators disconnected their batteries and sent messages using only the current induced by the storm.
### If it happened today
A storm of that size could induce currents that damage high-voltage transformers, causing long blackouts, and could disable many satellites and disrupt GPS. Studies estimate global costs in the trillions.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Modern risks", "items": ["Power grids: transformer damage", "Satellites: damage and drag", "GPS and timing: errors", "Radio: blackouts"]}
### Near misses
• 1989: a smaller storm blacked out Quebec for 9 hours
• 2012: a Carrington-class CME missed Earth by about nine days of orbit
> Grid operators now have procedures to protect transformers when big storms are forecast.

# Space weather forecasts
Space weather forecasters watch the Sun constantly, much like meteorologists watch the atmosphere. Spacecraft such as SOHO and the Solar Dynamics Observatory image eruptions, and probes at the L1 point, 1.5 million km sunward, measure the solar wind before it hits Earth.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Spot eruption on the Sun", "Estimate speed and direction", "Model arrival", "L1 probes confirm, 15–60 min warning"]}
### Scales
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "G1–G5", "b": "geomagnetic storms"}, {"a": "S1–S5", "b": "solar radiation storms"}, {"a": "R1–R5", "b": "radio blackouts"}, {"a": "Kp 0–9", "b": "geomagnetic activity"}]}
### Who uses it
• Power grid operators reduce load on vulnerable transformers
• Airlines reroute polar flights to avoid radiation and radio blackouts
• Satellite operators safe-mode their spacecraft
• Astronauts shelter in shielded parts of the station
### Forecast limits
It's easy to see a CME leave, but hard to predict how strong its effect will be: that depends on the direction of its magnetic field, only measured when it reaches L1, under an hour before arrival.
> Space weather is now listed among national risks in many countries, alongside pandemics and floods.

# Never look directly
The eye's lens focuses sunlight onto the retina like a magnifying glass. Staring at the Sun can burn retinal cells (solar retinopathy) without any pain, because the retina has no pain receptors. Damage can be permanent.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sunlight focused by the lens", "Concentrated on the retina", "Cells burned, painless", "Possible permanent blind spot"]}
### Partial eclipses are the danger
On a normal day, the Sun's glare makes you look away. During a partial eclipse, the sky darkens and people stare longer, but the visible sliver is just as intense.

### Safe ways
• Certified eclipse glasses meeting ISO 12312-2
• Pinhole projector: a hole in card projecting the Sun onto paper
• Telescopes only with proper solar filters on the front

### Totality exception
During the brief total phase of a total eclipse, it's safe to look without glasses, but put them back on the moment the Sun reappears.

# Parker Solar Probe
Parker Solar Probe, named after Eugene Parker who predicted the solar wind in 1958, is designed to "touch the Sun". On 24 December 2024 it passed just 6.1 million km from the surface, the closest any spacecraft has come.

### Surviving the heat
A 11.4 cm thick carbon-composite heat shield faces the Sun at up to about 1,400 °C, while the instruments behind it stay near room temperature. The probe must keep the shield perfectly angled, doing so autonomously.

### Getting close is hard

== sp-bigbang

# An expanding universe
In the 1910s Vesto Slipher measured the spectra of "spiral nebulae" and found most were redshifted: moving away from us. In 1929 Edwin Hubble combined these speeds with distances and found a simple pattern: the farther a galaxy, the faster it recedes.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = H₀ × d", "items": [{"s": "v", "m": "recession speed"}, {"s": "H₀", "m": "about 70 km/s per megaparsec"}, {"s": "d", "m": "distance"}]}
### What it means
This isn't galaxies flying through space away from us in particular. Space itself is stretching, carrying galaxies apart. Every observer in every galaxy would see the same pattern.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Space stretches", "Distances between galaxies grow", "Farther galaxies recede faster", "Same view from anywhere"]}
### Not everything expands
Galaxies, solar systems and atoms don't grow: gravity and other forces hold them together. Expansion only wins over huge distances between galaxy groups.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Expands", "items": ["space between distant galaxies"]}, "r": {"t": "Doesn't expand", "items": ["atoms", "you", "Solar System", "Milky Way"]}}
!? Einstein originally added a "cosmological constant" to keep the universe static; after Hubble's discovery he dropped it.

# Run it backwards
If galaxies are moving apart now, they were closer in the past. Run the expansion backwards and everything converges into a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. That early hot phase, and the expansion from it, is what scientists call the Big Bang.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Today: galaxies far apart", "Billions of years ago: closer", "Early universe: hot and dense", "13.8 billion years ago: the Big Bang"]}
### Measuring the age
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Expansion rate", "b": "~13–14 billion years"}, {"a": "Cosmic microwave background", "b": "13.79 ± 0.02 billion years (Planck)"}, {"a": "Oldest stars", "b": "~13 billion years"}]}
### What it doesn't say
The Big Bang theory describes how the universe evolved from a hot, dense state. It doesn't explain what, if anything, came "before" or what caused it; physics as we know it breaks down at the very beginning.
!! The Big Bang explains the origin of everything => It describes the evolution from a hot, dense early state; the very beginning is still unknown
### Three pillars of evidence
• Galaxies receding in proportion to distance
• The cosmic microwave background, leftover heat from the early universe
• The amounts of hydrogen, helium and lithium, matching predictions
> No other theory explains all three pieces of evidence as well.

# Not an explosion in space
The name suggests a bomb going off at a point, with debris flying into empty space. But the Big Bang happened everywhere at once: it was space itself expanding, not things flying through pre-existing space.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Explosion", "items": ["centre", "edge", "debris in empty space"]}, "r": {"t": "Big Bang", "items": ["no centre", "no edge", "space itself stretches"]}}
### No centre
Because every point in space was part of the early dense universe, there's no special place where it "happened". Wherever you are, the Big Bang happened there too.
### Faster than light?
Very distant galaxies recede faster than light. That doesn't break relativity: nothing is moving through space faster than light; space between us is stretching.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Small stretch per distance", "Adds up over huge distances", "Distant galaxies recede faster than light", "No law broken"]}
### Inflation
Many cosmologists think a tiny fraction of a second after the start, space expanded extremely fast, by a factor of at least 10²⁶, in "cosmic inflation". It would explain why the universe is so smooth and flat.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "10⁻³⁶ s", "l": "inflation begins"}, {"y": "10⁻³² s", "l": "inflation ends"}, {"y": "1 s", "l": "neutrinos decouple"}, {"y": "3 min", "l": "first nuclei"}]}
!? The term "Big Bang" was coined by Fred Hoyle, who didn't believe in it, and the theory's main early proponent was a Catholic priest, Georges Lemaître.

# Raisin bread
Picture a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven. Each raisin sees every other raisin moving away, and raisins twice as far apart separate twice as fast. No raisin is at the centre.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Dough", "b": "space"}, {"a": "Raisins", "b": "galaxy clusters"}, {"a": "Rising", "b": "cosmic expansion"}, {"a": "Raisins don't grow", "b": "galaxies stay the same size"}]}
### The balloon version
Another analogy: dots drawn on a balloon spread apart as it inflates. It shows there's no centre on the surface, but suggests a 3D centre inside the balloon, which doesn't exist for the real universe.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Raisin bread", "items": ["3D", "good for \"no centre\"", "but has an edge"]}, "r": {"t": "Balloon", "items": ["2D surface", "no edge", "but implies a centre inside"]}}
### Doing the maths
If every distance doubles in an hour, a raisin 1 cm away ends up 2 cm away (moved 1 cm), while one 3 cm away ends up 6 cm away (moved 3 cm). Speed is proportional to distance: exactly Hubble's law.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "distance after doubling, cm", "items": [{"l": "Raisin 1 cm away", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "2 cm away", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "3 cm away", "v": 6.0, "hi": true}]}
> All analogies break somewhere; the universe may have no edge and may be infinite.

# The first minutes
In the first second, the universe was a hot soup of particles. As it expanded and cooled, quarks bound into protons and neutrons. Between about 1 and 3 minutes, protons and neutrons fused into nuclei: Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "10⁻¹² s", "l": "fundamental forces separate"}, {"y": "10⁻⁶ s", "l": "protons and neutrons form"}, {"y": "1–3 min", "l": "helium nuclei form"}, {"y": "20 min", "l": "fusion stops"}]}
### The recipe
By the time fusion stopped, ordinary matter was about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, with traces of deuterium and lithium. Measurements of the oldest gas clouds and stars match these predictions closely.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of ordinary matter by mass", "items": [{"l": "Hydrogen", "v": 75.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Helium", "v": 25.0}, {"l": "Lithium", "v": 1e-07, "t": "0.0000001"}]}
### Why fusion stopped
The universe was cooling and thinning too fast. Unlike stars, which fuse for billions of years, the early universe had only minutes, so it couldn't build heavy elements. Those came much later, in stars.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hot dense soup", "Cools as it expands", "Brief window of fusion", "Too thin and cold to continue"]}
!? The amount of helium in the universe was a key test: the Big Bang predicted about 25%, and that's what we observe.

# The afterglow
For 380,000 years the universe was a glowing fog of charged particles that scattered light. When it cooled to about 3,000 °C, electrons joined nuclei to form neutral atoms and light could travel freely. That light still fills the universe, stretched by expansion into microwaves at 2.7 K (−270 °C).
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Opaque plasma fog", "Cools to ~3,000 °C", "Atoms form", "Light escapes", "Stretched into microwaves today"]}
### Found by accident
In 1965 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, testing a radio antenna in New Jersey, found an annoying hiss coming from every direction. They even cleaned pigeon droppings from the antenna. The hiss was the cosmic microwave background (CMB), and they won the 1978 Nobel Prize.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1948", "l": "CMB predicted"}, {"y": "1965", "l": "discovered"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "COBE finds ripples"}, {"y": "2013", "l": "Planck's detailed map"}]}
### The baby picture of the universe
The CMB is almost perfectly uniform, but has tiny temperature ripples, about 1 part in 100,000. These were the seeds of all later structure: galaxies and clusters grew from them.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Average temperature", "b": "2.725 K"}, {"a": "Ripples", "b": "1 part in 100,000"}, {"a": "Seeds of", "b": "galaxies and clusters"}]}
!? About 1% of the static on an old analogue TV tuned between channels came from the cosmic microwave background.

# Redshift
Light from receding objects has its wavelengths stretched towards the red end of the spectrum. For distant galaxies, the stretching is mostly due to space expanding while the light travels: cosmological redshift.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Light emitted", "Travels for billions of years", "Space stretches meanwhile", "Wavelength stretched: redshift"]}
### Measuring it
Astronomers find known spectral lines, like hydrogen's, and see how much they've shifted. The redshift "z" says how much the universe has expanded since the light left: z = 1 means wavelengths doubled.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "z", "b": "0.1 = ~1.3 billion years ago"}, {"a": "z", "b": "1 = ~7.9 billion years ago"}, {"a": "z", "b": "6 = ~12.8 billion years ago"}, {"a": "z", "b": "14 = ~13.5 billion years ago"}]}
### Doppler on Earth
The same principle, for sound, makes an ambulance siren drop in pitch as it passes. Police radar guns use a Doppler shift to measure car speeds.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Approaching", "items": ["waves squashed", "higher pitch", "blueshift"]}, "r": {"t": "Receding", "items": ["waves stretched", "lower pitch", "redshift"]}}
!? The record-holding galaxies seen by Webb have redshifts above 14: their light has been stretched more than 15 times.

# The observable universe
We can only see objects whose light has had time to reach us since the universe became transparent. Because space kept expanding while the light travelled, the most distant objects we see are now about 46 billion light-years away, not 13.8.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "46 billion ly", "l": "radius today"}, {"v": "93 billion ly", "l": "diameter"}, {"v": "~2 trillion", "l": "galaxies, perhaps"}]}
### Observable vs entire
The whole universe is likely much larger than the observable part, possibly infinite. What lies beyond our horizon is, in principle, unobservable.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Observable universe", "items": ["what light could reach us", "~93 billion ly wide"]}, "r": {"t": "Entire universe", "items": ["possibly much larger", "possibly infinite"]}}
### A shrinking view
Because expansion is accelerating, distant galaxies are slipping beyond our reach. In the far future, observers in the Milky Way (merged with Andromeda) would see only their own merged galaxy, with the rest of the universe invisible.
!! The universe is 13.8 billion light-years across => The observable part is about 93 billion light-years across, thanks to expansion
> Being "13.8 billion years old" and "46 billion light-years in radius" aren't contradictory: space stretched while the light travelled.

# Who named it?
In 1927 Georges Lemaître, a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist, proposed that the universe was expanding. In 1931 he suggested it began from a "primeval atom". The idea was controversial for decades.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1927", "l": "Lemaître's expanding universe"}, {"y": "1931", "l": "\"primeval atom\""}, {"y": "1948", "l": "Gamow and Alpher's hot Big Bang"}, {"y": "1949", "l": "Hoyle names it"}, {"y": "1965", "l": "CMB settles the debate"}]}
### Steady state
Fred Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold proposed the steady-state theory: the universe expands, but new matter appears continuously so it always looks the same, with no beginning.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Big Bang", "items": ["a beginning", "evolving universe", "predicts CMB"]}, "r": {"t": "Steady state", "items": ["no beginning", "unchanging", "no CMB expected"]}}
### The name
On BBC radio in 1949, Hoyle called the rival theory "this big bang idea". He insisted he didn't mean it as an insult, but the catchy name stuck. The 1965 discovery of the CMB decisively favoured the Big Bang.
### Science and faith
Pope Pius XII welcomed the Big Bang as support for creation; Lemaître himself asked him not to mix science and theology so directly, keeping the two separate.
!? Hoyle, though wrong about steady state, made major contributions, including explaining how stars make carbon.

# How will it end?
The universe's future depends on dark energy. If it stays constant, as current data mostly suggest, expansion accelerates forever.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Possible fates", "items": ["Heat death: endless cold expansion", "Big Rip: dark energy tears everything apart", "Big Crunch: expansion reverses", "Big Bounce: crunch leads to a new bang"]}
### The heat death timeline
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1 trillion years", "l": "distant galaxies vanish from view"}, {"y": "100 trillion years", "l": "last stars form and fade"}, {"y": "10⁴⁰ years", "l": "protons may decay"}, {"y": "10¹⁰⁰ years", "l": "last black holes evaporate"}]}
### Why "heat death"?
The universe would reach maximum entropy: everything at the same cold temperature, so no energy flows and nothing can happen. Not hot, but uniform.
### New uncertainty
In 2024–25 the DESI galaxy survey reported hints that dark energy may be weakening. If so, the long-term future could change, possibly even towards a slowdown. More data is needed.
> These timescales are so vast that the entire history of the universe so far is a tiny flicker at the very start.

== sp-blackholes

# The event horizon
The event horizon is a boundary in spacetime, not a physical surface. Outside it, a rocket with enough thrust can escape. Inside it, every possible path, even for light, leads inward.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Far away: normal gravity", "Photon sphere: light can orbit", "Event horizon: no return", "Singularity: physics breaks down"]}
### Its size
The radius of the event horizon, the Schwarzschild radius, is proportional to mass: about 3 km per solar mass.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "r = 2GM / c²", "items": [{"s": "r", "m": "Schwarzschild radius"}, {"s": "G", "m": "gravitational constant"}, {"s": "M", "m": "mass"}, {"s": "c", "m": "speed of light"}]}
### What you'd see
• Approaching, the sky would distort as light bends around the black hole
• Behind you, the universe's light would crowd into a shrinking circle
• Crossing a large black hole's horizon, you'd notice nothing special at that moment
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sun-mass black hole", "b": "horizon ~6 km wide"}, {"a": "Sgr A*", "b": "~24 million km wide"}, {"a": "M87*", "b": "~38 billion km wide, bigger than the Solar System's planets' orbits"}]}
!! The event horizon is a solid surface => It's an invisible boundary; nothing special happens to a falling observer at that point
> Once inside, the singularity is not a place in front of you but a moment in your future.

# How they form
Stellar black holes form when the core of a massive star collapses. Above about 2–3 solar masses, no known force can stop a dead core collapsing: not even the pressure of tightly packed neutrons.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Massive star", "Iron core", "Collapse", "Neutron pressure fails", "Black hole"]}
### Other routes
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Black hole origins", "items": ["Collapsing massive stars", "Neutron star mergers", "Direct collapse of gas clouds, early universe", "Primordial: maybe from the Big Bang"]}
### Failed supernovae
Some massive stars may collapse straight into black holes without a bright explosion, simply vanishing. Astronomers have watched a few candidate stars disappear.
### Growing
Black holes grow by swallowing gas, stars and other black holes. Mergers detected by gravitational waves often produce black holes of 30–150 solar masses.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mass, Suns", "items": [{"l": "Lightest known", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Typical stellar", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Merger products", "v": 60.0}, {"l": "GW231123 merger", "v": 225.0, "hi": true}]}
!? The first confirmed black hole, Cygnus X-1, was identified in the 1970s from X-rays emitted by gas falling in from a companion star.

# Size
A black hole's event horizon is surprisingly small for its mass. Squeeze any mass into a small enough sphere and it becomes a black hole.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Earth as a black hole", "b": "1.8 cm wide, a marble"}, {"a": "Sun as a black hole", "b": "6 km wide"}, {"a": "Sgr A*", "b": "~24 million km wide"}, {"a": "TON 618", "b": "wider than the Solar System"}]}
### Density isn't extreme for giants
The average density inside the horizon falls as mass grows. A supermassive black hole of a billion Suns has an average "density" inside its horizon lower than water.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Small black hole", "items": ["tiny", "enormous average density"]}, "r": {"t": "Supermassive", "items": ["huge", "average density can be below water's"]}}
### Mass, spin, charge
Physicists say "black holes have no hair": a black hole is fully described by just three numbers: mass, spin and electric charge. All other details of what fell in are hidden.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Mass", "Spin", "Electric charge"]}
!? Spinning black holes drag spacetime around with them, so close in, nothing can stay still relative to distant stars.

# Supermassive black holes
Nearly every large galaxy hosts a central black hole of millions to billions of solar masses. Their masses correlate with their galaxies' bulges, suggesting they grow together.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mass, million Suns", "items": [{"l": "Sgr A*, Milky Way", "v": 4.3}, {"l": "Andromeda's", "v": 140.0}, {"l": "M87", "v": 6500.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "TON 618", "v": 40000.0, "hi": true}]}
### Quasars
When a supermassive black hole swallows gas rapidly, the surrounding disc heats up and outshines its entire galaxy, sometimes thousands of times over. These are quasars, the brightest steady objects in the universe.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gas falls inward", "Forms a hot disc", "Friction heats it", "Brighter than a galaxy"]}
### Jets
Magnetic fields near spinning black holes launch jets of particles at nearly light speed, extending hundreds of thousands of light-years. M87's jet is visible in ordinary telescopes.
### A puzzle
Webb has found supermassive black holes already existing less than a billion years after the Big Bang. How they grew so big so fast is an active question.
> Our own Sgr A* is quiet, eating very little, which is lucky for life in the galaxy.

# Spaghettification
Gravity's pull weakens with distance, so near a black hole the pull on your feet can be much stronger than on your head. This difference, a tidal force, stretches you lengthwise and squeezes you sideways.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Feet pulled harder", "Head pulled less", "Stretched lengthways", "Squeezed sideways"]}
### Size matters, oddly
Tidal forces at the horizon are strongest for small black holes. Near a stellar black hole, you'd be torn apart long before reaching the horizon. A supermassive black hole's horizon is so big that you could cross it intact and only be spaghettified later.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Stellar black hole", "items": ["torn apart outside the horizon"]}, "r": {"t": "Supermassive", "items": ["cross the horizon intact"]}}
### Tidal disruption events
When a star wanders too close to a supermassive black hole, it's shredded. About half its gas falls in and flares brightly for months. Telescopes catch dozens of these events.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Star approaches", "Torn into a stream", "Gas spirals in", "Bright flare for months"]}
!? Earth's tides are the same effect, much gentler: the Moon pulls the near side of the oceans more than the far side.

# Time slows down
General relativity says time passes more slowly in stronger gravity. Near a black hole, the effect becomes extreme. A distant observer watching someone fall in sees their clock slow down and their image redden and fade as they approach the horizon, never quite seeming to cross.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Distant observer", "items": ["sees the faller freeze and fade"]}, "r": {"t": "Falling astronaut", "items": ["feels time normally", "crosses in finite time"]}}
### Interstellar's water planet
In the film *Interstellar*, physicist Kip Thorne helped calculate a planet near a giant spinning black hole where 1 hour equals 7 years on Earth. It's an extreme but physically inspired scenario.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "time passing elsewhere per hour on the planet", "items": [{"l": "Earth, years", "v": 7.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Planet, hours", "v": 1.0}]}
### Real measurements
Near Sgr A*, astronomers tracked the star S2 passing close in 2018 and measured its light being stretched by gravity, exactly as relativity predicts.
!? Even Earth's gravity slows time measurably: GPS clocks would drift without correcting for it.

# Seeing the invisible
Black holes emit no light, but their surroundings do. Astronomers find them through the effects of their gravity on matter and light around them.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Ways to detect", "items": ["Stars orbiting nothing visible", "X-rays from hot gas", "Jets and radio emission", "Gravitational waves", "Lensing of background light"]}
### Stars around Sgr A*
For decades, teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez tracked stars orbiting an invisible point at the Milky Way's centre. The star S2 orbits every 16 years, at up to 2.5% of light speed. Their work won the 2020 Nobel Prize.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "16 years", "l": "S2's orbit"}, {"v": "7,650 km/s", "l": "S2's top speed"}, {"v": "2020", "l": "Nobel Prize"}]}
### X-ray binaries
When a black hole orbits a normal star, it can pull gas off it. The gas heats to millions of degrees as it spirals in, glowing in X-rays.
### Lone black holes
In 2022 astronomers detected an isolated black hole wandering through the galaxy by the way it briefly magnified a background star: microlensing.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Black hole passes in front of a star", "Bends its light", "Star brightens and shifts", "Mass measured"]}
> The Milky Way may contain around 100 million stellar black holes, most of them invisible.

# Gravitational waves
Accelerating masses create ripples in spacetime that travel at light speed, stretching and squeezing space as they pass. Einstein predicted them in 1916 but doubted they'd ever be detected: the effect is incredibly small.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Two black holes spiral together", "Spacetime ripples outward", "Reach Earth", "Stretch detectors by a fraction of a proton"]}
### LIGO
Each LIGO detector has two 4 km arms at right angles. Lasers measure the arm lengths; a passing wave changes them by less than 1/10,000 of a proton's width. On 14 September 2015 both LIGO sites detected GW150914: two black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses merging 1.3 billion light-years away.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "4 km", "l": "arm length"}, {"v": "10⁻¹⁸ m", "l": "change measured"}, {"v": "3", "l": "solar masses turned into wave energy"}]}
### A new kind of astronomy
Since then, hundreds of mergers have been detected by LIGO, Virgo in Italy and KAGRA in Japan. In 2017 a neutron star merger was seen both in gravitational waves and in light, confirming that such collisions make gold.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1916", "l": "predicted"}, {"y": "1974", "l": "indirect evidence from a binary pulsar"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "first direct detection"}, {"y": "2017", "l": "Nobel Prize and neutron star merger"}]}
### Turned into sound
The signal's frequency rises as the black holes spiral in, producing a "chirp" when converted to audio.
!? The 2015 merger briefly released more power in gravitational waves than all the stars in the observable universe combined emitted as light.

# The first image
The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) linked radio observatories in Hawaii, Arizona, Mexico, Chile, Spain, the South Pole and elsewhere. Observing together at a 1.3 mm wavelength, they formed a virtual dish the size of Earth, sharp enough to read a newspaper in New York from Paris.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Dishes observe simultaneously", "Atomic clocks time-stamp data", "Hard drives flown to supercomputers", "Data combined into an image"]}
### M87* in 2019
Released on 10 April 2019, the image showed a bright ring of hot gas around a dark shadow: the black hole at the centre of galaxy M87. The ring's size matched Einstein's predictions for a 6.5-billion-Sun black hole.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bright ring", "b": "hot gas bent by gravity"}, {"a": "Dark centre", "b": "the black hole's shadow"}, {"a": "Brighter side", "b": "gas moving towards us"}]}
### Sgr A* in 2022
Our own black hole, though much closer, was harder to image because gas around it changes in minutes. The 2022 image showed a similar ring, confirming the physics scales from millions to billions of Suns.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "M87*", "items": ["6.5 billion Suns", "55 million ly", "changes over days"]}, "r": {"t": "Sgr A*", "items": ["4 million Suns", "26,000 ly", "changes in minutes"]}}
> The data was too large to send over the internet: about 5 petabytes, so hard drives were physically shipped.

# Hawking radiation
In 1974 Stephen Hawking combined quantum theory with general relativity and found that black holes aren't completely black: they should emit faint radiation and slowly lose mass. The smaller the black hole, the hotter it is and the faster it evaporates.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Quantum effects near the horizon", "Faint radiation escapes", "Black hole loses mass", "Evaporates over immense time"]}
### How faint?
A black hole the mass of the Sun would have a temperature of about 60 billionths of a degree above absolute zero, far colder than the cosmic microwave background, so for now it absorbs more than it emits.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sun-mass black hole", "b": "~10⁶⁷ years to evaporate"}, {"a": "Sgr A*", "b": "~10⁸⁷ years"}, {"a": "Age of the universe so far", "b": "~10¹⁰ years"}]}
### The information paradox
If a black hole evaporates into random radiation, what happens to the information about what fell in? Quantum theory says information can't be destroyed. This puzzle remains one of the deepest in physics.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Relativity view", "items": ["information lost inside"]}, "r": {"t": "Quantum view", "items": ["information must be preserved"]}}
### Never observed
Hawking radiation from real black holes is far too faint to detect. Physicists have seen analogous effects in lab systems with sound waves in fluids, called "dumb holes".
!? Hawking's gravestone in Westminster Abbey is engraved with his formula for black hole temperature.

== sp-telescopes

# Looking back in time
Light travels at 300,000 km/s: fast, but not infinitely fast. Everything we see is a message from the past, delayed by the distance it travelled. The farther we look, the older the light.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Moon", "b": "1.3 seconds ago"}, {"a": "Sun", "b": "8 minutes ago"}, {"a": "Polaris", "b": "~430 years ago"}, {"a": "Andromeda", "b": "2.5 million years ago"}]}
### A time machine for astronomers
Because distant light is old, telescopes can watch the universe's history directly. Webb sees galaxies as they were less than 400 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was about 3% of its current age.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Nearby galaxies: recent past", "Distant galaxies: billions of years ago", "Cosmic microwave background: 380,000 years after the Big Bang"]}
### Is that star still there?
For most stars you see, the light left them decades to centuries ago. Stars live millions to billions of years, so almost all are still there; but for a few distant massive stars, we might be seeing a star that has already exploded.
!! Many stars we see are already dead => Most naked-eye stars are within a few hundred light-years and still shining
> Looking out is looking back: the deepest images are also the oldest photographs ever taken.

# Galileo's telescope
Galileo didn't invent the telescope; Dutch spectacle makers did in 1608. Hearing about it, he built his own, improving it to about 20× magnification, and was the first to point one systematically at the sky.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1608", "l": "Dutch telescope patent request"}, {"y": "1609", "l": "Galileo builds his own"}, {"y": "1610", "l": "Sidereus Nuncius published"}, {"y": "1633", "l": "Galileo's trial"}]}
### What he saw
• **Mountains and craters on the Moon**: it wasn't a perfect heavenly sphere
• **Four moons of Jupiter**: proof that not everything orbits Earth
• **Phases of Venus**: only possible if Venus orbits the Sun
• **Countless stars in the Milky Way**: the universe was bigger than anyone thought
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old view", "items": ["perfect heavens", "everything orbits Earth"]}, "r": {"t": "Galileo's evidence", "items": ["flawed Moon", "moons orbit Jupiter", "Venus orbits the Sun"]}}
### Why it was explosive
The phases of Venus in particular contradicted Ptolemy's Earth-centred model. Galileo's support for Copernicus led to his trial by the Inquisition in 1633 and house arrest for the rest of his life.
!? Galileo's best telescope had a field of view so narrow he could see only a quarter of the full Moon at once.

# Refractors and reflectors
A refractor bends light with a big lens at the front; a reflector collects light with a curved mirror at the back. Newton built the first practical reflector in 1668 to avoid the coloured fringes that early lenses produced.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Refractor", "items": ["lens", "sealed tube", "colour fringes", "heavy at large sizes"]}, "r": {"t": "Reflector", "items": ["mirror", "no colour fringes", "can be huge and segmented"]}}
### Why lenses hit a limit
A lens can only be supported at its edge, so big ones sag under their own weight, and light must pass through flawless glass. The largest refractor ever, at Yerkes Observatory (1897), has a 1-metre lens. Mirrors can be supported from behind, segmented and made enormous.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "aperture, m", "items": [{"l": "Yerkes lens", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Hubble", "v": 2.4}, {"l": "GTC, La Palma", "v": 10.4}, {"l": "ELT", "v": 39.0, "hi": true}]}
### Modern tricks
• **Segmented mirrors**: many hexagons acting as one, as on Keck and Webb
• **Adaptive optics**: deformable mirrors that cancel atmospheric blur hundreds of times a second
• **Interferometry**: combining several telescopes to sharpen detail
> For backyard astronomy, a simple reflector (a "Dobsonian") gives the most light-gathering power per euro.

# Bigger is better
A telescope's light-gathering power grows with the area of its mirror, which goes with the diameter squared. Double the diameter and you collect four times as much light, seeing fainter, more distant objects.

### Sharper too
Bigger mirrors also resolve finer detail, though on the ground the atmosphere blurs the view unless adaptive optics correct it.

### The Extremely Large Telescope
ESO's ELT in Chile's Atacama Desert will have a 39 m mirror made of 798 hexagonal segments, collecting more light than all today's large research telescopes combined. Portugal is an ESO member and contributes to its instruments.

# The electromagnetic spectrum
Light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields. Visible light, wavelengths of about 400–700 nanometres, is a sliver of a much larger spectrum, from radio waves metres long to gamma rays smaller than an atom.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Radio: metres", "Microwave: cm", "Infrared: µm", "Visible: 0.4–0.7 µm", "Ultraviolet", "X-ray", "Gamma: tinier than atoms"]}
### What each reveals
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Radio", "b": "cold gas, pulsars, black hole jets"}, {"a": "Infrared", "b": "dust-hidden stars, early galaxies"}, {"a": "Visible", "b": "stars and galaxies"}, {"a": "X-ray", "b": "million-degree gas, black hole surroundings"}, {"a": "Gamma", "b": "explosions, neutron star collisions"}]}
### Energy and wavelength
Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon. That's why UV burns skin and X-rays pass through it, while radio waves pass harmlessly through your body all day.
### Multi-wavelength astronomy
The same object looks completely different across the spectrum. The Crab Nebula glows in radio, infrared, visible, X-ray and gamma rays, each revealing a different process.
> Your eyes evolved for visible light because that's where the Sun is brightest and where Earth's atmosphere is transparent.

# Infrared
Infrared is light with wavelengths a bit longer than red, felt as heat. Everything warm glows in infrared, including you. Infrared also slips through dust clouds that block visible light, revealing stars being born inside them.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Visible light", "items": ["blocked by dust", "shows hot stars"]}, "r": {"t": "Infrared", "items": ["passes through dust", "shows cool objects and newborn stars"]}}
### Redshift pushes light into infrared
As the universe expands, light from distant galaxies is stretched. Ultraviolet and visible light emitted 13 billion years ago arrives today as infrared. To see the first galaxies, you need an infrared telescope.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Young galaxy emits UV and visible light", "13 billion years of expansion", "Wavelengths stretched", "Arrives as infrared"]}
### Why Webb is so cold
An infrared telescope must be colder than what it observes, or its own heat drowns the signal. Webb sits 1.5 million km away, shielded by a five-layer sunshield, at about −233 °C; one instrument is cooled further to −266 °C.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "85 °C", "l": "sun-facing shield side"}, {"v": "−233 °C", "l": "Webb's mirror"}, {"v": "−266 °C", "l": "MIRI instrument"}]}
!? Herschel discovered infrared in 1800 by putting thermometers beyond the red end of a rainbow from a prism and finding it was warmest there.

# Why go to space?
Earth's atmosphere does three annoying things to astronomers: it shimmers (making stars twinkle and images blurry), it glows faintly, and it blocks whole parts of the spectrum.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Atmosphere problems", "items": ["Turbulence: blurring", "Absorption: blocks X-rays, UV, much infrared", "Glow: sky background"]}
### What gets through
Only visible light, some infrared and radio reach the ground well. Ozone blocks UV; water vapour blocks much infrared; X-rays and gamma rays are absorbed high up. That's lucky for life, but bad for astronomy.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% reaching the ground (rough)", "items": [{"l": "Radio", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Visible", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Infrared", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Ultraviolet", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "X-rays", "v": 0.0, "hi": true}]}
### Ground strikes back
Space telescopes are expensive and hard to repair. Ground telescopes can be much bigger and upgraded easily, and adaptive optics now remove much of the blur. The best sites are high and dry: Chile's Atacama, Hawaii's Mauna Kea, La Palma in the Canaries.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Space", "items": ["no blur", "all wavelengths", "costly", "hard to fix"]}, "r": {"t": "Ground", "items": ["huge mirrors", "upgradable", "limited wavelengths"]}}
> Modern astronomy needs both: space telescopes for sharpness and invisible wavelengths, ground giants for light-gathering power.

# Radio telescopes
Radio waves from space are incredibly faint: all the radio energy ever collected by radio telescopes is less than the energy of a falling snowflake. Huge dishes are needed to gather enough.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Faint radio waves", "Large dish focuses them", "Receiver amplifies", "Computer builds an image"]}
### History
Karl Jansky discovered radio waves from the Milky Way's centre in 1932 while studying static for Bell Labs. Radio astronomy then found quasars, pulsars and the cosmic microwave background.

### Arrays
By combining dishes far apart, astronomers get the sharpness of one telescope as wide as the distance between them. The Event Horizon Telescope linked dishes across the world into an Earth-sized virtual telescope.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "ALMA", "b": "66 dishes in Chile"}, {"a": "Very Large Array", "b": "27 dishes in New Mexico"}, {"a": "SKA", "b": "thousands of antennas, South Africa and Australia"}, {"a": "EHT", "b": "Earth-sized array"}]}
### Protecting radio quiet
Phones, Wi-Fi and satellites cause interference, so radio telescopes are built in remote "radio quiet zones".

# Spectroscopy
Pass starlight through a prism or grating and it spreads into a rainbow, a spectrum. Atoms of each element absorb light at exact, unique wavelengths, leaving dark lines like a barcode. Matching barcodes reveals what a star is made of.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Starlight", "Prism or grating", "Rainbow spectrum", "Dark lines = element fingerprints"]}
### Helium was found in the Sun first
In 1868, astronomers saw an unknown yellow line in the Sun's spectrum during an eclipse. They named the element helium, after Helios, the Sun. It wasn't found on Earth until 1895.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1814", "l": "Fraunhofer maps solar lines"}, {"y": "1859", "l": "Kirchhoff and Bunsen link lines to elements"}, {"y": "1868", "l": "helium found in the Sun"}, {"y": "1895", "l": "helium found on Earth"}]}
### Much more than ingredients

### Philosopher's challenge
In 1835 philosopher Auguste Comte said we would never know the chemical composition of stars. Within 25 years, spectroscopy proved him wrong.

== sp-gravity

# Newton's gravity
In 1687 Newton published his law of universal gravitation: every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to both masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The same law explains falling apples, tides and planetary orbits.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "F = G·m₁·m₂ / r²", "items": [{"s": "F", "m": "force"}, {"s": "G", "m": "6.67 × 10⁻¹¹"}, {"s": "m₁, m₂", "m": "masses"}, {"s": "r", "m": "distance between centres"}]}
### Gravity is weak
G is tiny, so gravity between everyday objects is minuscule. Two people standing 1 m apart attract with a force less than a millionth of a newton. Gravity only dominates because planets and stars are enormous and it never cancels out, unlike electric charges.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gravity", "items": ["always attracts", "very weak", "dominates at large scales"]}, "r": {"t": "Electric force", "items": ["attracts and repels", "very strong", "cancels out in bulk matter"]}}
### Weighing the Earth
In 1798 Henry Cavendish measured G with a delicate torsion balance in his shed, which let scientists calculate Earth's mass: about 6 × 10²⁴ kg.
### Where Newton falls short
Newton's law is superb for spacecraft navigation, but it couldn't explain a small drift in Mercury's orbit. Einstein's general relativity (1915) did.
!? The apple story is probably partly true: Newton told friends that a falling apple in his family garden set him thinking about gravity.

# The inverse square law
Gravity spreads out in all directions, like light from a bulb. At twice the distance, the same influence covers four times the area, so it's four times weaker. Many things follow this rule: light, sound and electric force.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "strength ∝ 1 / distance²", "items": [{"s": "2× distance", "m": "¼ strength"}, {"s": "3×", "m": "⅑"}, {"s": "10×", "m": "1/100"}]}
### Gravity with height
Because Earth's centre is 6,371 km below your feet, going up a few hundred kilometres changes gravity only a little. At the ISS (400 km), it's still about 89% as strong.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "gravity, % of surface", "items": [{"l": "Surface", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "ISS, 400 km", "v": 89.0}, {"l": "GPS orbit, 20,200 km", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Moon's distance", "v": 0.03, "hi": true}]}
### Light works the same way
A star twice as far away appears four times dimmer. Astronomers use this to estimate distances to stars whose true brightness they know.
> Inverse-square laws appear whenever something spreads evenly from a point into three-dimensional space.

# Orbits are falling
In his *Principia*, Newton drew a cannon on a very tall mountain. Each faster shot lands farther away; at the right speed, the ball falls towards Earth but the curved surface drops away just as fast, and it circles forever.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Slow shot: lands nearby", "Faster: lands farther", "Orbital speed: falls around Earth", "Faster still: elliptical orbit or escape"]}
### Speed depends on height
Lower orbits need higher speeds and are quicker. The ISS at 400 km circles in 92 minutes; the Moon, 384,000 km away, takes 27 days.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "orbital period", "items": [{"l": "ISS, minutes", "v": 92.0}, {"l": "GPS, hours", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Geostationary, hours", "v": 24.0}, {"l": "Moon, days", "v": 27.0, "hi": true}]}
### Orbital oddities
• To catch up with something ahead in orbit, you slow down, drop to a lower, faster orbit, then rise again
• Speeding up raises your orbit, which makes you slower overall
• A dropped spanner keeps orbiting alongside the astronaut who dropped it
> In orbit you're not floating above gravity; you're falling continually and always missing the ground.

# Weightlessness
Weight is the feeling of the floor pushing up on you. In a freely falling lift, the floor would fall with you and you'd float. Astronauts in orbit are in permanent free fall along with their station, so nothing pushes on anything: weightlessness.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gravity pulls everything equally", "Station and crew fall together", "Nothing presses on anything", "Weightless"]}
!! There's no gravity in space => At the ISS, gravity is about 89% as strong as on the ground
### Feeling it on Earth
• The stomach "drop" at the top of a roller-coaster hill is a moment of free fall
• Parabolic flights (the "vomit comet") give about 20–25 seconds of weightlessness per arc
• Drop towers give a few seconds for experiments
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Climb steeply", "Over the top: ~22 s weightless", "Dive and recover", "Repeat up to 30 times"]}
### Microgravity
Scientists say "microgravity" because tiny forces remain: air drag, crew moving, the station's rotation. Flames burn as round blue spheres, and water forms floating balls.
!? Astronauts feel briefly heavy after landing; some have dropped cups, forgetting objects now fall.

# Mass and weight
Mass is how much matter you contain, measured in kilograms; it's the same anywhere in the universe. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass, measured in newtons. Bathroom scales show "kilograms", but they really measure force and assume Earth's gravity.

### Same you, different worlds
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "weight of a 70 kg person, newtons", "items": [{"l": "Moon", "v": 113.0}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 260.0}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 687.0}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 1730.0, "hi": true}]}
### Mass still matters in space
Even weightless, mass resists acceleration: pushing a heavy object in orbit is hard to start and hard to stop. Astronauts measure their body mass with a spring chair that oscillates; heavier people bounce more slowly.

# Kepler's laws
Using Tycho Brahe's precise observations of Mars, Johannes Kepler found three laws of planetary motion between 1609 and 1619, decades before Newton explained why they work.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["First law: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus", "Second law: a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times", "Third law: period² ∝ distance³"]}
### The second law in action
A planet speeds up when close to the Sun and slows when far. Earth moves fastest in early January, at perihelion, about 30.3 km/s, and slowest in early July, about 29.3 km/s. That's partly why northern winter is a few days shorter than summer.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Near the Sun, perihelion", "items": ["faster", "early January"]}, "r": {"t": "Far from the Sun, aphelion", "items": ["slower", "early July"]}}
### The third law
The square of a planet's year is proportional to the cube of its distance. In units of Earth years and AU, period² = distance³ exactly.
ex: Jupiter: 5.2 AU | 5.2³ ≈ 140.6 | √140.6 ≈ 11.9 years
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "orbital period, years", "items": [{"l": "Mars", "v": 1.9}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 11.9}, {"l": "Saturn", "v": 29.5}, {"l": "Neptune", "v": 165.0, "hi": true}]}
> Kepler's third law is used today to weigh stars and black holes from the orbits around them.

# Escape velocity
Escape velocity is the speed needed to coast away from a body forever without further thrust, ignoring air resistance. It depends on the body's mass and radius, not on the mass of the object escaping.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = √(2GM / r)", "items": [{"s": "G", "m": "gravitational constant"}, {"s": "M", "m": "planet's mass"}, {"s": "r", "m": "distance from its centre"}]}
### Around the Solar System
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "km/s", "items": [{"l": "Moon", "v": 2.4}, {"l": "Mars", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Earth", "v": 11.2}, {"l": "Jupiter", "v": 59.5}, {"l": "Sun's surface", "v": 618.0, "hi": true}]}
### Rockets don't need it all at once
Escape velocity applies to a cannonball fired once. Rockets keep firing their engines as they climb, so they can leave gradually. Still, to leave Earth for good, a spacecraft must eventually gain that much energy.
### Escaping the Sun
From Earth's orbit, escaping the Sun requires about 42 km/s, but Earth already moves at 30 km/s, so probes need "only" about 12 km/s extra in the right direction.

!? A black hole is an object whose escape velocity exceeds the speed of light.

# Gravity assists
A spacecraft flying close behind a moving planet gets pulled along and leaves faster, relative to the Sun. The planet loses an immeasurably tiny bit of orbital speed in exchange. Flying in front of a planet does the opposite and slows the craft down.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Approach a moving planet", "Swing around it", "Exit with planet's motion added", "Faster relative to the Sun"]}
### The train analogy
Throw a tennis ball at an approaching train: it bounces back faster than you threw it, having picked up the train's speed. The planet is the train; gravity is the bounce.
### Famous slingshots
• Voyager 2 used Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus to reach Neptune in 12 years instead of 30
• Parker Solar Probe used Venus seven times to slow down and fall towards the Sun
• Rosetta used Earth three times and Mars once to catch a comet
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Voyager 2", "b": "Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus"}, {"a": "Cassini", "b": "Venus twice, Earth, Jupiter"}, {"a": "Parker", "b": "Venus seven times"}, {"a": "JUICE", "b": "Earth, Moon, Venus"}]}
### Discovered by a student
In 1961 UCLA student Michael Minovitch used computers to calculate gravity-assist trajectories, showing they could make outer-planet missions practical.
> Gravity assists are free fuel: they let small spacecraft reach places their rockets alone never could.

# Lagrange points
Around any two orbiting bodies, like the Sun and Earth, there are five points where gravity and orbital motion balance, so a small spacecraft can stay in the same position relative to both. Joseph-Louis Lagrange worked them out in 1772.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "L1", "b": "1.5 million km sunward, SOHO watches the Sun"}, {"a": "L2", "b": "1.5 million km outward, Webb and Gaia"}, {"a": "L3", "b": "behind the Sun, unused"}, {"a": "L4 and L5", "b": "60° ahead and behind, stable"}]}
### Stable and unstable
L1, L2 and L3 are like balancing on a hilltop: spacecraft need small engine burns every few weeks to stay. L4 and L5 are like valleys: objects naturally collect there. Jupiter has thousands of "Trojan" asteroids at its L4 and L5 points.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "L1, L2, L3", "items": ["unstable", "need station-keeping"]}, "r": {"t": "L4, L5", "items": ["stable", "collect asteroids"]}}
### Why L2 for Webb
At L2, the Sun, Earth and Moon are all on one side, so a single sunshield blocks them all, keeping the telescope cold. And it stays in constant contact with Earth.
!? NASA's Lucy mission, launched in 2021, is touring Jupiter's Trojan asteroids, named after heroes of the Trojan War.

# Geostationary orbit
At 35,786 km above the equator, an orbit takes exactly one sidereal day. A satellite there moves as fast as Earth turns beneath it, appearing fixed in the sky. Science-fiction author Arthur C. Clarke popularised the idea for communications in 1945.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Altitude 35,786 km", "Orbit period = one day", "Moves with Earth's spin", "Hangs over one spot"]}
### Uses
• TV broadcasting: dishes can be fixed in one direction
• Weather satellites watching the same half of Earth continuously, like Meteosat for Europe
• Some communications and military satellites
### Downsides
The great distance means a radio delay of about 0.25 s for a round trip: noticeable on phone calls and online games. And geostationary satellites can't see the polar regions well.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Geostationary", "items": ["fixed in sky", "wide coverage", "~0.25 s delay"]}, "r": {"t": "Low orbit", "items": ["moves fast", "needs many satellites", "low delay"]}}
### A crowded ring
There's only one geostationary ring, so slots are allocated by the International Telecommunication Union. Old satellites are boosted a few hundred km higher into a "graveyard orbit".
> In Portugal, satellite TV dishes point roughly south, about 40° up, towards satellites above the equator.

== sp-relativity

# The speed of light
In 1887 Michelson and Morley expected the speed of light to vary with Earth's motion through space; it didn't. In 1905, Einstein took this as a principle: light in a vacuum always moves at the same speed, c, for every observer, however fast they're moving.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "299,792,458 m/s", "l": "exact speed of light"}, {"v": "1887", "l": "Michelson–Morley"}, {"v": "1905", "l": "special relativity"}]}
### Why that's strange
If you throw a ball forwards from a moving train, its speed adds to the train's. But shine a torch forwards from a rocket at half light speed, and both you and a watcher on the ground measure the light at exactly c. For that to work, time and space themselves must adjust.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ball from a train", "items": ["speeds add"]}, "r": {"t": "Light from a rocket", "items": ["always c for everyone"]}}
### Defining the metre
Since 1983 the speed of light has been fixed by definition, and the metre is defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second.
• Light takes 1.3 s to reach the Moon and about 0.13 s to circle Earth
• In glass or water, light slows, but c in a vacuum is the universal limit
> c isn't really about light: it's the speed limit of cause and effect in the universe.

# Time dilation
If light's speed is the same for everyone, moving clocks must tick slowly from the point of view of someone watching them pass. The effect is tiny at everyday speeds and huge near light speed.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "t' = t / √(1 − v²/c²)", "items": [{"s": "t", "m": "time for the traveller"}, {"s": "t'", "m": "time for the observer"}, {"s": "v", "m": "speed"}, {"s": "c", "m": "light speed"}]}
### How big?
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "time dilation factor", "items": [{"l": "10% of c", "v": 1.005}, {"l": "50% of c", "v": 1.15}, {"l": "90% of c", "v": 2.3}, {"l": "99% of c", "v": 7.1, "hi": true}]}
### Evidence
• **Muons**: particles made high in the atmosphere live only 2.2 microseconds, too short to reach the ground, yet many do, because their clocks run slow at 99.9% of c
• **Atomic clocks on planes** (Hafele–Keating, 1971) showed the predicted tiny differences
• **Particle accelerators** see fast unstable particles live longer exactly as predicted
!! Time dilation is just a clock error => Every process slows, including ageing; time itself runs differently
> Astronauts on the ISS age about 0.01 seconds less than us over six months.

# The twin paradox
Twin A stays on Earth; twin B flies to a star 10 light-years away at 87% of c and returns. Earth clocks show about 23 years; B's ship clocks show about 11.5 years. B comes back younger.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "years aged", "items": [{"l": "Twin on Earth", "v": 23.0}, {"l": "Travelling twin", "v": 11.5, "hi": true}]}
### Why is it a "paradox"?
From B's view, Earth moved away and back, so shouldn't A be younger? The situations aren't symmetric: B accelerates, turns around and decelerates, switching reference frames. A stays in one frame the whole time. Calculations from either side agree: the traveller ages less.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth twin", "items": ["one inertial frame", "ages more"]}, "r": {"t": "Travelling twin", "items": ["changes frame at turnaround", "ages less"]}}
### A one-way ticket to the future
Time dilation makes travel to the future possible in principle: go fast enough and you return to a distant future. Travel to the past is a different matter, with no known way to do it.
!? Cosmonaut Gennady Padalka, after 879 days in orbit, is about 0.02 seconds younger than if he'd stayed on Earth.

# Length contraction
Objects moving fast are measured as shorter along their direction of motion by an observer they're passing. A spaceship flying at 87% of c would appear half as long as it is at rest.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "L = L₀ × √(1 − v²/c²)", "items": [{"s": "L₀", "m": "length at rest"}, {"s": "L", "m": "measured length"}, {"s": "v", "m": "speed"}]}
### Hand in hand with time dilation
The muon example shows both views: from the ground, the muon's clock runs slow; from the muon's view, the atmosphere is squashed thin, so it reaches the ground in time. Both descriptions agree on the result.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ground view", "items": ["muon's clock slow"]}, "r": {"t": "Muon's view", "items": ["atmosphere contracted"]}}
### What it doesn't do
• It doesn't squash objects physically; it's about measurements between frames
• The traveller notices nothing odd on their own ship
• At everyday speeds it's far too small to measure
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "measured length, %", "items": [{"l": "At rest", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "50% of c", "v": 87.0}, {"l": "90% of c", "v": 44.0}, {"l": "99% of c", "v": 14.0, "hi": true}]}
!? Very fast objects would actually look rotated rather than squashed in photos, an effect called Terrell rotation, because light from different parts takes different times to reach the camera.

# E = mc²
Published by Einstein in 1905 as a follow-up to special relativity, E = mc² says mass and energy are equivalent. Because c² is huge (9 × 10¹⁶), a tiny bit of mass is equivalent to a vast amount of energy.
ex: 1 gram of mass | × (3 × 10⁸ m/s)² | = 9 × 10¹³ joules ≈ 25 million kWh
### Where it shows up
• **The Sun**: converts about 4 million tonnes of mass to energy each second
• **Nuclear power**: splitting uranium releases energy as products weigh slightly less
• **Particle physics**: collisions turn energy into new particles of matter
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy from 1 kg, million kWh", "items": [{"l": "Coal burned", "v": 8e-06, "t": "0.000008"}, {"l": "Uranium fission", "v": 22.0}, {"l": "Complete mass conversion", "v": 25000.0, "hi": true}]}
### A common misunderstanding
E = mc² doesn't mean things easily turn into energy. In fission only about 0.1% of the mass is converted; in fusion about 0.7%. Total conversion happens only when matter meets antimatter.
### Even chemistry
When you burn wood, the products weigh very slightly less than the wood and oxygen did, but the difference is far too small to measure.
> A paperclip's worth of mass (1 g), fully converted, would supply the electricity of a Portuguese town of about 5,000 people for a year.

# Gravity bends spacetime
In 1915 Einstein's general relativity replaced Newton's force of gravity with geometry: mass and energy curve spacetime, and objects move along the straightest possible paths through that curved spacetime. "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve," as John Wheeler put it.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Mass and energy", "Curve spacetime", "Objects follow curved paths", "We see gravity"]}
### The trampoline picture
A heavy ball on a trampoline makes a dip; marbles roll towards it. It's a helpful image but imperfect: in real spacetime, time itself is curved too, which is what mostly makes things fall.
### Predictions confirmed
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1915", "l": "Mercury's orbit explained"}, {"y": "1919", "l": "light bending measured"}, {"y": "1959", "l": "gravity slows time, measured"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "gravitational waves detected"}, {"y": "2019", "l": "black hole imaged"}]}
### Gravity slows time
Clocks run slower in stronger gravity. A clock at sea level runs slightly slower than one at the top of a mountain. Modern atomic clocks detect the difference over just 1 cm of height.
!? Your head ages slightly faster than your feet, about 90 billionths of a second over a lifetime.

# Light bends
If gravity curves spacetime, light passing a massive object should bend. Einstein predicted that starlight grazing the Sun would shift by 1.75 arcseconds, twice what a Newtonian calculation gave.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Newton-style prediction", "items": ["0.87 arcseconds"]}, "r": {"t": "Einstein's prediction", "items": ["1.75 arcseconds"]}}
### The 1919 eclipse
Stars near the Sun are normally hidden by its glare, so Arthur Eddington organised expeditions to photograph them during the total eclipse of 29 May 1919: one to Sobral in Brazil and one to Príncipe, then a Portuguese colony off Africa. Their measurements favoured Einstein.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Total eclipse", "Photograph stars near the Sun", "Compare with night-time positions", "Shift matches Einstein"]}
### Overnight fame
On 7 November 1919, *The Times* headline read "Revolution in Science". Einstein became a global celebrity almost overnight.
• The Príncipe expedition is commemorated in São Tomé and Príncipe and in Portuguese science history
• Later radio measurements confirmed the bending to better than 0.01%
!? Eddington's team was nearly clouded out on Príncipe; the clouds cleared only partly during totality.

# GPS needs relativity
GPS satellites carry atomic clocks and orbit at 20,200 km, moving at about 14,000 km/h. Two relativistic effects change their clocks' rates compared with clocks on the ground.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Special relativity", "b": "speed slows the clocks by ~7 µs a day"}, {"a": "General relativity", "b": "weaker gravity speeds them by ~45 µs a day"}, {"a": "Net effect", "b": "~38 µs a day faster"}]}
### Why microseconds matter
GPS works by timing signals that travel at light speed. A timing error of 1 microsecond means a position error of about 300 m. Uncorrected, 38 µs a day would accumulate into errors of around 10 km a day.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "error ≈ c × time error", "items": [{"s": "c", "m": "300,000 km/s"}, {"s": "38 µs", "m": "≈ 11 km"}]}
### The fix
Engineers set satellite clocks to tick slightly slower before launch, at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so that in orbit they match ground time.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Predict relativity effects", "Adjust clock frequency before launch", "In orbit, clocks match Earth", "Accurate positions"]}
> Every time your phone finds your location, it's quietly confirming Einstein.

# Gravitational lensing
Massive objects bend light from things behind them, acting as cosmic lenses. A galaxy cluster can magnify, distort and multiply the image of a distant galaxy into arcs or even a complete ring.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Lensing types", "items": ["Strong: arcs, rings, multiple images", "Weak: slight distortions, mapping dark matter", "Microlensing: brief brightening, finds planets"]}
### Einstein rings
When source, lens and observer line up perfectly, the background galaxy appears as a ring around the lens. Webb and Hubble have imaged many.
### Uses
• **Natural telescopes**: lensing magnifies faint early galaxies Webb couldn't otherwise see
• **Dark matter maps**: lensing reveals mass we can't see
• **Finding exoplanets**: a star passing in front of another briefly brightens it; a planet adds a tell-tale blip
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Foreground mass", "Bends light from background galaxy", "Magnified, distorted images", "Map the mass"]}
!? A lensed supernova, "Refsdal", appeared four times around a galaxy in 2014, and astronomers correctly predicted a fifth image would appear about a year later.

# Nothing faster than light
As an object with mass speeds up, the energy needed to accelerate it further rises without limit as it approaches c. Reaching light speed would take infinite energy, so particles with mass can only get closer and closer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "total energy ÷ rest energy", "items": [{"l": "10% of c", "v": 1.005}, {"l": "90% of c", "v": 2.3}, {"l": "99% of c", "v": 7.1}, {"l": "99.99% of c", "v": 71.0, "hi": true}]}
### Fastest so far
• The LHC accelerates protons to 99.9999991% of c
• Parker Solar Probe, the fastest spacecraft, reached about 0.064% of c
• Light itself has no mass, so it always travels at c
### Causality
If information could travel faster than light, some observers would see effects before their causes. Relativity's speed limit protects cause and effect.
### Loopholes that aren't
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Quantum entanglement", "b": "correlations, but no usable message"}, {"a": "Expanding universe", "b": "space stretches, distant galaxies recede faster than c"}, {"a": "Warp drives", "b": "need exotic negative energy, speculative"}]}
> The universe's speed limit means the nearest stars, let alone other galaxies, will remain years to millions of years away, even for light.

# Becoming an astronaut
Agencies look for people who are healthy, calm under pressure, good team players and expert in something useful: medicine, engineering, science or flying. Candidates go through rounds of tests over more than a year.

# Training
Basic training takes about a year; mission-specific training can take two more. Astronauts must master the station's systems, science experiments, robotics and emergencies.

### Underwater spacewalks
NASA's Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory is a pool 62 m long and 12 m deep holding a full-size ISS mock-up. Weighted suits make astronauts neither float nor sink, imitating weightlessness for six-hour sessions.
### The skills list
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Russian language", "Spacewalk rehearsals underwater", "Robotic arm operation", "Medical first aid", "Survival training in forest, sea and caves"]}
### Expeditionary skills
ESA's CAVES programme sends astronauts underground for days to practise teamwork in isolation. Survival training covers landing off course in the wilderness, a real risk with capsules.
> Much training is about failure: fire, depressurisation and toxic leaks are rehearsed until responses become automatic.
!? Astronauts learn to fly jets and keep flying them; NASA uses T-38 trainers to practise fast decision-making.

# Eating in space
Space food has come a long way from 1960s tubes of paste. ISS crews choose from about 200 items: thermostabilised, freeze-dried, irradiated and fresh fruit when cargo arrives.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Freeze-dried", "b": "add water, e.g. soups"}, {"a": "Thermostabilised", "b": "heated pouches"}, {"a": "Natural form", "b": "nuts, biscuits"}, {"a": "Fresh", "b": "fruit after resupply"}]}
### Rules of the table
• No crumbs: they float into eyes and equipment, so tortillas replace bread
• Salt and pepper come as liquids
• Food sticks to forks by surface tension; drinks are sipped from pouches
• Fizzy drinks don't work well: gas and liquid don't separate in the stomach
### Taste changes
Fluid shifting to the head causes a "stuffy nose" feeling, dulling smell and taste. Astronauts often crave hot sauces and strong flavours.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "On Earth", "items": ["normal taste", "bread", "free-pouring drinks"]}, "r": {"t": "In orbit", "items": ["dull taste", "tortillas", "pouches with straws"]}}
### Growing food
Astronauts have grown lettuce, radishes, chillies and flowers in the Veggie chamber. Future Mars missions will need much more home-grown food.
!? In 1965 astronaut John Young smuggled a corned-beef sandwich onto Gemini 3; its crumbs caused a fuss in Congress.

# Sleeping
Each ISS astronaut has a small private crew quarter, the size of a phone booth, with a sleeping bag strapped to the wall, a laptop and a light. Without gravity there's no need for a bed: you just float in the bag.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Sleeping bag tied to the wall", "Eye mask for 16 sunrises a day", "Earplugs against fan noise", "Ventilation to avoid CO₂ pockets"]}
### Why ventilation matters
In weightlessness, warm breath doesn't rise. Exhaled CO₂ can pool around your face, causing headaches, so crew quarters need constant airflow.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Breathe out", "No convection to carry CO₂ away", "Fans move the air", "Fresh air around the face"]}
### Sleep problems
• Crews average about 6 hours a night, less than on Earth
• 16 sunrises a day disrupt the body clock, so lighting is tuned to mimic day and night
• Some astronauts' arms float up in front of them while asleep
!? Station "night" follows UTC (Greenwich time), so on the ISS everyone lives on London's clock, Portugal's winter time.

# Bodies in space
Weightlessness affects nearly every body system. Without the constant load of gravity, the body adapts as if those muscles and bones aren't needed.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Effects of microgravity", "items": ["Bones: ~1% density lost a month", "Muscles: weaken and shrink", "Fluids: shift to the head", "Eyes: some develop vision changes", "Balance: inner ear confused"]}
### Puffy face, bird legs
About 2 litres of fluid shift from the legs to the upper body. Astronauts get puffy faces and thin "bird legs" in the first days.
### Space adaptation
Over half of astronauts get space sickness during the first two or three days: nausea and disorientation as the brain reinterprets the inner ear's signals.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Days 1–3", "l": "space sickness"}, {"y": "Weeks", "l": "fluids settle"}, {"y": "Months", "l": "bone and muscle loss"}, {"y": "Back on Earth", "l": "months of recovery"}]}
### The Twin Study
In 2015–16 NASA compared astronaut Scott Kelly, who spent nearly a year in orbit, with his identical twin Mark on Earth. Most changes, in genes, immune system and microbes, returned to normal after landing; a few persisted.
!? Astronauts grow up to about 3 cm taller in orbit as their spine discs expand; they shrink back on return, sometimes with back pain.

# Exercise
Exercise is the main defence against bone and muscle loss. ISS crews exercise about 2 hours a day, six days a week, as part of their work schedule.

### How the machines work
In weightlessness, free weights weigh nothing. The ARED resistance device uses vacuum cylinders to create up to about 270 kg of resistance for squats and deadlifts. Treadmills use bungee harnesses to pull runners onto the belt.

### It works, partly
Modern exercise regimes have greatly reduced muscle loss and helped protect bone, though some bone loss still occurs. Diet with enough vitamin D and calories helps too.

> These lessons help on Earth too: research on astronaut bones informs osteoporosis treatment.

# Water recycling
Water is heavy to launch, so the ISS recycles almost everything: urine, sweat, shower water and even moisture from the air. In 2023 its system reached 98% water recovery.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Urine and humidity collected", "Distilled and filtered", "Catalytic oxidation and iodine", "Tested and drunk"]}
### The "coffee" joke
Astronauts joke that "yesterday's coffee becomes today's coffee". The recycled water is cleaner than most tap water on Earth.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of water recovered (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Before 2008", "v": 0.0}, {"l": "2017", "v": 93.0}, {"l": "2023", "v": 98.0, "hi": true}]}
### Air too
• Oxygen is made by splitting water with electricity (electrolysis)
• CO₂ is scrubbed from the air; some is combined with hydrogen to make more water
• Trace gases are filtered to avoid build-up
### Why it matters for Mars
A Mars trip would last 2–3 years with no resupply. Closed-loop life support that recycles water and air almost completely is essential.
!? The same technologies have been adapted for water purification in disaster zones and remote villages on Earth.

# Spacewalks
A spacewalk, or EVA (extravehicular activity), is one of the riskiest jobs in space. Astronauts install equipment, repair the station and fix science instruments, working in pairs and always tethered.

### The suit
A spacesuit is a one-person spacecraft: it provides oxygen, removes CO₂, protects against micrometeoroids and temperature extremes from about −150 °C to +120 °C, and has water-cooled underwear to remove body heat.

Suits run at lower pressure than the station, so astronauts first breathe pure oxygen to purge nitrogen from their blood, avoiding the bends, like divers.

# The overview effect
Author Frank White coined "the overview effect" in 1987 for the shift in perception many astronauts describe: seeing Earth whole, with no visible borders and a paper-thin atmosphere, creates a sense of unity and fragility.

Astronauts often say the atmosphere looks like a thin blue line, "like the skin of an apple". Many return with stronger feelings about protecting the planet.

# Coming home
Returning from months in orbit is physically hard. The brain has adapted to weightlessness, so on return gravity feels overwhelming: heads feel heavy, walking is wobbly and turning causes dizziness.

Soyuz capsules land under parachutes on the steppes of Kazakhstan, firing small rockets a metre above the ground. Crew Dragon splashes down in the ocean. Recovery teams help crews out, often in reclining chairs.

== sp-sky

# Constellations
A constellation is a pattern of stars, and since 1922 also an official region of the sky. The International Astronomical Union recognises 88 constellations that together tile the entire sky, so every star belongs to exactly one.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "88", "l": "official constellations"}, {"v": "48", "l": "from Ptolemy's 2nd-century list"}, {"v": "1930", "l": "borders fixed"}]}
### Patterns aren't neighbours
Stars in a constellation usually lie at very different distances. The stars of Orion's belt are about 700–1,300 light-years away, unrelated to each other except by line of sight.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "distance, light-years", "items": [{"l": "Bellatrix", "v": 250.0}, {"l": "Betelgeuse", "v": 550.0}, {"l": "Rigel", "v": 860.0}, {"l": "Alnilam, in the belt", "v": 1300.0, "hi": true}]}
### Asterisms
Some famous patterns aren't official constellations. The Big Dipper (or Plough) is part of Ursa Major; the Summer Triangle links stars from three constellations.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Big Dipper", "b": "part of Ursa Major"}, {"a": "Summer Triangle", "b": "Vega, Deneb, Altair"}, {"a": "Winter Hexagon", "b": "six bright stars around Orion"}]}
### Many cultures, many skies
Aboriginal Australians saw an "Emu in the Sky" in dark dust lanes; Chinese astronomers divided the sky into 28 lunar mansions; Polynesian navigators used star paths to cross the Pacific.
!? Several southern constellations, like Telescopium and Microscopium, were named by Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille in the 1750s after scientific instruments.

# Finding the North Star
Polaris lies almost exactly above Earth's North Pole, so as Earth spins, it stays nearly fixed while other stars circle around it. Its altitude above the horizon equals your latitude: about 39° in Lisbon.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Find the Big Dipper", "Two pointer stars on the bowl", "Extend ~5 times their gap", "Polaris"]}
### Not the brightest
Polaris is only about the 48th brightest star: moderately bright, not dazzling. People often expect it to be brighter.
!! The North Star is the brightest star in the sky => It's only moderately bright; Sirius is the brightest
### Navigation
Sailors, including Portuguese navigators, measured Polaris' height with quadrants and astrolabes to find their latitude. Near the equator, Polaris sinks to the horizon; south of it, it's invisible.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lisbon", "b": "Polaris ~39° up"}, {"a": "Porto", "b": "~41° up"}, {"a": "Equator", "b": "on the horizon"}, {"a": "North Pole", "b": "straight overhead"}]}
### Pole stars change
Earth's axis wobbles in a 26,000-year cycle (precession). About 4,500 years ago the pole star was Thuban; in about 12,000 years it will be Vega.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~2700 BCE", "l": "Thuban"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "Polaris"}, {"y": "~14000 CE", "l": "Vega"}]}
> No equivalent bright star marks the South Pole; southern navigators use the Southern Cross instead.

# The sky turns
As Earth rotates west to east, the whole sky appears to turn east to west around the celestial poles, once every 23 hours 56 minutes. Stars near Polaris never set; they're called circumpolar.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Rise in the east", "Climb to the south", "Set in the west", "Circle below the horizon"]}
### Circumpolar stars
From Portugal, stars within about 39° of Polaris never set, including the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia. Farther south, fewer stars are circumpolar.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Circumpolar", "items": ["never set", "near Polaris"]}, "r": {"t": "Rising and setting", "items": ["cross the horizon daily"]}}
### Star trails
A long-exposure photo pointed at Polaris shows stars as concentric arcs. A full circle would need a 24-hour exposure in darkness, only possible in polar winter.
### Earlier each night
Each night, a star rises about 4 minutes earlier, adding up to 2 hours a month. That's why the constellations shift with the seasons.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Night 1: rises 22:00", "A week later: ~21:32", "A month later: ~20:00"]}
!? The 4-minute difference is why a sidereal day (23 h 56 min) is shorter than a solar day.

# Seasonal stars
At night we look away from the Sun. As Earth orbits, the night side faces different directions in space, so different constellations dominate the evening sky through the year.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Winter: Orion, Taurus, Gemini", "Spring: Leo, Virgo", "Summer: Scorpius, Sagittarius, Summer Triangle", "Autumn: Pegasus, Andromeda"]}
### The zodiac
The Sun appears to move through 13 constellations along the ecliptic over a year, including Ophiuchus, which astrology ignores. The traditional zodiac dates are also off by about a month because of precession.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Astrology", "b": "12 signs, fixed dates from ~2,000 years ago"}, {"a": "Astronomy", "b": "13 constellations on the ecliptic, dates shifted"}, {"a": "Precession", "b": "shifts the sky over 26,000 years"}]}
### Highlights from Portugal
• **Winter**: Orion, Sirius, the Pleiades cluster
• **Spring**: Leo's sickle, Arcturus
• **Summer**: the Milky Way through Sagittarius and Scorpius, low in the south
• **Autumn**: the Andromeda galaxy, Pegasus' great square

# Orion
Orion, the Hunter, is one of the most recognisable constellations, visible from almost anywhere on Earth because it straddles the celestial equator. It dominates winter evenings in the Northern Hemisphere.

### The Orion Nebula
Below the belt, Orion's "sword" contains a fuzzy patch: the Orion Nebula, a stellar nursery 1,300 light-years away where thousands of stars are forming. It's visible with the naked eye on dark nights and stunning in binoculars.
### Betelgeuse
Betelgeuse is a red supergiant so large that, placed where the Sun is, it would extend past the orbit of Mars, maybe Jupiter. In 2019–20 it dimmed dramatically after belching out a dust cloud: the "Great Dimming".

# Star brightness
Ancient astronomer Hipparchus ranked stars from 1st magnitude (brightest) to 6th (faintest visible). The modern scale keeps that backwards system and makes it precise: each step of 1 magnitude means about 2.5 times brighter; 5 magnitudes means exactly 100 times.

### The scale
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": -27.0, "hi": 8.0, "unit": "magnitude", "items": [{"l": "Sun", "v": -26.7}, {"l": "Full Moon", "v": -12.7}, {"l": "Sirius", "v": -1.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Eye limit", "v": 6.0}]}
### Apparent vs absolute
Apparent magnitude is how bright a star looks from Earth; absolute magnitude is how bright it would look from a standard distance of 32.6 light-years. Sirius looks bright mainly because it's close; Rigel looks fainter but is far more luminous.

# Planets wander
Ancient sky-watchers noticed five bright "stars" that moved against the fixed constellations: the planets, from the Greek *planētēs*, "wanderer". All stay close to the ecliptic, because the Solar System is flat.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Planet near a star", "Weeks later: moved along the ecliptic", "Sometimes loops backwards", "Then continues"]}
### Retrograde motion
Sometimes a planet seems to stop and move backwards for weeks. This happens when Earth, on its faster inside track, overtakes an outer planet, like passing a slower car on a motorway.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth's-centred explanation", "items": ["epicycles, loops on loops"]}, "r": {"t": "Sun-centred explanation", "items": ["Earth overtaking the planet"]}}
### Mercury retrograde
Mercury appears to go retrograde about three times a year. Astronomically it's just a viewing angle; it has no effect on phones, emails or relationships.
!! Mercury retrograde causes chaos => It's an optical effect of orbital motion with no influence on Earth
### Conjunctions
When planets appear close together in the sky, it's a conjunction: a lovely sight, though they're actually hundreds of millions of km apart. In December 2020, Jupiter and Saturn appeared closer than at any time since 1623.
> Copernicus' explanation of retrograde motion was one of the strongest arguments for a Sun-centred Solar System.

# The Milky Way band
The Milky Way is shaped like a thin disc. From inside, looking along the disc we see a band crowded with stars; looking out of it, we see far fewer. The band's dark lanes are clouds of dust blocking distant starlight.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["We sit inside a flat disc", "Look along it: band of stars", "Look away: sparse stars"]}
### Best seen
• On moonless nights away from cities, ideally where the sky is rated Bortle 1–3
• From Portugal, the brightest part, towards the galactic centre in Sagittarius, is low in the south in summer
• The Alentejo and the interior mountains have some of Europe's darkest skies

### Its name
The Greeks saw spilled milk from the goddess Hera: *galaxias*, from *gala*, milk, gave us "galaxy". In Portuguese it's the Via Láctea, and pilgrims once called it the Way of St James, guiding to Santiago de Compostela.
!? Galileo was the first to show with a telescope, in 1610, that the Milky Way's glow is made of countless faint stars.

# Using your eyes
Your eye adapts to darkness in two stages: pupils widen within seconds, then the retina's rod cells build up a light-sensitive pigment, rhodopsin, over 20–30 minutes. One glance at a bright phone resets much of it.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "0 s", "l": "pupil opens"}, {"y": "5–10 min", "l": "cones adapt"}, {"y": "20–30 min", "l": "rods fully adapted"}, {"y": "1 glance at a phone", "l": "reset"}]}
### Tricks of experienced observers
• **Red light**: rods are almost blind to deep red, so red torches preserve night vision
• **Averted vision**: look slightly to the side of a faint object; the edges of the retina have more rods
• **Breathing and comfort**: a reclining chair and warm clothes help you stay patient
• **Phone apps** with night mode turn the screen red

### Why stars look colourless
Faint light is seen by rods, which don't sense colour, so most stars look white. Bright stars like Betelgeuse and Rigel show their orange and blue tints.

# The asteroid belt
The main belt lies between Mars and Jupiter, about 2.1 to 3.3 AU from the Sun. It contains more than a million asteroids over 1 km, yet its total mass is only about 3% of the Moon's, and half of that is in four bodies: Ceres, Vesta, Pallas and Hygiea.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of belt mass (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Ceres", "v": 39.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Vesta", "v": 9.0}, {"l": "Pallas", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Hygiea", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Everything else", "v": 42.0}]}
### Why no planet formed
Jupiter's strong gravity stirred up the region, making collisions too violent for rocks to stick together. Many fragments were flung out, leaving a sparse belt.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Planetesimals form", "Jupiter's gravity stirs them", "Collisions shatter instead of merge", "Thin belt remains"]}
### Mostly empty space
The average distance between asteroids over 1 km is around a million km. More than a dozen spacecraft have crossed the belt without needing to dodge.
!! The belt is a dense field of rocks => Typical gaps are hundreds of thousands to millions of km
### Families
Collisions shatter larger asteroids into "families" sharing similar orbits. Vesta's family came from a giant impact that also sent meteorites to Earth; we have pieces of Vesta in museums.
!? Kirkwood gaps, empty zones in the belt, mark orbits where Jupiter's regular tugs clear asteroids out.

# Ceres
Ceres, discovered by Giuseppe Piazzi on 1 January 1801, was first called a planet, then an asteroid, and since 2006 a dwarf planet. It's the only dwarf planet in the inner Solar System and is large enough to be round.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1801", "l": "discovered, called a planet"}, {"y": "1850s", "l": "reclassified as an asteroid"}, {"y": "2006", "l": "dwarf planet"}, {"y": "2015–18", "l": "Dawn orbits Ceres"}]}
### Dawn's discoveries
NASA's Dawn spacecraft, using efficient ion engines, orbited first Vesta and then Ceres. It found bright spots in Occator Crater: deposits of sodium carbonate salts left by briny water seeping up from below.

### A wet world
• Ceres may be about a quarter water ice by mass
• It has ammonia-bearing clays, suggesting it formed farther out and migrated inwards
• Organic molecules were detected on its surface
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Vesta", "items": ["rocky", "dry", "source of meteorites"]}, "r": {"t": "Ceres", "items": ["icy", "briny", "salt deposits"]}}

# Comet anatomy
A comet's nucleus is a dark, porous lump of ice, dust and organic material, typically 1–20 km across, darker than charcoal. Far from the Sun it's inert. Within roughly 3 AU, sunlight turns ice directly into gas, which drags dust with it.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Ion tail: blue, straight, pushed by solar wind", "Dust tail: curved, yellowish", "Coma: up to 100,000+ km cloud", "Nucleus: a few km"]}
### Two tails
The **ion tail** is made of charged gas swept straight back by the solar wind, glowing blue. The **dust tail** is pushed more gently by sunlight pressure and curves along the comet's path. Both point away from the Sun, even when the comet moves outwards.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ion tail", "items": ["straight", "blue", "solar wind"]}, "r": {"t": "Dust tail", "items": ["curved", "yellow-white", "sunlight pressure"]}}
### Rosetta
ESA's Rosetta orbited comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko for two years (2014–16) and dropped the Philae lander on it. The comet was shaped like a rubber duck and smelled, according to its gas, of rotten eggs, ammonia and almonds.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2004", "l": "launch"}, {"y": "2014", "l": "arrival and Philae landing"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Rosetta lands on the comet, mission ends"}]}
!? Comet tails can stretch over 100 million km, nearly the Earth–Sun distance.

# Halley's Comet
Halley's Comet returns roughly every 76 years. Edmond Halley, using Newton's new laws, realised that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 were the same object and predicted its return in late 1758. It reappeared on Christmas night 1758, after Halley's death, a triumph for Newtonian physics.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "240 BCE", "l": "earliest certain record, China"}, {"y": "1066", "l": "on the Bayeux Tapestry"}, {"y": "1682", "l": "Halley observes it"}, {"y": "1758", "l": "predicted return"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "last visit"}, {"y": "2061", "l": "next return"}]}
### Halley in history
• Its 1066 appearance was seen as an omen before the Battle of Hastings and embroidered on the Bayeux Tapestry
• Giotto painted it as the Star of Bethlehem around 1305
• In 1910 Earth passed through its tail, and some sold "comet pills" to frightened people
### The 1986 flotilla
Spacecraft from Europe, the USSR and Japan met the comet. ESA's Giotto flew within 600 km of the nucleus and photographed it: a dark, peanut-shaped body 15 km long with jets of gas.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "15 km", "l": "nucleus length"}, {"v": "600 km", "l": "Giotto's closest pass"}, {"v": "~76 years", "l": "orbital period"}]}
### Meteor showers from Halley
Dust shed by Halley produces two meteor showers each year: the Eta Aquariids in May and the Orionids in October.
!? Mark Twain was born in 1835 during Halley's appearance and predicted he'd die when it returned; he died in 1910, the day after its perihelion.

# Where comets come from
Comets are frozen leftovers from the outer Solar System. They're stored in two great reservoirs and occasionally nudged inwards.

### The Oort Cloud
Proposed by Jan Oort in 1950, the Oort Cloud may contain trillions of icy bodies, extending perhaps a light-year or more. It has never been seen directly; its existence is inferred from comets arriving from all directions with very long orbits.

### Interstellar visitors
Some comets come from other stars entirely. 'Oumuamua (2017), 2I/Borisov (2019) and 3I/ATLAS (2025) passed through our Solar System on one-time paths, carrying material from other planetary systems.

# The dinosaur killer
About 66 million years ago, an asteroid about 10 km wide struck what is now the Yucatán Peninsula at around 20 km/s. The Chicxulub crater it left is about 180 km wide, now buried under sediment.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Impact", "Megatsunamis and global fires", "Soot and sulphur block sunlight", "Years of \"impact winter\"", "~75% of species extinct"]}
### The evidence
In 1980 Luis and Walter Alvarez found a thin clay layer worldwide, rich in iridium, an element rare on Earth's surface but common in asteroids, exactly at the boundary between the Cretaceous and the Paleogene. The crater itself was identified in 1991.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Paleogene rocks: no dinosaurs", "Iridium-rich clay layer", "Cretaceous rocks: dinosaurs"]}
### Winners and losers
• All non-bird dinosaurs, pterosaurs and most marine reptiles disappeared
• Small mammals, birds, crocodiles and turtles survived
• Mammals then diversified, eventually including us
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of species lost (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "All species", "v": 75.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Non-bird dinosaurs", "v": 100.0}]}
### Volcanoes too?
India's Deccan Traps erupted vast lava floods around the same time, adding climate stress. Most scientists now see the impact as the main trigger.
!? The asteroid hit sulphur-rich rock; if it had struck deep ocean, the climate effects and extinction might have been much smaller.

# Tunguska
On 30 June 1908, a fireball exploded over the Podkamennaya Tunguska River in Siberia, about 5–10 km above the ground. It was probably a stony asteroid or comet fragment roughly 50–60 m wide.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "30 Jun 1908", "l": "date"}, {"v": "~10–15 megatons", "l": "energy"}, {"v": "2,000 km²", "l": "forest flattened"}]}
### No crater
Because it exploded in the air, there was no crater. Trees at the centre were stripped but left standing, while those further out were flattened radially, pointing away from the blast.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Rock enters at ~15–30 km/s", "Heats and breaks up", "Air burst at 5–10 km", "Shock wave flattens forest"]}
### Late investigation
The remote area wasn't studied until Leonid Kulik's expedition in 1927. Eyewitnesses 60 km away described being thrown from their chairs and feeling intense heat.
### If it hit a city
An air burst of that size over a big city would cause enormous destruction. That's part of why 30 June is now Asteroid Day, to raise awareness of impact risks.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Over Siberia", "items": ["few people", "no deaths confirmed"]}, "r": {"t": "Over a city", "items": ["catastrophic damage"]}}
!? For several nights after the event, skies over Europe were so bright that people could read newspapers outdoors at midnight.

# Chelyabinsk
On 15 February 2013, a 20 m, 12,000-tonne asteroid entered over Russia at about 19 km/s. It exploded about 30 km up with the energy of roughly 500 kilotons of TNT, about 30 Hiroshima bombs.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "15 Feb 2013", "l": "date"}, {"v": "~500 kt", "l": "energy"}, {"v": "~1,500", "l": "people injured"}, {"v": "~7,200", "l": "buildings damaged"}]}
### Why people were hurt
The flash was brighter than the Sun. Curious residents went to windows to look, and about a minute later the shock wave arrived, shattering glass. Most injuries came from flying glass.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Bright flash", "People go to windows", "Shock wave arrives", "Windows shatter"]}
### Unseen until it hit
It came from the direction of the Sun, where telescopes can't look, so nobody saw it coming. By coincidence, a different, unrelated asteroid passed close to Earth the same day.
### Lessons
• Even small asteroids can cause damage
• Dashcams in Russia captured the event, giving scientists precise trajectories
• It boosted funding for telescopes to find smaller asteroids
!? A 650 kg fragment was recovered from Lake Chebarkul and is displayed in a local museum.

# Planetary defence
Planetary defence is about finding dangerous asteroids early and, if necessary, changing their path. Survey telescopes have found over 95% of near-Earth asteroids larger than 1 km; the hunt continues for smaller ones.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Survey telescopes find it", "Calculate orbit", "Assess risk", "If needed, deflect years ahead"]}
### DART
On 26 September 2022 NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately crashed into Dimorphos, a 160 m moonlet orbiting the asteroid Didymos, at 6.1 km/s. It shortened Dimorphos' orbit by 33 minutes, far more than the minimum goal of 73 seconds, partly because ejected debris acted like a rocket.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "orbit change, minutes", "items": [{"l": "Minimum goal", "v": 1.2}, {"l": "Achieved", "v": 33.0, "hi": true}]}
### Hera
ESA's Hera mission, launched in 2024, will reach Didymos in 2026 to study the crater and measure Dimorphos' mass, turning DART into a repeatable technique.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2021", "l": "DART launched"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "impact"}, {"y": "2024", "l": "Hera launched"}, {"y": "2026", "l": "Hera arrives"}]}
### Other options
• Kinetic impactor: hit it hard, like DART
• Gravity tractor: hover nearby for years, gently pulling it off course
• Nuclear device: last resort for large, late-detected threats
> The key is time: a tiny nudge decades in advance is far easier than a big push weeks before impact.

# How worried should we be?
Impact risk is low probability but high consequence. The smaller the object, the more often it arrives, and the less harm it does.

### Known threats
No known asteroid has a significant chance of hitting Earth in the next century. Apophis, 340 m, will pass safely at about 32,000 km on 13 April 2029, closer than geostationary satellites and visible to the naked eye from Europe.

### The Torino scale
Astronomers rate impact threats from 0 (no hazard) to 10 (certain global catastrophe). Almost all objects sit at 0. When an asteroid briefly scores higher, extra observations nearly always bring it back to 0.

Your lifetime risk of dying in an impact is extremely small but not zero, comparable to some rare natural hazards. Unlike earthquakes, though, impacts are preventable with enough warning.

# What the universe is made of
Measurements of the cosmic microwave background, galaxy clusters and supernovae all agree on the universe's recipe: about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of the universe's energy", "items": [{"l": "Ordinary matter", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Dark matter", "v": 27.0}, {"l": "Dark energy", "v": 68.0, "hi": true}]}
### Even ordinary matter is mostly hidden
Of the 5% that's ordinary matter, only a small part is in stars. Most is hot, thin gas between galaxies, hard to detect.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of ordinary matter (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Stars", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Gas in galaxies", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Gas between galaxies", "v": 90.0, "hi": true}]}
### How we know
• The pattern of hot and cold spots in the cosmic microwave background depends on these amounts
• Galaxy rotation and cluster motions show extra unseen mass
• Distant supernovae reveal accelerating expansion
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Evidence", "items": ["Cosmic microwave background", "Galaxy rotation", "Gravitational lensing", "Supernova distances"]}
> Everything humans have ever seen through any telescope makes up about 5% of what's out there.

# Spinning too fast
In a solar system, outer planets move slower than inner ones because most mass is at the centre. Astronomers expected the same for stars in galaxies. In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured galaxy rotation and found outer stars moving just as fast as inner ones: "flat rotation curves".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Expected", "items": ["speed falls with distance", "like the planets"]}, "r": {"t": "Measured", "items": ["speed stays flat", "hidden mass needed"]}}
### The interpretation
Flat rotation curves mean galaxies must contain much more mass than we see, spread in a large halo extending far beyond the visible disc. Without it, the outer stars would fly off.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Dark matter halo: most of the mass", "Visible disc: stars and gas", "Bulge and centre"]}
### Vera Rubin's legacy
Rubin faced barriers as a woman in astronomy, yet her work became the cornerstone evidence for dark matter. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, which released its first images in 2025, bears her name.
!? In the Milky Way, dark matter may outweigh stars and gas by about 5 to 1, perhaps more including the full halo.

# Fritz Zwicky
Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky, working at Caltech, studied the Coma Cluster in 1933. Galaxies were moving so fast that the cluster should have flown apart, unless it contained hundreds of times more mass than its visible light suggested. He called it "dunkle Materie".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Measure galaxy speeds in Coma", "Calculate required mass", "Far more than the visible stars", "Invisible \"dark matter\""]}
### Ignored for decades
His conclusion was largely overlooked for 40 years, partly because his estimates were rough and partly because he was famously abrasive. Rubin's rotation curves in the 1970s brought the idea back.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1933", "l": "Zwicky, Coma Cluster"}, {"y": "1970s", "l": "Rubin and Ford, rotation curves"}, {"y": "1980s", "l": "dark matter mainstream"}, {"y": "2006", "l": "Bullet Cluster"}]}
### A prolific mind
Zwicky also coined "supernova" with Walter Baade and proposed that supernovae create neutron stars, years before neutron stars were found.
> Good ideas can wait decades for evidence and attention to catch up.

# The Bullet Cluster
The Bullet Cluster is two galaxy clusters that collided about 150 million years ago, 3.7 billion light-years away. It offered one of the most direct pieces of evidence that dark matter is a real substance, not just a quirk of gravity.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Two clusters collide", "Gas clouds smash and slow down", "Galaxies and dark matter pass through", "Mass and gas end up separated"]}
### Two maps compared
X-ray telescopes (Chandra) mapped the hot gas, which holds most of the ordinary matter. Gravitational lensing mapped where the mass actually is. The mass sat with the galaxies, not with the gas.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "X-ray map", "items": ["hot gas", "slowed in the middle"]}, "r": {"t": "Lensing map", "items": ["most mass", "passed straight through"]}}
### Why it matters
Alternative theories that modify gravity struggle to explain mass located away from most ordinary matter. Dark matter that barely interacts, passing through like ghosts, explains it naturally.
!? The "bullet" is a shock wave of hot gas shaped like a bullet, moving at about 4,500 km/s.

# What could it be?
Dark matter must have mass and gravity but almost no interaction with light or ordinary matter. It can't be ordinary atoms, which would show up in measurements of the early universe, so physicists look for new particles.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Candidates", "items": ["WIMPs: heavy weakly interacting particles", "Axions: extremely light particles", "Sterile neutrinos", "Primordial black holes"]}
### How we hunt
• **Direct detection**: huge tanks of liquid xenon deep underground wait for a dark matter particle to bump a nucleus
• **Indirect detection**: telescopes look for radiation from dark matter particles annihilating
• **Colliders**: the LHC tries to create dark matter particles
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Shield detector deep underground", "Wait for rare collisions", "Filter out background", "So far: nothing"]}
### Modified gravity
A minority of physicists propose that gravity behaves differently at very low accelerations (MOND). It explains galaxy rotation well but struggles with clusters and the cosmic microwave background.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Dark matter", "items": ["new particles", "fits many observations"]}, "r": {"t": "Modified gravity", "items": ["new laws", "fits galaxies, struggles elsewhere"]}}
!? Billions of dark matter particles may be passing through your body every second without leaving any trace.

# Dark energy
In 1998 two teams measuring distant Type Ia supernovae found they were fainter, so farther, than expected. The universe's expansion is accelerating. Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess won the 2011 Nobel Prize.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1917", "l": "Einstein's cosmological constant"}, {"y": "1929", "l": "expansion discovered"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "acceleration discovered"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Nobel Prize"}]}
### What might it be?
The simplest explanation is Einstein's "cosmological constant": an energy belonging to empty space itself. As space expands, there's more of it, so its push grows relative to matter's pull.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Matter", "items": ["pulls together", "dilutes as space grows"]}, "r": {"t": "Dark energy", "items": ["pushes apart", "stays constant per volume"]}}
### A cosmic tug-of-war
For the first ~9 billion years, gravity slowed the expansion. About 5 billion years ago, dark energy took over and expansion started to accelerate.

# Standard candles
To measure distance, astronomers need objects whose true brightness they know: "standard candles". Comparing true and apparent brightness gives distance, just as you can judge how far away a 100 W bulb is from how dim it looks.

### The cosmic distance ladder
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Type Ia supernovae: billions of ly", "Cepheid stars: millions of ly", "Parallax: thousands of ly", "Radar: Solar System"]}
### Type Ia supernovae
They happen when a white dwarf in a binary system gains matter until it reaches about 1.4 Suns and explodes. Because they explode at a similar mass, their peak brightness is similar, and so bright they're visible across billions of light-years.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["White dwarf steals matter", "Reaches ~1.4 Suns", "Thermonuclear explosion", "Known peak brightness"]}
### The Hubble tension
Measuring the expansion rate with the distance ladder gives about 73 km/s per megaparsec; measuring it from the early universe gives about 67. This "Hubble tension" may signal new physics.

# The cosmic web
Maps of millions of galaxies reveal a vast web: galaxies gather along filaments and sheets, with clusters at the knots, surrounding enormous voids up to hundreds of millions of light-years across.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Cosmic web", "items": ["Nodes: galaxy clusters", "Filaments: chains of galaxies", "Walls: sheets", "Voids: nearly empty"]}
### How it formed
Tiny density ripples in the early universe, visible in the cosmic microwave background, grew under gravity. Dark matter collapsed first into a scaffolding; ordinary gas flowed along it and formed galaxies at the densest points.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tiny ripples after the Big Bang", "Dark matter clumps", "Gas follows", "Galaxies light up the web"]}
### Simulations
Supercomputer simulations like Millennium and IllustrisTNG start from early-universe conditions, add dark matter and physics, and reproduce a web that looks strikingly like the real one.
### Our place
The Milky Way sits in a modest filament near the edge of the Local Void. Galaxy surveys have mapped the Laniakea Supercluster, our "immeasurable heaven", about 500 million light-years across.
!? The cosmic web's structure looks remarkably similar to networks of neurons in the brain, though the similarity is only visual.

# How GPS works
Each GPS satellite broadcasts its precise position and the exact time from an onboard atomic clock. Your receiver notes when each signal arrives; the delay, multiplied by the speed of light, gives the distance to each satellite.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "distance = signal delay × speed of light", "items": [{"s": "delay", "m": "tiny fractions of a second"}, {"s": "speed of light", "m": "300,000 km/s"}]}
### Trilateration
Knowing your distance from one satellite puts you somewhere on a sphere. Two spheres intersect in a circle; three narrow it to two points, one of which is usually far out in space.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["One distance: a sphere", "Two: a circle", "Three: two points", "Earth's surface picks one"]}
### Accuracy
• Phones typically get 3–5 m; combining satellite systems and Wi-Fi improves it
• Dual-frequency receivers in newer phones correct atmospheric errors
• Surveyors using correction stations reach centimetre accuracy
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "typical accuracy, m", "items": [{"l": "Early civil GPS, before 2000", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Phone today", "v": 4.0}, {"l": "Dual-frequency", "v": 1.0}, {"l": "Survey grade", "v": 0.02, "hi": true}]}
### Selective availability
Until May 2000 the US deliberately degraded civilian GPS to about 100 m accuracy. Switching it off overnight made car navigation practical.
> Your phone doesn't send anything to the satellites: GPS reception is one-way, which is why it works offline.

# Why four satellites?
To calculate position from timing, you need perfectly synchronised clocks. Satellites have atomic clocks; your phone has a cheap quartz clock that drifts. That unknown clock error is a fourth unknown, alongside latitude, longitude and altitude.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Unknown 1", "b": "latitude"}, {"a": "Unknown 2", "b": "longitude"}, {"a": "Unknown 3", "b": "altitude"}, {"a": "Unknown 4", "b": "receiver clock error"}]}
### Four equations, four unknowns
With signals from four satellites, the receiver solves four equations at once, correcting its own clock to nanosecond accuracy as a bonus. Your phone is, in effect, briefly synchronised to an atomic clock.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["4 satellite signals", "4 equations", "Solve position and clock error", "Accurate fix"]}
### More is better
• Modern receivers often track 20–40 satellites from several systems
• Satellites spread across the sky give better geometry than clustered ones
• In cities, tall buildings block and reflect signals, causing errors ("urban canyons")
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Good geometry", "items": ["satellites spread out", "precise"]}, "r": {"t": "Poor geometry", "items": ["clustered or blocked", "less precise"]}}
!? GPS timing synchronises mobile phone networks and power grids worldwide, a use as important as navigation.

== sp-pioneers

# Ancient astronomers
Ancient peoples watched the sky for calendars, agriculture, navigation and religion. Babylonian scribes kept astronomical diaries for centuries and could predict lunar eclipses using cycles such as the 18-year Saros.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~1800 BCE", "l": "Babylonian records"}, {"y": "~585 BCE", "l": "Thales' eclipse prediction"}, {"y": "~240 BCE", "l": "Eratosthenes"}, {"y": "~130 BCE", "l": "Hipparchus' star catalogue"}]}
### Eratosthenes measures the Earth
At noon on the summer solstice, the Sun shone straight down a well in Syene (Aswan). In Alexandria, 800 km north, a stick cast a shadow at about 7°, one-fiftieth of a circle. So Earth's circumference was about 50 × 800 km = 40,000 km, remarkably close to the true 40,075 km.
ex: 7.2° ÷ 360° = 1/50 | 50 × 800 km | ≈ 40,000 km
### Other Greek achievements
• Aristarchus proposed a Sun-centred system around 270 BCE
• Hipparchus discovered the precession of the equinoxes and catalogued about 850 stars
• The Antikythera mechanism, a geared bronze calculator, predicted eclipses and planet positions
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Eratosthenes", "b": "Earth's size"}, {"a": "Aristarchus", "b": "Sun at the centre"}, {"a": "Hipparchus", "b": "precession, star catalogue"}, {"a": "Antikythera mechanism", "b": "ancient astronomical computer"}]}
!? Columbus underestimated Earth's size; scholars who used Eratosthenes-style estimates were closer to the truth.

# Ptolemy's model
Claudius Ptolemy, working in Alexandria around 150 CE, wrote the *Almagest*, which put Earth at the centre with the Sun, Moon and planets moving on circles. To explain retrograde loops, planets moved on small circles (epicycles) riding on bigger ones (deferents).
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Planet on small epicycle", "Epicycle rides on big deferent", "Combined motion", "Loops in the sky"]}
### Why it lasted
Ptolemy's model predicted planetary positions well enough for navigation, calendars and astrology for 1,400 years. It was translated into Arabic and refined by astronomers in Baghdad, Damascus and Samarkand before returning to Europe.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~150 CE", "l": "the Almagest"}, {"y": "800s", "l": "Arabic translations"}, {"y": "1100s", "l": "Latin translations"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "challenged by Copernicus"}]}
### Its weaknesses
• Many arbitrary adjustments, like off-centre points called equants
• No physical explanation for why planets moved that way
• Couldn't explain why Mercury and Venus always stay near the Sun without special tweaks
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ptolemy", "items": ["accurate enough", "complicated", "Earth-centred"]}, "r": {"t": "Copernicus", "items": ["similar accuracy at first", "simpler logic", "Sun-centred"]}}
> A model can make good predictions and still be wrong about how the world really is.

# Copernicus
Nicolaus Copernicus, a Polish canon and astronomer, placed the Sun near the centre with Earth as one of the planets, orbiting yearly and spinning daily. His book *On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres* appeared in 1543, the year he died.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Earth-centred", "items": ["Earth still", "everything orbits it"]}, "r": {"t": "Sun-centred", "items": ["Earth spins and orbits", "planets in order from the Sun"]}}
### What it explained elegantly
• Retrograde motion: Earth overtaking outer planets
• Why Mercury and Venus stay near the Sun: their orbits are inside ours
• The order and relative distances of the planets
### What it didn't fix
Copernicus kept perfect circles, so he still needed epicycles, and his model was not much more accurate than Ptolemy's. It took Kepler's ellipses to make the Sun-centred system clearly superior.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1514", "l": "first sketch circulated"}, {"y": "1543", "l": "book published"}, {"y": "1609", "l": "Kepler's ellipses"}, {"y": "1687", "l": "Newton's gravity"}]}
### A slow revolution
For decades, many astronomers used Copernicus' tables for calculation without believing Earth moved. Galileo's observations and Kepler's laws gradually made the case.
!? The phrase "Copernican revolution" now means any change that removes humans from a special central place.

# Tycho and Kepler
Danish nobleman Tycho Brahe (1546–1601) built the best observatory of his age on the island of Hven, measuring planetary positions to about 1 arcminute without a telescope. He proposed his own hybrid model: the Sun orbits Earth, while the planets orbit the Sun.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tycho", "b": "precise data, hybrid model"}, {"a": "Kepler", "b": "mathematics, elliptical orbits"}, {"a": "Together", "b": "data plus theory"}]}
### Kepler's struggle with Mars
Kepler spent years trying to fit Mars' orbit with circles. The best circle missed Tycho's data by 8 arcminutes. Trusting the data, he abandoned circles and found an ellipse fitted perfectly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tycho's precise Mars data", "Circles miss by 8 arcminutes", "Try ovals", "Ellipse fits exactly"]}
### Lessons
• Precise data can overturn centuries of assumption
• Kepler's laws were empirical: they described motion without explaining why
• Newton later showed gravity produces exactly Kepler's ellipses
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1572", "l": "Tycho's supernova"}, {"y": "1600", "l": "Kepler joins Tycho"}, {"y": "1609", "l": "first two laws"}, {"y": "1619", "l": "third law"}]}
!? Tycho lost part of his nose in a duel over a mathematical dispute and wore a metal replacement.

# Galileo's trial
In 1616 the Catholic Church declared the Sun-centred idea "formally heretical" and warned Galileo not to hold or defend it. In 1632 he published *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, which seemed to mock the Earth-centred view.

### The trial
Aged 69, Galileo was tried by the Roman Inquisition, forced to recant and sentenced to house arrest for life. He continued working, writing his important book on motion and materials, *Two New Sciences* (1638).
### The legend
The famous line "Eppur si muove" ("and yet it moves"), supposedly muttered after recanting, probably wasn't said at the trial; it first appears in print over a century later.

In 1992 Pope John Paul II formally acknowledged errors in the Church's handling of the case. The episode remains a powerful symbol of conflicts between authority and evidence.

# Newton
Isaac Newton's *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) set out three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. With them, he derived Kepler's laws, explained tides and predicted the shape of the Earth.

Before Newton, many believed the heavens obeyed different rules from Earth. Newton showed the same gravity governs a falling apple and the Moon's orbit: a unification that shaped all of later science.

• Invented calculus, independently of Leibniz
• Showed white light is a mixture of colours using prisms

# Herschel
William Herschel, a German-born musician in England, built his own excellent reflecting telescopes. On 13 March 1781 he noticed a "star" that showed a disc and moved: Uranus, doubling the known size of the Solar System.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1781", "l": "Uranus discovered"}, {"y": "1787", "l": "two moons of Uranus"}, {"y": "1789", "l": "12 m-long telescope"}, {"y": "1800", "l": "infrared discovered"}]}
### Mapping the galaxy
Herschel counted stars in hundreds of directions to estimate the Milky Way's shape: the first attempt at mapping our galaxy, concluding it was a flattened disc.
### Caroline Herschel
Caroline worked alongside William, discovered eight comets and several nebulae, and became the first woman paid for scientific work in Britain and the first to receive the Royal Astronomical Society's Gold Medal.

### Discovering infrared
In 1800 William put thermometers in the colours of a sunlight spectrum and found it hottest beyond the red, where no light was visible: infrared radiation.

# Henrietta Leavitt
Henrietta Swan Leavitt worked as a "computer" at Harvard College Observatory, analysing photographic plates for 30 cents an hour. Studying thousands of variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds, she noticed that brighter Cepheid stars pulsed more slowly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Measure pulsing period", "Period reveals true brightness", "Compare with apparent brightness", "Distance"]}
### Why it mattered
Because all the stars in a Magellanic Cloud are about the same distance away, the pattern meant that a Cepheid's period reveals its true luminosity. Find a Cepheid anywhere, time its pulses, and you can calculate its distance.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before 1912", "items": ["distances only to nearby stars"]}, "r": {"t": "After Leavitt", "items": ["distances to other galaxies"]}}
### The Harvard computers
Dozens of women, including Annie Jump Cannon and Williamina Fleming, catalogued and classified hundreds of thousands of stars. Cannon devised the OBAFGKM star classes still used today.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Henrietta Leavitt", "b": "Cepheid distances"}, {"a": "Annie Jump Cannon", "b": "star classification"}, {"a": "Williamina Fleming", "b": "discovered the Horsehead Nebula"}, {"a": "Cecilia Payne", "b": "stars are mostly hydrogen"}]}
!? Leavitt died in 1921; a Swedish mathematician wanted to nominate her for a Nobel Prize in 1925, not knowing she'd already died.

# Hubble and the galaxies
In the early 1920s, astronomers debated whether "spiral nebulae" were clouds inside the Milky Way or separate "island universes". In 1923–24, Edwin Hubble found Cepheid stars in the Andromeda nebula using the 2.5 m Hooker telescope, showing it lay far beyond our galaxy.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1920", "l": "the Great Debate"}, {"y": "1923", "l": "Cepheid found in Andromeda"}, {"y": "1925", "l": "results announced"}, {"y": "1929", "l": "expansion of the universe"}]}
### The expanding universe
Hubble then compared galaxy distances with their redshifts, measured largely by Vesto Slipher and Milton Humason, and found that farther galaxies recede faster: the universe is expanding.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "v = H₀ × d", "items": [{"s": "v", "m": "recession speed"}, {"s": "H₀", "m": "Hubble constant"}, {"s": "d", "m": "distance"}]}
### Lemaître's credit
Belgian physicist Georges Lemaître published the expansion law in 1927, two years before Hubble. Since 2018 astronomers officially call it the Hubble–Lemaître law.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before", "items": ["one galaxy, static universe"]}, "r": {"t": "After", "items": ["billions of galaxies, expanding universe"]}}
!? The Hubble Space Telescope, launched 1990, was named in his honour; it measured the expansion rate to within a few per cent.

# Pedro Nunes
Pedro Nunes (1502–1578), royal cosmographer of Portugal and professor at Coimbra, was one of the great mathematicians of the age of discoveries. He applied mathematics to the practical problems of ocean navigation.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1502", "l": "born in Alcácer do Sal"}, {"y": "1529", "l": "royal cosmographer"}, {"y": "1537", "l": "Treatise of the Sphere"}, {"y": "1542", "l": "describes the nonius"}, {"y": "1578", "l": "dies in Coimbra"}]}
### The rhumb line
Nunes showed that a ship holding a constant compass bearing doesn't follow a great circle but spirals towards the pole: the loxodrome, or rhumb line. This insight later shaped the Mercator projection.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Great circle", "items": ["shortest route", "bearing changes"]}, "r": {"t": "Rhumb line", "items": ["constant bearing", "easier to steer", "spirals to the pole"]}}
### The nonius
To read angles more precisely on instruments like astrolabes, Nunes devised a set of concentric scales: the nonius. It inspired the vernier scale used on callipers today.
### Legacy
• A lunar crater, Nonius, is named after him
• A Portuguese research centre and schools carry his name
• He tutored future kings and navigators
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rhumb line", "b": "navigation insight"}, {"a": "Nonius", "b": "precise angle scale"}, {"a": "Twilight duration", "b": "astronomical calculations"}]}
> Nunes is a reminder that Portugal's voyages were also scientific achievements built on mathematics.

# Why the lunar south pole?
The Moon's axis is barely tilted (about 1.5°), so near the poles some crater floors never see sunlight. These "permanently shadowed regions" are among the coldest places in the Solar System, around −240 °C, cold enough to trap water ice for billions of years.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Comets and solar wind bring water", "Molecules hop across the surface", "Freeze in shadowed craters", "Ice accumulates"]}
### Evidence
• India's Chandrayaan-1 (2008) mapped water ice signatures with a NASA instrument
• NASA's LCROSS (2009) crashed into a crater and found water in the plume
• Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole in 2023
### Why it's valuable
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Water", "b": "drinking and growing food"}, {"a": "Oxygen", "b": "breathing"}, {"a": "Hydrogen plus oxygen", "b": "rocket propellant"}, {"a": "Ice", "b": "radiation shielding"}]}
### Light and power
Some ridges nearby are almost always sunlit, ideal for solar panels, right next to the dark ice-filled craters.

# Gateway
The Lunar Gateway is a planned small space station in a highly elliptical "near-rectilinear halo orbit" around the Moon. Much smaller than the ISS, it would host crews for short stays and serve as a staging point for landings.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Power and propulsion module", "Habitation modules", "Airlock", "Canadian robotic arm"]}
### Why that orbit?
The orbit swings close over the lunar south pole and far out over the north. It's stable, needs little fuel to maintain, keeps constant contact with Earth and is reachable from Earth and lunar landers.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "ISS", "items": ["low Earth orbit", "~420 t", "permanent crew"]}, "r": {"t": "Gateway", "items": ["lunar orbit", "much smaller", "visiting crews"]}}
### European role
ESA contributes habitation and refuelling modules and the ESPRIT communications module. In return, European astronauts are to fly to the Moon.
### Uncertain future
Gateway's schedule and scope have changed as budgets and priorities shift, and its future has been debated in US budget discussions.
> Space programmes depend on politics as much as engineering; plans often change.

# Mining asteroids
Some asteroids are rich in metals like iron, nickel, cobalt and platinum-group metals; others contain water and carbon compounds. In space, the most valuable resource may be water, for fuel and life support, rather than gold.

NASA's Psyche mission, launched in 2023, will arrive in 2029 at asteroid 16 Psyche, which may be the exposed metal core of a shattered protoplanet. Headlines valued its metal at quadrillions of dollars, but that number is meaningless: flooding the market would crash prices.

# Interstellar travel
Proxima Centauri is 4.2 light-years away: 40 trillion km. Voyager 1, moving at 17 km/s, would take about 75,000 years to cover that distance. Reaching another star within a human lifetime needs radically new propulsion.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 1.0, "hi": 100000.0, "unit": "years", "log": true, "items": [{"l": "Laser sail at 20% c", "v": 20.0}, {"l": "Nuclear pulse, theoretical", "v": 100.0}, {"l": "Voyager speed*", "v": 75000.0}]}
### Breakthrough Starshot
Announced in 2016, this concept would launch thousands of gram-scale chips on ultra-thin light sails, pushed by a huge ground-based laser array to about 20% of light speed. They'd reach Alpha Centauri in about 20 years and send back images.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Tiny chip on a light sail", "Giant laser array pushes", "20% of light speed", "Alpha Centauri in ~20 years"]}
### Huge challenges
• A laser array of about 100 gigawatts
• Sails surviving intense laser heating
• Surviving impacts with interstellar dust at 60,000 km/s
• Sending signals home from tiny transmitters
### Generation ships
Some imagine slow ships where generations live and die during a voyage of centuries. It raises deep questions about consent and survival.
!? A one-tonne probe at 20% of light speed would carry kinetic energy comparable to hundreds of nuclear weapons, which is why only tiny probes are proposed.

# Space telescopes to come
The next generation of telescopes aims to answer: are there other Earths with signs of life? The challenge is enormous: an Earth-like planet is about 10 billion times fainter than its star in visible light.

### Blocking the starlight
To see a faint planet next to a bright star, telescopes use a coronagraph inside or a "starshade" flying far in front, both designed to block the star's glare while letting the planet's light through.

### Biosignatures
Splitting a planet's light reveals its air. Oxygen, ozone, methane and water together, out of chemical balance, would be a strong hint of life.
