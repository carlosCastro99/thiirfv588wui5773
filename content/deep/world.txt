== wo-planet

# Why we have seasons
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4° relative to its path around the Sun, and it keeps pointing the same way in space all year (towards the Pole Star). So for half the year the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun, and for the other half it leans away.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "June (north)", "items": ["leans towards Sun", "Sun high, rays direct", "long days"]}, "r": {"t": "December (north)", "items": ["leans away", "Sun low, rays slanted", "short days"]}}
### Two effects that add up
• **Angle**: when the Sun is high, a beam of light falls on a small patch of ground and heats it strongly. When it's low, the same beam spreads over a bigger area.
• **Day length**: in summer the Sun is above the horizon longer, so the ground collects heat for more hours than it loses at night.
Distance hardly matters. Earth's orbit is almost a circle: we are about 147 million km from the Sun in early January and about 152 million km in early July, a difference of only about 3%.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "23.4°", "l": "tilt of Earth's axis"}, {"v": "147 M km", "l": "closest, early January"}, {"v": "152 M km", "l": "farthest, early July"}]}
### Opposite hemispheres
Because of the tilt, the hemispheres take turns. When Lisbon has summer, Rio de Janeiro and Maputo have winter, and Australians eat Christmas lunch on the beach. Near the equator the Sun is high all year, so there are wet and dry seasons rather than hot and cold ones.
!! Summer happens when Earth is closest to the Sun => Earth is closest in January; seasons come from the tilt
> Seasons are about the angle of sunlight and the length of the day, not distance from the Sun.

# Try it: time zones
The Sun is highest in the sky at noon, but "noon" happens at a different moment for every line of longitude. Before railways, each town kept its own **solar time**: Porto's clocks ran about 2 minutes ahead of Lisbon's. Fast trains and telegraphs made that chaotic, so in 1884 an international conference chose Greenwich in London as the zero line, and standard time zones followed.
= 360° ÷ 24 h = 15° per hour
ex: Tokyo is near 140° E | 140 ÷ 15 ≈ 9.3 hours | Japan uses UTC+9
### Clocks follow politics, not just the Sun
Zones bend around borders. China spans about four zones' worth of longitude but uses one time (UTC+8), so in western China the Sun can rise after 9 a.m. by the clock. India uses UTC+5:30, and Nepal even UTC+5:45.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lisbon (winter)", "b": "UTC+0"}, {"a": "Madrid", "b": "UTC+1, though west of Greenwich"}, {"a": "Azores (winter)", "b": "UTC−1"}, {"a": "India", "b": "UTC+5:30, a half-hour zone"}]}
### Portugal's odd neighbour
Spain lies mostly west of Greenwich, like Portugal, but it has used Central European Time since 1940. That is why Spanish clocks are an hour ahead of Portuguese ones and Spaniards eat dinner so "late" by the clock. Portugal itself tried Central European Time from 1992 to 1996 and went back, partly because winter mornings stayed dark until after 8:30.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earth turns 15° per hour", "Noon moves westward", "Zones round to borders", "Summer time adds 1 hour"]}
!? France has more time zones than any other country, about twelve, thanks to overseas territories from the Pacific to the Caribbean.
> A time zone is a deal between the Sun and politics: roughly 15° per hour, adjusted to suit countries.

# Latitude and longitude
Coordinates turn the round Earth into a grid. **Latitude** lines (parallels) run east–west and measure the angle north or south of the equator. **Longitude** lines (meridians) run from pole to pole and measure the angle east or west of Greenwich.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Latitude", "items": ["north–south position", "0° to 90°", "lines never meet"]}, "r": {"t": "Longitude", "items": ["east–west position", "0° to 180°", "lines meet at poles"]}}
### How big is a degree?
One degree of latitude is always about 111 km. A degree of longitude is 111 km at the equator but shrinks towards the poles, because the meridians converge. At Viana do Castelo's latitude it is only about 83 km.
ex: 1° of latitude ≈ 111 km | 1 minute (1/60°) ≈ 1.85 km | that's one nautical mile
### Why latitude was easy and longitude hard
Sailors could find latitude from the height of the Sun at noon or of the Pole Star at night, which Portuguese navigators did with astrolabes and quadrants in the 1400s. Longitude needs an accurate clock: you compare local noon with the time at home. Only in the 1760s did John Harrison's marine chronometer solve the problem at sea.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1400s", "l": "Portuguese use astrolabes"}, {"y": "1760s", "l": "Harrison's chronometer"}, {"y": "1884", "l": "Greenwich chosen as 0°"}, {"y": "1990s", "l": "GPS in everyday use"}]}
!? Your phone's GPS gives coordinates to about 5 decimal places of a degree, which pins you down to roughly a metre.
> Latitude tells you how far from the equator you are; longitude tells you how far round the world from Greenwich.

# The ocean's central heating
Porto (41° N) has January days around 14 °C; New York (41° N) averages around 0 °C and often sees snow. Both sit beside the same ocean, so why the difference? Two things work together: warm water and the direction of the wind.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "°C, average January temperature (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Porto", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "New York", "v": 1.0}]}
### The warm conveyor
The **Gulf Stream** carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico up the American coast and out across the Atlantic. Its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, brings that warmth towards Ireland, Britain and Norway. As the water cools and becomes salty and dense near Greenland, it sinks and flows back south at depth.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Warm water flows north", "Releases heat to air", "Cools and sinks", "Returns south at depth"]}
### Wind matters even more
At these latitudes the winds mostly blow from the west. Europe's west coast receives air that has crossed thousands of kilometres of relatively warm ocean. New York gets air from the frozen interior of North America. Scientists think this wind pattern explains much of the contrast, with the ocean current adding to it.
!! The Gulf Stream alone keeps Europe warm => Westerly winds off the ocean do much of the work too
!? Scientists are watching whether this Atlantic circulation is slowing as the Arctic warms; a big slowdown could bring colder winters to north-western Europe even in a warmer world.
> Western Europe is mild because westerly winds blow in off a warm ocean.

# Day and night
Earth turns on its axis from west to east, so the Sun, Moon and stars appear to move from east to west. One full turn relative to the Sun takes 24 hours: that's a **solar day**. Relative to the distant stars, a turn takes about 23 h 56 min, because Earth also moves along its orbit and must turn a little extra to face the Sun again.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "24 h", "l": "one turn relative to Sun"}, {"v": "1,670 km/h", "l": "spin speed at the equator"}, {"v": "~1,280 km/h", "l": "spin speed in Portugal"}]}
### Why you don't feel it
We spin at over 1,000 km/h, but smoothly and steadily, and the air turns with us. You only feel changes in speed, not speed itself, just as you can sleep on a smooth flight.
### Sunrise travels west
Japan is about 9 hours ahead of Portugal, so its sunrise happens while Portugal is still in the night before. The line between day and night, the **terminator**, sweeps westward across the globe at the speed of the spin.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earth spins west to east", "Sun seems to rise in east", "Sunrise sweeps westward", "Japan wakes before Lisbon"]}
!? Earth's spin is slowly getting slower, mainly because of the Moon's tides. About 400 million years ago a day lasted only around 22 hours.
!! The Sun moves across the sky => The Sun stays put; Earth turns beneath it
> Sunrise is not the Sun rising; it is your part of Earth turning to face it.

# The International Date Line
Imagine flying west around the world, setting your watch back an hour in each zone. After 24 zones you'd have lost a whole day. Somewhere the calendar has to jump to keep the count honest, and that place is the **International Date Line**, roughly along 180° longitude, opposite Greenwich.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Crossing westward", "items": ["add a day", "Mon becomes Tue"]}, "r": {"t": "Crossing eastward", "items": ["repeat a day", "Tue becomes Mon"]}}
### Why it zigzags
The line runs through the Pacific because few people live there, but it bends around island nations so that each one keeps a single date. In 2011 Samoa switched sides to share business days with Australia and New Zealand; it skipped Friday 30 December entirely.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1522", "l": "Magellan's crew lose a day"}, {"y": "1884", "l": "180° line proposed"}, {"y": "1995", "l": "Kiribati moves the line"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Samoa skips a Friday"}]}
### A famous puzzle
When the survivors of Magellan's expedition reached Cape Verde in 1522 after sailing west around the world, their careful log said Wednesday, but the Portuguese on the islands said Thursday. They had not miscounted: sailing west, they had turned one fewer time relative to the Sun.
!? For a few hours each day there are three different dates on Earth at once, because some islands use UTC+14 and others UTC−12.
> The date line is where the world agrees to restart the calendar day, bent to keep nations in one piece.

# Equinoxes and solstices
As Earth orbits, its tilt makes the Sun's highest point drift north and south over the year. The four turning points are named moments. A **solstice** ("Sun stands still") is when the Sun's noon height stops climbing or falling and turns back. An **equinox** ("equal night") is when the Sun is directly above the equator.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["March equinox", "June solstice", "September equinox", "December solstice"]}
### What happens at each
• June solstice: Sun overhead at the Tropic of Cancer (23.4° N); longest day in the north
• December solstice: Sun overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn (23.4° S); longest day in the south
• Equinoxes: Sun overhead at the equator; around 12 hours of day almost everywhere
The tropics and polar circles are defined by these extremes. The Arctic Circle (about 66.6° N) marks where the Sun doesn't set on the June solstice.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tropic of Cancer", "b": "23.4° N, overhead Sun in June"}, {"a": "Equator", "b": "0°, overhead Sun at equinoxes"}, {"a": "Tropic of Capricorn", "b": "23.4° S, overhead in December"}, {"a": "Arctic Circle", "b": "66.6° N, midnight Sun in June"}]}
!! Equinox means exactly 12 hours of day => Light bending in the air makes day a few minutes longer
!? Portugal's São João and Santo António festivals in June grew out of much older midsummer celebrations around the solstice.
> Solstices are the turning points of the Sun's yearly swing; equinoxes are the moments it crosses the equator.

# Daylight in Portugal
Day length depends on latitude and season. At the equator it stays near 12 hours all year. The further you go from the equator, the bigger the swing between summer and winter. Portugal, around 37–42° N, sits in the middle: a strong seasonal change, but nothing like Scandinavia's.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "hours of daylight, June solstice (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Quito (equator)", "v": 12.1}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 14.8, "hi": true}, {"l": "London", "v": 16.6}, {"l": "Reykjavík", "v": 21.0}]}
ex: Lisbon, June: ~14 h 50 min | Lisbon, December: ~9 h 30 min | difference ≈ 5 h 20 min
### Midnight sun and polar night
North of the Arctic Circle the Sun doesn't set for some days around midsummer. At Tromsø, in northern Norway, it stays up from about late May to late July, and in midwinter it doesn't rise for about seven weeks. At the poles there is one six-month day and one six-month night.
### Why summer evenings feel so long
In summer Portugal also moves its clocks forward an hour (UTC+1). Lisbon's sunset in late June comes after 21:00, so evenings stay light for outdoor dinners. Because of where Portugal sits in its time zone, winter sunrises come around 7:50.

> The further from the equator, the bigger the gap between summer and winter days.

# Earth's layers
Earth is about 12,740 km across, but nobody has drilled through more than about 12 km of it. Everything we know about the interior comes mostly from **seismic waves**: earthquake vibrations change speed and bend as they pass through different materials, and some kinds can't cross liquids at all.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Crust: 5–70 km, rock", "Mantle: to 2,900 km, hot rock", "Outer core: liquid iron", "Inner core: solid iron, ~5,400 °C"]}
### Layer by layer
• **Crust**: thin and rocky, about 5–10 km under oceans and 30–70 km under continents. Like the skin of an apple.
• **Mantle**: solid rock, but so hot that over millions of years it flows slowly, like very stiff toffee. This movement drives plate tectonics.
• **Outer core**: liquid iron and nickel. Its swirling creates Earth's magnetic field, which shields us from the solar wind and makes compasses work.
• **Inner core**: solid despite the heat, because the pressure is enormous.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~12 km", "l": "deepest hole ever drilled"}, {"v": "6,371 km", "l": "distance to Earth's centre"}, {"v": "~5,400 °C", "l": "inner core temperature"}]}
!? The Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia reached about 12.2 km in 1989. That's less than 0.2% of the way to the centre.
!! The mantle is a sea of lava => It's mostly solid rock that creeps very slowly
> We know Earth's deep interior mostly by listening to earthquakes echo through it.

# Plate tectonics
The crust and the top of the mantle form a rigid shell, the **lithosphere**, broken into about 15 major plates and many smaller ones. They float on softer mantle below and drift a few centimetres a year, pushed and pulled by the slow churn of the mantle and by old, heavy plate edges sinking.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Plate boundaries", "items": ["Apart: new ocean floor", "Together: mountains, trenches", "Sideways: earthquake faults"]}
### What happens at the edges
Where plates pull apart, as along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, magma rises and makes new sea floor. Where they collide, one may dive under the other, making deep trenches and volcanoes, or both crumple up into mountains. Where they slide past, stress builds and releases in earthquakes, as on California's San Andreas Fault.
### Portugal's place
Portugal sits near the boundary between the Eurasian and African plates, which runs west through the Azores and the Gulf of Cádiz. Movement there caused the great **Lisbon earthquake of 1755**, which, with the tsunami and fires that followed, destroyed much of the city and killed tens of thousands of people.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "cm per year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Fingernail growth", "v": 3.5}, {"l": "Atlantic widening", "v": 2.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Fastest Pacific plates", "v": 10.0}]}
!? The idea of drifting continents was proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912 and widely mocked; it was only accepted in the 1960s, once sea-floor maps showed spreading ridges.
> Earth's surface is a slow-moving jigsaw, and the edges of the pieces are where the action is.

# Why cities sit on rivers and coasts
For most of history, moving heavy goods by land was slow and expensive. A horse-drawn cart might carry a tonne; a river barge could carry dozens of tonnes with the same effort. Water was the cheapest motorway, so towns grew where water routes met.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Drinking water and irrigation", "Fertile soil from floods", "Fish and food", "Cheap transport for trade", "Defence behind water"]}
### The best spots
The most valuable places combined a sheltered harbour for sea ships with a river reaching inland. Lisbon sits on the wide Tagus estuary, one of Europe's finest natural harbours. Porto grew where the Douro could be crossed and where boats could bring wine down from the valley. London grew at the first point where the Thames could be bridged.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lisbon", "b": "Tagus estuary"}, {"a": "Porto", "b": "Douro, near the mouth"}, {"a": "Shanghai", "b": "mouth of the Yangtze"}, {"a": "Cairo", "b": "where the Nile delta begins"}]}
### Still on the water
Railways and roads changed the rules, but the old cities stayed where they were, and trade still follows water. About 80% of world trade by volume still travels by sea. The world's busiest ports, like Shanghai, Singapore and Rotterdam, sit on coasts or river mouths.
!? Roughly 40% of humanity lives within about 100 km of a coast, which makes coastal cities especially exposed to sea-level rise.
> Cities grew where water offered food, safety and above all cheap transport.

# How many countries are there?
"How many countries?" sounds like a simple question, but it depends on who recognises whom. The most common answer, 195, counts the 193 UN member states plus the two observer states. Other lists give anything from about 190 to over 200.

# Population growth
For most of history, human numbers grew very slowly because many babies and children died. Then, from the 1800s, cleaner water, vaccines, better farming and modern medicine cut death rates sharply. Births stayed high for a while, so population shot up.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1804", "l": "1 billion"}, {"y": "1927", "l": "2 billion"}, {"y": "1974", "l": "4 billion"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "8 billion"}]}
### The demographic transition
Demographers describe a pattern seen in country after country. First deaths fall, then, a generation or two later, births fall too, as children survive, women gain education and work, and cities grow. Portugal went through this in the 20th century; many African countries are in the middle of it now.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["High births, high deaths", "Deaths fall: rapid growth", "Births fall too", "Low births, low deaths"]}
### Slowing down
Growth peaked at about 2% a year in the late 1960s and is now under 1%. The UN expects the world to peak at around 10.3 billion in the 2080s and then slowly decline. The speed at which billions were added shows the change: the jump from 7 to 8 billion took about 12 years.
ex: 1 → 2 billion: ~123 years | 3 → 4 billion: ~14 years | 7 → 8 billion: ~12 years
!! Population will keep exploding forever => Growth is already slowing and should peak this century
> Population boomed when death rates fell before birth rates did.

# The most populous countries
For centuries China was the world's most populous country. In 2023, according to UN estimates, India overtook it. China's population is now shrinking, while India's is still growing, so the gap will widen.

### Why China stopped growing
China's one-child policy (1980–2015) and rapid urbanisation pushed birth rates very low. Today Chinese women have on average around one child each, well below the 2.1 needed to keep a population stable without migration. India's rate has also fallen, to about 2.
### Who's coming up
Nigeria, already over 220 million, is growing fast and may pass the USA later this century. Pakistan, Ethiopia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo are also climbing the table. Europe's largest country, Russia, is around ninth, and Germany is around 19th.

# Ageing populations
A population ages when fewer babies are born and people live longer. Both are signs of success, better health and more choice for women, but they change the shape of society. The key number is the **fertility rate**: about 2.1 children per woman keeps a population stable.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "children per woman (approx., 2020s)", "items": [{"l": "South Korea", "v": 0.75}, {"l": "Italy", "v": 1.2}, {"l": "Portugal", "v": 1.4, "hi": true}, {"l": "World", "v": 2.3}, {"l": "Niger", "v": 6.0}]}
### Portugal's case
Portugal's fertility rate has been below replacement since the early 1980s. About a quarter of residents are 65 or older, one of the highest shares in the EU. Many young people have also emigrated, which speeds up ageing in the regions they leave.
### What it means
• Fewer workers per pensioner, putting pressure on pensions and health care
• Schools closing in villages while care homes fill up
• More demand for nurses, carers and accessible housing
• Immigration becomes important to fill jobs

!? Japan sells more adult nappies than baby nappies, a vivid sign of an ageing society.
> Ageing is the price of long lives and small families, and it reshapes everything from pensions to schools.

# Life expectancy
**Life expectancy at birth** is the average number of years a newborn would live if today's death rates stayed the same. It was low in 1900 mainly because so many children died young, not because adults rarely reached old age. Someone who survived childhood could often live to 60 or more.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "years, world average (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1900", "v": 32.0}, {"l": "1950", "v": 46.0}, {"l": "2000", "v": 67.0}, {"l": "2023", "v": 73.0, "hi": true}]}
### What drove the rise
• Clean water and sewers stopped cholera and typhoid
• Vaccines against smallpox, measles and polio
• Antibiotics from the 1940s
• Better food and fewer famines
• More recently, treatment of heart disease and cancer
### Big gaps remain
Japan and Switzerland reach around 84 years; some countries in Africa remain in the low 60s. Portugal is around 81–82, with women living about five to six years longer than men. Its infant mortality fell from among Western Europe's highest in the 1960s to among the lowest today.

# Migration
People move for work, safety, study, family or love. The UN counts about 280 million **international migrants**, people living outside their country of birth. That's a small share of humanity, but they matter hugely to economies through work and the money they send home.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Why people move", "items": ["Work and higher wages", "War or persecution", "Family and study", "Climate and disasters"]}
### Portugal: from leaving to arriving
For centuries Portugal sent people abroad, to Brazil, then in the 1960s to France, Germany and Luxembourg, often escaping poverty and the dictatorship's colonial wars. Over a million left in that decade. Since the 2010s, and especially the 2020s, Portugal has received many immigrants, notably from Brazil, India, Nepal, Cape Verde and Angola, plus retirees and remote workers from across Europe.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1500s", "l": "Settlers to Brazil"}, {"y": "1960s", "l": "Mass move to France"}, {"y": "2011–14", "l": "Crisis emigration"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "Immigration boom"}]}
### Money sent home
**Remittances**, money migrants send home, add up to more than all foreign aid combined. For some countries, like Cape Verde, they are a major part of the economy.
!? Around 1.5–2 million people born in Portugal live abroad, one of the largest diasporas in Europe relative to population.
> Migration is small as a share of humanity but huge in its effect on the countries people leave and join.

# Portuguese around the world
Portuguese spread with sailors, traders and colonists from the 1400s onwards. Today it's spoken by around 260 million people and is official in nine countries, linked through the **CPLP**, the Community of Portuguese Language Countries, founded in 1996.

== wo-continents

# Seven continents
A continent is a big landmass, but there's no strict scientific rule for where one ends and another begins. The list we learn is partly geology and partly tradition, which is why different countries teach different numbers.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "million km² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Asia", "v": 44.6, "hi": true}, {"l": "Africa", "v": 30.4}, {"l": "North America", "v": 24.2}, {"l": "South America", "v": 17.8}, {"l": "Europe", "v": 10.2}]}
### Not everyone counts seven
• In Portugal, Spain and much of Latin America, schools often teach the Americas as one continent.
• Some geographers merge Europe and Asia into **Eurasia**, since no sea separates them.
• The Olympic rings stand for five inhabited continents: Africa, the Americas, Asia, Europe and Oceania.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "7 continents", "b": "UK, USA, China, Australia"}, {"a": "6 (one America)", "b": "Portugal, Spain, Latin America"}, {"a": "5 (Olympic rings)", "b": "inhabited continents only"}]}
### Size and people don't match
Antarctica (about 14 million km²) is bigger than Europe or Australia, yet has no permanent population. Australia is the smallest continent at about 8.6 million km². Asia is both the largest and the most populous, with about 60% of humanity on 30% of the land.
!? Geologists have identified a mostly sunken continent called Zealandia around New Zealand; about 94% of it lies under the sea.
!! There is one official list of continents => How many you count depends on convention
> Continents are partly nature and partly agreement, so the number depends on who you ask.

# Five oceans
All the oceans are connected into one World Ocean, but we divide it into five basins. The **Southern Ocean**, around Antarctica, was the last to gain wide recognition: the International Hydrographic Organization proposed it in 2000, and National Geographic added it to its maps in 2021.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "million km² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Pacific", "v": 165.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Atlantic", "v": 85.0}, {"l": "Indian", "v": 70.0}, {"l": "Southern", "v": 22.0}, {"l": "Arctic", "v": 15.0}]}
### The giant Pacific
The Pacific covers about a third of Earth's surface, more than all the continents together (about 149 million km²). It is also the deepest ocean, with an average depth of about 4 km, and is ringed by volcanoes and earthquake zones, the "Ring of Fire".
### Portugal's ocean
The Atlantic is the youngest of the big oceans, opening about 180 million years ago as Pangaea split. It is still widening by a few centimetres a year. Portugal's sea zone, including the waters of the Azores and Madeira, is one of the largest in the EU, about 1.7 million km², around 18 times the country's land area.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Pacific", "b": "largest, deepest, Ring of Fire"}, {"a": "Atlantic", "b": "S-shaped, still widening"}, {"a": "Southern", "b": "circles Antarctica"}, {"a": "Arctic", "b": "smallest, ice-covered in winter"}]}
!? Magellan named the Pacific ("peaceful") because his crossing in 1520–21 met calm weather, despite its fierce storms elsewhere.
> The five oceans are really one connected World Ocean, with the Pacific alone bigger than all the land.

# Mostly water
From space Earth is blue, because about 71% of its surface is ocean. Yet water is a thin film: all of it together would form a ball only about 1,400 km across, smaller than the Moon.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of all water on Earth", "items": [{"l": "Oceans (salt)", "v": 97.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Ice and snow", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "Groundwater", "v": 0.8}, {"l": "Lakes and rivers", "v": 0.01, "t": "<0.1"}]}
### Where the fresh water is
Only about 3% of Earth's water is fresh. Most of that is frozen in the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland. Much of the rest is groundwater, some of it too deep to pump. The lakes, rivers and soil moisture we can easily use are a tiny fraction.
ex: All water = 100% | Fresh water ≈ 3% | Easily reached (lakes, rivers) ≈ well under 1%
### Why water is renewable, but not unlimited
The water cycle constantly purifies water: the Sun evaporates sea water, leaving the salt behind, and it falls again as rain. The problem isn't running out of water globally but having enough in the right place at the right time. Southern Portugal and Spain face more frequent droughts, and the Algarve is turning to desalination to supplement supply.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Sea water evaporates", "Clouds form", "Rain and snow fall", "Rivers return to sea"]}
!! Earth has plenty of drinkable water => Most is salty or frozen; usable fresh water is scarce
> The blue planet is mostly salty: usable fresh water is a tiny, precious sliver.

# The deepest point
The Mariana Trench is a curved gash in the western Pacific, about 2,500 km long, near Guam. It forms where the old, heavy Pacific Plate dives beneath the smaller Mariana Plate. Its deepest known spot, the **Challenger Deep**, lies about 10,900–11,000 m below the surface.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Everest", "items": ["8,849 m above sea", "on land, Himalaya"]}, "r": {"t": "Challenger Deep", "items": ["~10,935 m below sea", "Pacific trench"]}}
### Crushing pressure
Every 10 m of water adds about one atmosphere of pressure. At the bottom it's over 1,000 times the pressure at the surface, roughly the weight of an elephant pressing on every postage stamp of surface. It's also pitch dark and close to freezing. Yet animals live there, including tiny shrimp-like amphipods.
ex: 10 m of water ≈ 1 atmosphere | 10,900 m ÷ 10 ≈ 1,090 atmospheres | about 1,100 times surface pressure
### Who's been there

!? More people have walked on the Moon (12) than had reached the Challenger Deep before 2019 (3).
> The ocean's deepest point is deeper than the tallest mountain is high, by about 2 km.

# Pangaea
Look at a map and the bulge of Brazil seems to slot into the Gulf of Guinea. That's no coincidence. About 300 million years ago almost all land was joined in one supercontinent, **Pangaea** ("all land"), surrounded by a single ocean, Panthalassa.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~300 Ma", "l": "Pangaea assembles"}, {"y": "~200 Ma", "l": "Begins splitting"}, {"y": "~130 Ma", "l": "South Atlantic opens"}, {"y": "Today", "l": "7 continents"}]}
### The evidence
Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist, argued in 1912 that the continents had drifted apart. His clues still hold:
• Coastlines that fit together, especially at the edge of the continental shelf
• The same fossils, like the reptile *Mesosaurus*, in Brazil and southern Africa
• Matching rock layers and old mountain belts across oceans
• Traces of ancient glaciers in today's tropical lands
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Coastlines fit like a puzzle", "Same fossils on two continents", "Matching rocks across oceans", "Glacier scars in the tropics"]}
### Supercontinents come and go
Pangaea wasn't the first. Earth seems to assemble and break up supercontinents every few hundred million years. Geologists predict another in perhaps 200–250 million years; one model, "Pangaea Proxima", closes the Atlantic, which would push Portugal against America again.
!! Continents have always been where they are => They drift a few cm a year and have moved thousands of km
> The world map is a snapshot of a slow dance; 300 million years ago the continents were one.

# Where does Europe end?
Physically, Europe is a western peninsula of the huge Eurasian landmass. Its eastern border is a cultural agreement, not a coastline. The line most geographers use was drawn in the 1700s by the Swedish officer Philip Johan von Strahlenberg and later adjusted.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ural Mountains", "Ural River", "Caspian Sea", "Caucasus", "Black Sea, Bosporus"]}
### Countries on both sides
• **Russia**: about 75% of its land is in Asia, but about 75% of its people live in the European part.
• **Turkey**: Istanbul straddles the Bosporus; most of the country, Anatolia, is in Asia.
• **Kazakhstan**: a small part lies west of the Ural River.
• Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan sit on the disputed Caucasus border.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Russia's land", "items": ["~75% in Asia", "Siberia"]}, "r": {"t": "Russia's people", "items": ["~75% in Europe", "Moscow, St Petersburg"]}}
### Culture versus maps
Membership of European clubs adds to the blur. Cyprus is geographically in Asia but is an EU member. Israel, Azerbaijan and Australia compete in the Eurovision Song Contest. Football's UEFA includes Kazakhstan and Israel.
!? Istanbul is the largest city in Europe if you count its whole population, with more than 15 million people, ahead of Moscow and London.
> Europe's eastern edge is a line people agreed on, not one nature drew.

# Islands
An island is land surrounded by water and smaller than a continent. Australia is counted as a continent, which makes **Greenland**, at about 2.2 million km², the world's largest island. It's about 24 times the size of Portugal, but most of it is under an ice sheet up to 3 km thick, and only about 57,000 people live there.

### How islands form
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Island origins", "items": ["Volcanoes: Azores, Hawaii", "Broken from continents", "Coral reefs: Maldives", "Hills cut off by sea rise"]}
### Portugal's volcanic islands
Madeira rose from a volcanic **hotspot**, a plume of hot mantle rock beneath the African Plate. The Azores are more unusual: they sit at the **triple junction** where the North American, Eurasian and African (Nubian) plates meet, astride the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The islands are still active: Capelinhos on Faial erupted in 1957–58 and added new land, and earthquakes are frequent.
!? Flores and Corvo, the westernmost Azores islands, sit on the North American Plate, while the others lie on the Eurasian and African side: two continents' plates in one Portuguese region.
> Portugal's Atlantic islands are volcanoes, the Azores sitting right where three plates meet.

# Ocean currents
Surface currents are driven mostly by steady winds, steered by Earth's rotation and the shape of the continents. In each big ocean they form giant loops called **gyres**: clockwise in the northern hemisphere, anticlockwise in the southern.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Gulf Stream heads NE", "North Atlantic Drift", "Canary Current flows S", "Trade winds push W"]}
### Warm and cold currents
Currents flowing away from the equator, like the Gulf Stream, carry warm water to cooler places. Those flowing towards the equator, like the **Canary Current** off Portugal, bring cool water south. That's why the sea at Viana do Castelo is often only around 17–19 °C in August, while the Mediterranean at the same latitude can reach 25 °C.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Warm currents", "items": ["flow from equator", "Gulf Stream", "milder coasts"]}, "r": {"t": "Cold currents", "items": ["flow towards equator", "Canary Current", "cool seas, fog"]}}
### Upwelling: good for fish
In summer, northerly winds along Portugal's coast push surface water offshore, and cold, nutrient-rich water rises from below. This **upwelling** feeds plankton and makes Portuguese waters rich in sardines, one reason the sardine is the star of the Santo António festivals.
!? In 1992, a container of rubber ducks fell into the Pacific; the toys drifted for years and helped scientists track currents around the world.
> Currents move heat around the planet: warm ones soften winters, cold ones cool summer seas.

# Seas and straits
A **strait** is a narrow channel between two bodies of water; a **canal** is one built by people. These chokepoints concentrate the world's shipping, so a blockage or conflict at one can raise prices worldwide within days.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Gibraltar", "b": "Atlantic ↔ Mediterranean"}, {"a": "Suez Canal", "b": "Mediterranean ↔ Red Sea"}, {"a": "Hormuz", "b": "Gulf ↔ Arabian Sea, oil route"}, {"a": "Malacca", "b": "Indian Ocean ↔ Pacific"}]}
### Why they matter
• About a fifth of the world's oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz.
• The Strait of Malacca carries roughly a quarter of world trade by sea, between China, India and the Middle East.
• The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, saves ships about 7,000 km between Europe and Asia.
• Ships too big for canals must go around the Cape of Good Hope, as the Portuguese first did in 1488.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1488", "l": "Dias rounds the Cape"}, {"y": "1869", "l": "Suez Canal opens"}, {"y": "1914", "l": "Panama Canal opens"}, {"y": "2021", "l": "Ever Given blocks Suez"}]}
### A lesson from 2021
In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given got stuck across the Suez Canal for six days. The canal normally carries around 12% of world trade, and hundreds of ships queued at each end. Attacks on Red Sea shipping from late 2023 again sent many ships the long way around Africa.
> A few narrow channels carry a huge share of world trade, so trouble there is felt everywhere.

# Antarctica
Antarctica is about 14 million km², about one and a half times the size of the USA. Almost all of it lies under an ice sheet averaging about 2 km thick, up to about 4.8 km. If all that ice melted, sea levels would rise by around 58 m.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "−89.2 °C", "l": "coldest air measured, 1983"}, {"v": "~70%", "l": "of world's fresh water"}, {"v": "~58 m", "l": "sea rise if all ice melted"}]}
### Cold, dry, windy
The interior gets so little snow that it counts as a desert. Cold, dense air pours down the slopes towards the coast as **katabatic winds**, which can exceed 200 km/h. No humans lived there until scientific bases arrived; today a few thousand researchers stay in summer, around a thousand in winter.
### A continent for science
The **Antarctic Treaty**, signed in 1959 by 12 countries and now joined by over 50 (including Portugal), bans military activity and mining, freezes territorial claims and keeps the continent for peaceful research. Seven countries have claims, some overlapping, but these are on hold.

!! The North and South Poles are alike => The Arctic is frozen ocean; Antarctica is a continent under ice
> Antarctica is a frozen desert holding most of the world's fresh water, shared by treaty for science.

== wo-climate

# Climate vs weather
**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a given moment: today's rain in Braga or a heatwave in Évora. **Climate** is the long-term pattern, usually averaged over 30 years: how warm, wet and windy a place tends to be, including how often extremes occur.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Weather", "items": ["hours to days", "today's forecast", "very variable"]}, "r": {"t": "Climate", "items": ["30-year averages", "what to expect", "changes slowly"]}}
### Why forecasts fade but climate can be predicted
Weather is chaotic: tiny differences grow, so detailed forecasts lose skill after about a week to ten days. Climate is about averages, which are governed by bigger things like sunlight, oceans and greenhouse gases. You can't say whether 15 August 2040 will be sunny in Lisbon, but you can say August will very likely be hot and dry.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Weather", "b": "rain in Porto tomorrow"}, {"a": "Season", "b": "wet winters, dry summers"}, {"a": "Climate", "b": "1991–2020 average"}, {"a": "Climate change", "b": "averages shifting over decades"}]}
### One cold day proves nothing
A snowy week doesn't disprove global warming, just as one hot day doesn't prove it. Climate change shows up in the trend: Portugal's average temperature has risen by well over 1 °C in the last century, and very hot days are now more frequent.
!! A cold winter means the climate isn't warming => Climate is the long-term trend, not one season
> Weather is your mood today; climate is your personality.

# Latitude and sunlight
The single biggest control on climate is how much sunlight a place gets, and that depends on latitude. Near the equator the midday Sun is nearly overhead all year. Near the poles it skims the horizon and disappears for months.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Same beam of sunlight", "High Sun: small patch", "Low Sun: spread wide", "Less heat per m²"]}
### Two extra penalties for the poles
• **Spreading**: a beam hitting at a low angle covers a larger area, so each square metre gets less energy.
• **More air**: slanted light passes through much more atmosphere, losing more energy along the way.
• **Bright ice**: snow reflects up to 80–90% of sunlight back to space, keeping cold places cold.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": -10.0, "hi": 30.0, "unit": "°C, yearly average", "items": [{"l": "Svalbard", "v": -4.0}, {"l": "Oslo", "v": 6.5}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 17.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Singapore", "v": 28.0}]}
### Heat on the move
If each latitude kept its own sunlight, the tropics would be even hotter and the poles even colder. Winds and ocean currents carry heat poleward, smoothing the difference, and they also create local exceptions, which is why Lisbon is milder than places further inland at the same latitude.
!? The equator gets roughly two and a half times as much yearly sunshine energy as the poles.
> The lower the Sun, the more its heat is spread thin: that's why the poles are cold.

# Climate zones
In 1884 the German-Russian scientist Wladimir **Köppen** devised a system that still underpins climate maps. He noticed that plants reflect climate, so he classified places by temperature and rainfall patterns using letter codes.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "A – Tropical", "b": "hot all year"}, {"a": "B – Dry", "b": "deserts and steppe"}, {"a": "C – Temperate", "b": "mild winters (Portugal)"}, {"a": "D – Continental", "b": "cold winters, warm summers"}]}
### Reading the code
The fifth main group is **E – Polar**. Extra letters add detail: "s" means dry summer, "w" dry winter, "f" no dry season. Then "a" means hot summers and "b" warm ones. So Lisbon's **Csa** reads: temperate, dry summer, hot summer. Porto and Viana do Castelo are **Csb**: dry, warm (not hot) summer.
ex: C = temperate | s = dry summer | a = hot summer → Csa (Lisbon, Seville, Athens)
### Mediterranean climates worldwide
This climate type is rare, covering only a few percent of land. It occurs on west coasts around 30–45° latitude: the Mediterranean basin, California, central Chile, the Cape in South Africa and parts of southern Australia. All are famous for wine, olives and wildfire risk.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Mediterranean basin", "California", "Central Chile", "Cape region, South Africa", "Southwest Australia"]}
!? Some parts of the far southeast of Portugal are now close to the boundary of a semi-arid (BSk) climate.
> A Köppen code compresses a climate into letters: Portugal is mostly Csa and Csb.

# Deserts
A desert is defined by dryness, not heat: less than about 250 mm of precipitation a year, and often more evaporation than rain. For comparison, Lisbon gets around 700 mm and parts of the Minho over 1,500 mm.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "million km² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Antarctica", "v": 14.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Arctic", "v": 13.9}, {"l": "Sahara", "v": 9.2}, {"l": "Arabian", "v": 2.3}, {"l": "Gobi", "v": 1.3}]}
### Why deserts form where they do
• **Sinking air** around 30° latitude: air that rose and rained over the equator descends, warms and dries out (Sahara, Arabian, Australian deserts).
• **Rain shadows** behind mountains (the Gobi, Patagonia).
• **Cold coastal currents** that chill the air so it can't rise and rain (the Atacama, the Namib).
• **Distance from the sea** in continental interiors.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Hot equator air rises", "Rains over tropics", "Dry air flows poleward", "Sinks near 30°: desert"]}
### Not just sand
Only about a fifth of the Sahara is sand dunes; much of it is rock and gravel. Deserts can freeze at night because dry air holds little heat. The Atacama in Chile is the driest non-polar place on Earth: some weather stations there have recorded almost no rain for decades.
!! Deserts are always hot and sandy => Antarctica is a desert, and most deserts are rock, not dunes
> A desert is any place that hardly gets rain, which makes icy Antarctica the biggest of all.

# Rainforests
Tropical rainforests grow within about 10° of the equator, where it's warm (around 25–28 °C) and wet all year, with typically 2,000 mm of rain or more. Rising hot, moist air forms clouds and afternoon downpours almost daily.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Emergents: giant trees", "Canopy: the leafy roof", "Understorey: dim, humid", "Forest floor: little light"]}
### Why so many species?
Steady warmth and water mean plants grow all year, and the forest's layers create many different homes. A single hectare of Amazon can hold more tree species than all of Europe. Rainforests probably host over half of all land species, many still undescribed.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "million km² of tropical rainforest (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Amazon", "v": 5.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Congo Basin", "v": 2.0}, {"l": "New Guinea & SE Asia", "v": 2.0}]}
### Rich forest, poor soil
Surprisingly, rainforest soil is often thin and poor. Nutrients sit in the living plants and are recycled fast by fungi and insects. Once trees are cleared for cattle or soy, the soil is quickly exhausted. Deforestation of the Amazon peaked in the early 2000s, fell, rose again in the late 2010s and fell sharply from 2023.
!! The Amazon is the lungs of the world, supplying our oxygen => It uses about as much oxygen as it makes; its value is carbon, rain and species
> Rainforests are thin strips of the planet holding over half its land life.

# Biomes
A **biome** is a big community of plants and animals shaped by a similar climate. Unrelated places can look alike: the Portuguese montado, California's chaparral and Chile's matorral all have tough, evergreen shrubs because they share hot, dry summers.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Tundra: frozen ground, mosses", "Taiga: conifers, long winters", "Temperate forest: oak, beech", "Savanna: grass, scattered trees", "Desert: cacti, succulents"]}
### Temperature plus rain
Two numbers predict a biome surprisingly well: average temperature and yearly rainfall. Hot and wet gives rainforest; hot with a long dry season gives savanna; cold and dry gives tundra. Altitude repeats the pattern: climb a high mountain and you pass through forests, then alpine meadows, then bare rock and snow.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Taiga", "items": ["largest land biome", "conifers, cold", "Russia, Canada"]}, "r": {"t": "Mediterranean", "items": ["small, rich in species", "drought-hardy shrubs", "Portugal, California"]}}
### Portugal's biomes
Portugal sits where Atlantic temperate forest (oaks and chestnuts in the north) meets Mediterranean woodland and scrub (cork and holm oak, rockrose, rosemary in the south). The **montado**, a cork oak savanna grazed by pigs, sheep and cattle, is a human-shaped landscape that supports rich wildlife and about half the world's cork.
!? The Mediterranean basin is one of the world's biodiversity hotspots, with around 25,000 plant species, about half found nowhere else.
> Similar climates produce similar-looking landscapes, even on opposite sides of the world.

# Mountains change climate
As air rises it expands, because there's less air pressing down from above, and expanding air cools. On average temperature falls about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m you climb. That's why the summit of Kilimanjaro, almost on the equator, has glaciers.
ex: Lisbon (sea level): yearly average ~17 °C | Torre, Serra da Estrela: ~2,000 m up | 17 − 2 × 6.5 ≈ 4 °C
### The rain shadow
When moist wind meets a mountain, it's forced up. It cools, its moisture condenses, and it rains on the windward side. Coming down the far side, the now-dry air warms and gives little rain.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Moist sea wind arrives", "Forced up the slope", "Cools, rain falls", "Dry air sinks beyond"]}
### Examples close to home
The Gerês and Peneda mountains catch Atlantic storms and are among the wettest places in Portugal, with over 3,000 mm in places. Just inland, behind them, parts of Trás-os-Montes and the Douro valley are far drier and hotter in summer, perfect for port wine grapes.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mm of rain per year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Serra do Gerês", "v": 3000.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Braga", "v": 1450.0}, {"l": "Pinhão (Douro)", "v": 650.0}]}
!? Funchal and the high peaks of Madeira are only about 15 km apart, but the peaks can be frosty while the capital stays above 15 °C.
> Climb 1,000 m and you lose about 6.5 °C; cross a mountain and you may lose the rain too.

# Monsoons
"Monsoon" comes from the Arabic *mawsim*, meaning season. It describes winds that reverse with the seasons, bringing a wet season and a dry season. Portuguese sailors learned to ride these winds: they sailed to India in summer and returned in winter.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Summer", "items": ["land heats fast", "moist wind from ocean", "heavy rain"]}, "r": {"t": "Winter", "items": ["land cools", "dry wind from land", "little rain"]}}
### How it works
Land heats up faster than sea. In summer the Indian subcontinent becomes very hot, air rises over it, and moist air is drawn in from the Indian Ocean. Rising over land and the Himalaya, it pours down as rain. In winter the pattern reverses.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Land heats in summer", "Air rises over land", "Wet ocean wind pulled in", "Rain; winter reverses"]}
### Life and death
India gets about three-quarters of its yearly rain in the June–September monsoon. A late or weak monsoon can mean poor harvests; an extreme one brings floods. Cherrapunji and Mawsynram in north-east India are among the wettest places on Earth, with over 11,000 mm a year.
!? The monsoon's arrival on the Kerala coast, around 1 June, is so important that it is tracked by Indian news like a national event.
> A monsoon is a seasonal wind reversal: land heats, sucks in ocean air, and it rains for months.

# Portugal's contrasts
Portugal is small, but its climate changes sharply over short distances. Two factors drive it: distance from the Atlantic, and mountains that catch rain in the north-west.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "mm of rain per year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Braga", "v": 1450.0}, {"l": "Porto", "v": 1200.0}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 730.0}, {"l": "Faro", "v": 480.0}, {"l": "Beja", "v": 550.0}]}
### The Atlantic north-west
The Minho faces the westerly storm track, and the land rises quickly inland, squeezing rain out of the clouds. Winters are mild and wet, summers warm and fresh. That's why the landscape is green and why vinho verde grapes are grown high on pergolas.
### The continental interior
Far from the sea, the Alentejo and the Beira Interior heat up and cool down quickly. Summer afternoons regularly pass 40 °C. Portugal's record, 47.3 °C, was set at Amareleja (Alentejo) in 2003. Winter nights in Trás-os-Montes or Guarda can drop below −5 °C.

!? Some years, Portugal sees snowfall in the north on the same day sunbathers are on the Algarve's beaches.
> Portugal's climate runs from Atlantic green in the north-west to near-desert heat in the south-east.

# Microclimates
A **microclimate** is a small area whose climate differs from its surroundings: a city centre, a valley floor, a south-facing wall. Differences of a few degrees can decide whether lemons survive winter or vines ripen.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Microclimates", "items": ["Cities: heat islands", "Valleys: frost pockets", "Coasts: mild and foggy", "Slopes: sunny or shady"]}
### Urban heat islands
Asphalt and concrete absorb sunlight by day and release it at night, while buildings block cooling winds and there are few trees to give shade and evaporate water. City centres can be several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside on summer nights. Trees, parks and lighter-coloured roofs help cool them.
### Cold air drains downhill
On clear, calm nights the ground loses heat and the air above it cools. Cold air is dense, so it slides downhill and pools in valleys. That's why frost often forms on valley floors while the slopes stay milder, and why farmers plant sensitive crops on hillsides.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clear, calm night", "Ground and air cool", "Cold air flows downhill", "Frost pools in valley"]}
### Terraces and walls
The Douro's terraced vineyards exploit sun-baked schist slopes. Old walled gardens and south-facing walls store warmth, letting gardeners in cooler places grow figs and citrus.
!? In the Douro, vineyard plots are officially graded from A to F, partly on altitude, slope and how much sun they face.
> Small differences in surface, slope and shelter create their own little climates.

# The longest rivers
Measuring a river sounds simple, but it isn't. Where exactly is the source: the farthest trickle, or the main spring? Where does it end in a wide, braided delta or estuary? And do you follow every bend? Different choices change the answer by hundreds of kilometres.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "km (approx., usual figures)", "items": [{"l": "Nile", "v": 6650.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Amazon", "v": 6400.0}, {"l": "Yangtze", "v": 6300.0}, {"l": "Mississippi-Missouri", "v": 6275.0}, {"l": "Tagus", "v": 1007.0}]}
### Nile or Amazon?
Most reference books give the title to the Nile, at about 6,650 km. But some Brazilian and Peruvian studies trace the Amazon from a more distant source in the Peruvian Andes and measure it at about 7,000 km, which would make it the longest. The debate is still open.
### Length versus size
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Nile", "items": ["~6,650 km long", "flows through desert", "modest flow"]}, "r": {"t": "Amazon", "items": ["~6,400–7,000 km", "drains a rainforest", "~70× the Nile's flow"]}}
The Nile crosses the Sahara with little rain to feed it and loses water to evaporation and irrigation, so it carries surprisingly little. The Amazon is fed by rain over a basin almost as big as Australia.
!? The Yangtze in China is the longest river that flows entirely within one country.
> Length depends on how you measure; the Amazon wins easily on the amount of water.

# The Amazon's flow
A river's **discharge** is the volume of water passing a point each second. The Amazon's averages around 200,000 cubic metres per second, enough to fill an Olympic swimming pool in about 1/80 of a second.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "thousand m³ per second (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Amazon", "v": 200.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Congo", "v": 41.0}, {"l": "Ganges–Brahmaputra", "v": 38.0}, {"l": "Tagus", "v": 0.5}]}
### Why so much?
• Its basin covers about 7 million km², about 40% of South America.
• It drains the rainiest large region on Earth, with 2,000–3,000 mm of rain a year in places.
• Hundreds of tributaries, a dozen of them longer than 1,500 km, feed it from both hemispheres, so some are always in flood.
### A river you can drink at sea
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~200,000", "l": "m³ of water per second"}, {"v": "~20%", "l": "of all river water to oceans"}, {"v": "~300 km", "l": "freshwater plume at sea"}]}
The Amazon pushes so much fresh water into the Atlantic that the sea stays noticeably less salty far from the coast. In 1500 the Spanish captain Vicente Yáñez Pinzón found fresh water far out at sea before he even saw land, and named it the "Mar Dulce", the sweet sea.
!? No bridge crosses the main Amazon anywhere along its length; the river is wide, floods seasonally and flows through few roads.
> The Amazon carries about a fifth of all river water reaching the oceans.

# Rivers and civilisation
The first cities, writing systems and states appeared in river valleys between about 3500 and 2000 BCE. Rivers offered reliable water in dry lands, yearly floods that left fertile silt, and a highway for boats.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Tigris–Euphrates", "b": "Mesopotamia, ~3500 BCE"}, {"a": "Nile", "b": "Egypt, ~3100 BCE"}, {"a": "Indus", "b": "Harappan cities, ~2600 BCE"}, {"a": "Yellow River", "b": "China, ~2000 BCE"}]}
### From floods to states
Controlling water was hard work. Canals, dykes and storage needed many people to cooperate, calendars to predict floods and records to share out land and harvests. Some historians see this as one root of government, mathematics and writing.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Flood brings silt", "Big harvests, surplus", "Canals, cities, rulers", "Writing and records"]}
### Rivers still shape power
Egypt depends on the Nile for about 90% of its fresh water, which is why Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam upstream, completed in 2025, has caused tension. Iberia has similar, if calmer, arrangements: the 1998 **Albufeira Convention** sets how much water Spain must let flow into Portugal down the Tagus, Douro, Guadiana, Minho and Lima.
!? Ancient Egyptians measured the Nile's rise with "nilometers", stone staircases or wells marked with levels, and used the readings to set taxes.
> Civilisation began where rivers made dry land fertile and forced people to organise.

# Portugal's rivers
Most of Portugal's big rivers are Iberian rivers: they rise on the Spanish plateau (the Meseta) and flow west to the Atlantic, because the peninsula tilts gently towards the ocean. Portugal is downstream, so water management has to be shared with Spain.

# The highest mountains
The 14 peaks above 8,000 m, the "eight-thousanders", are all in the Himalaya and the neighbouring Karakoram, on the borders of Nepal, China, India and Pakistan. They are there because the Indian plate is still pushing into Asia, lifting the land.

### Measuring Everest
Everest's official height, 8,848.86 m, was agreed by Nepal and China in 2020 after new surveys with GPS and satellite data. It is called Sagarmatha in Nepal and Chomolungma in Tibet. It was first climbed in 1953 by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay.

# Highest in Portugal
Portugal's highest summit is not on the mainland at all. **Mount Pico** on Pico island in the Azores rises 2,351 m straight from the Atlantic, a near-perfect volcanic cone. Its last eruptions on the island were in the 1700s, and it is considered dormant, not extinct.

### Serra da Estrela
On the mainland, the **Torre** in the Serra da Estrela reaches 1,993 m. A stone tower was built on top in the 1800s, reportedly so the summit would reach a round 2,000 m. ### From the coast you can see the difference

# How mountains form
Mountains are made in a few main ways, almost all linked to plate tectonics. Rock can be pushed up, folded, piled up as lava, or left standing as everything around it wears away.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Mountain types", "items": ["Fold: plates collide", "Volcanic: lava builds up", "Block: faults lift land", "Erosion: leftovers stand"]}
### Collision ranges
When two continents collide, neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples and thickens. The **Himalaya** began rising when India hit Asia about 50 million years ago; they still rise a few millimetres a year. The **Alps** come from Africa's slow push into Europe. The **Andes** are different: the ocean plate dives under South America, and melting rock feeds a chain of volcanoes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plates converge", "Crust crumples, thickens", "Mountains rise", "Erosion wears them down"]}
### Old and young mountains
Young ranges like the Himalaya and Alps are tall and jagged. Old ranges have been worn down by hundreds of millions of years of rain, ice and frost. Northern Portugal's granites and schists are roots of a range formed when Pangaea assembled, about 300 million years ago, long since eroded to rounded hills.
!! Mountains are permanent => They rise for millions of years and are slowly worn away
> Most great mountains are the crumple zones of colliding plates, then sculpted by erosion.

# Glaciers
A glacier forms where more snow falls each winter than melts each summer. Over years the snow compacts into dense ice, which becomes so heavy that it flows downhill under its own weight, usually a few centimetres to a few metres a day.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Snow builds up", "Compacts into ice", "Ice flows downhill", "Melts at lower end"]}
### Sculptors of landscape
Ice armed with rocks grinds the land like sandpaper. Glaciers turn V-shaped river valleys into broad **U-shaped valleys**, leave long ridges of rubble called moraines, and carve deep troughs. When the sea flooded those troughs after the Ice Age, Norway's **fjords** were born.
### Retreating everywhere
Alpine glaciers have lost roughly 40% of their volume since 2000, and Switzerland alone lost about 10% in just 2022–2023. Scientists expect many small glaciers, including in the Pyrenees, to disappear this century. Melting land ice also raises sea levels.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Ice Age", "items": ["ice over Scandinavia", "glaciers in Estrela", "sea ~120 m lower"]}, "r": {"t": "Today", "items": ["Alps' ice shrinking", "Pyrenees nearly gone", "sea rising"]}}
!? About 20,000 years ago, the sea was so low that you could have walked from France to Britain, and Portugal's coast lay many kilometres west of today's.
> Glaciers are slow rivers of ice that carve valleys, and almost all of them are shrinking.

# Lakes
A lake is a body of water surrounded by land. They form in hollows left by glaciers, in rift valleys where the crust pulls apart, in volcanic craters, or behind dams built by people or by landslides.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Caspian Sea", "b": "largest by area, salty"}, {"a": "Lake Superior", "b": "largest freshwater by area"}, {"a": "Lake Baikal", "b": "deepest (~1,640 m), oldest"}, {"a": "Lake Titicaca", "b": "high navigable lake, 3,800 m"}]}
### The Caspian: lake or sea?
The **Caspian** covers about 370,000 km², about four times Portugal. It's slightly salty, a leftover of an ancient ocean. Whether it counts as a sea or a lake mattered for oil and gas rights; the five countries around it signed a special convention in 2018.
### Baikal, the record-breaker
Lake **Baikal** in Siberia fills a rift where the crust is splitting apart. It is about 25 million years old and 1,640 m deep, holding about 23,000 km³ of water, more than all five North American Great Lakes together. Around 80% of its animal species, including the Baikal seal, live nowhere else.

!? The Azores' Sete Cidades lakes, one blue and one green, sit inside a volcanic crater about 5 km across.

# Waterfalls
Waterfalls form where a river meets a sudden drop: hard rock over softer rock, a fault, a cliff carved by glaciers, or a plateau edge. Water erodes the soft layer, undercutting the hard one, so many falls slowly retreat upstream.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "metres, total drop", "items": [{"l": "Angel Falls", "v": 979.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Victoria Falls", "v": 108.0}, {"l": "Iguaçu", "v": 82.0}, {"l": "Niagara", "v": 51.0}]}
### Tallest and widest
**Angel Falls** (Kerepakupai Merú) in Venezuela tumbles 979 m from the flat top of a tepui, a table mountain. The water often turns to mist before reaching the bottom. **Victoria Falls**, between Zambia and Zimbabwe, is about 1.7 km wide, forming one of the largest sheets of falling water. Locals call it *Mosi-oa-Tunya*, "the smoke that thunders".
### Iguaçu
On the border of Brazil and Argentina, **Iguaçu** is a chain of about 275 falls along almost 3 km. The Devil's Throat, a U-shaped chasm, takes about half the flow.

Europe is a small continent, about 10 million km², with a lot of countries. ### Dense and urban

# The European Union
The EU began in 1951 with six countries pooling coal and steel to make another war between France and Germany impossible. It grew into a union of 27 states with about 450 million people, its own parliament, court and laws in many areas.

# Schengen
The Schengen Agreement was signed in 1985 in the village of Schengen, in Luxembourg, where the borders of France, Germany and Luxembourg meet. Since 1995 its members have scrapped routine passport checks at their shared borders. Portugal was one of the first countries to apply it.

# Languages
Most European languages descend from one ancestor, **Proto-Indo-European**, spoken perhaps 6,000 years ago somewhere around the steppes north of the Black Sea. Its descendants spread across Europe and into Iran and India, which is why Portuguese "mãe", German "Mutter" and Sanskrit "mātṛ" all sound alike.

# Madeira
Madeira lies about 1,000 km south-west of Lisbon and about 600 km from Africa's coast. It's the top of a huge volcanic shield rising from the ocean floor, so steep that much of the island seems to stand on end. Its peaks reach 1,862 m at Pico Ruivo.

### The laurel forest
The **Laurissilva** is a forest of laurel-type trees that once covered much of southern Europe millions of years ago. Madeira holds the largest surviving area, about 15,000 hectares, listed by UNESCO in 1999. It catches moisture from clouds and feeds the island's springs.

Most rain falls on the wet north side, while farms and towns cluster on the sunnier south. From the 1500s, Madeirans cut **levadas**, narrow channels, into cliffs to carry water around the mountains. ### The Rift Valley
The **East African Rift** is where Africa is slowly splitting apart. It created deep lakes like Tanganyika and Malawi, volcanoes like Kilimanjaro, and some of the oldest human fossil sites. In tens of millions of years, East Africa may become a separate landmass.

The Okavango river never reaches the sea. It spreads into a vast inland delta in Botswana's Kalahari, a green oasis teeming with elephants, hippos and birds. It's a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
!? Kilimanjaro, just south of the equator, has glaciers on its summit, though they have shrunk by over 80% in the last century.
> Africa's wonders span desert, rift, river and the largest land migrations on Earth.

# Resources
Africa holds a large share of the minerals the modern world needs. The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's **cobalt**, vital for batteries in phones and electric cars. South Africa has most of the world's **platinum**. Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half the world's **cocoa**.

### The resource curse
Being rich in resources doesn't guarantee prosperity. Economists talk of a **resource curse**: when wealth comes from a few mines or oil fields, it can feed corruption, conflict and dependence on volatile prices, while other industries struggle.

# Mobile money
In 2007 the Kenyan mobile operator Safaricom, partly owned by Vodafone, launched **M-Pesa** ("pesa" means money in Swahili). Users deposit cash with a local agent, often a small shop, and can then send it by text message to anyone with a phone.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cash in at local agent", "Balance on SIM", "Send by SMS", "Cash out anywhere"]}
### Why Africa led
Most people had no bank account and banks had few branches, but mobile phones spread quickly. Mobile money leapfrogged traditional banking, just as mobile phones leapfrogged landlines.
### Effects
• Workers in cities send money home to villages instantly and safely.
• Small traders pay suppliers without carrying cash.
• People can save, get small loans and buy insurance.
• Studies in Kenya linked M-Pesa access to lower poverty, especially for women-headed households.

# The cradle of humankind
Africa is where our lineage split from that of chimpanzees, about 6–7 million years ago, and where our own species evolved. Humans spread to the rest of the world from Africa, mostly in the last 70,000 years or so.

### Lucy
In 1974 at Hadar, Ethiopia, Donald Johanson's team found 40% of a skeleton of *Australopithecus afarensis*, about 3.2 million years old. She was about 1.1 m tall and walked upright but had a small brain. She was nicknamed after the Beatles song playing in the camp that night.
### Jebel Irhoud
In 2017, fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco were dated to about 300,000 years old, pushing back the origin of *Homo sapiens* by about 100,000 years. They suggest our species evolved across Africa, not in one small "Eden".

> Every human family tree has its roots in Africa.

Africa's story is often told as all crisis or all boom. The reality is both, and it varies hugely between 54 countries. In recent years several economies, such as Ethiopia, Rwanda, Côte d'Ivoire and Tanzania, have grown at 5–8% a year.

### Tech hubs
**Lagos** has fintech companies valued in the billions. **Nairobi**'s "Silicon Savannah" grew around M-Pesa. **Kigali** in Rwanda has made itself a clean, well-run conference and tech centre. Africa's huge solar potential and its minerals could power its future.

# China
China has had a continuous written civilisation since at least the Shang dynasty, about 3,500 years ago, whose oracle-bone script is the ancestor of today's characters. It was first unified as an empire in 221 BCE under Qin Shi Huang.

### From poverty to powerhouse
After 1978, Deng Xiaoping opened China to markets and foreign investment. Growth averaged close to 10% a year for three decades, and several hundred million people left extreme poverty. China is now the world's biggest manufacturer and exporter, and the second-largest economy at market prices.

== wo-americas

# North, Central, South
The Americas cover about 42 million km², nearly 30% of Earth's land, and are home to just over a billion people. They run from the Arctic islands of Canada to Cape Horn, about 56° south.

### One continent or two?
In Portugal, Spain and Latin America, schools usually teach one continent, "América". In the English-speaking world it is normally two. Geologically, Central America and the Caribbean belong to North America; the join with South America is near the Panama–Colombia border.
### A late land bridge
The **Isthmus of Panama** rose from the sea only about 3 million years ago. Animals crossed in both directions: armadillos and opossums went north, while cats, bears and deer went south. Closing the gap also redirected ocean currents and probably helped strengthen the Gulf Stream, which keeps western Europe mild.

# First peoples
During the last Ice Age, sea levels were so low that Siberia and Alaska were joined by a land bridge called **Beringia**. People crossed from Asia, then spread south along the coast and through ice-free corridors.

### How long ago?
The site of Monte Verde in Chile shows people about 14,500 years ago. Footprints in White Sands, New Mexico, may be over 20,000 years old, though that date is still debated.
### Great civilisations

# European conquest
In 1492 Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, reached the Caribbean. In 1494 the **Treaty of Tordesillas** split the newly found lands between Spain and Portugal along a line in the Atlantic. That line is why Brazil, which Pedro Álvares Cabral reached in 1500, speaks Portuguese.

### Why so few conquered so many
• **Disease**: smallpox, measles and flu were new to the Americas, and people had no immunity
• **Allies**: Cortés won with thousands of native allies who hated Aztec rule
• **Weapons and horses**: steel, guns and cavalry gave an edge in battle
### The human cost
Estimates of the population in 1492 vary widely, but within about a century it may have fallen by as much as 90% in many regions. To replace native labour, Europeans shipped about 12.5 million enslaved Africans across the Atlantic. Portugal and Brazil were at the centre of this trade: Brazil alone received about 4.9 million people, around 40% of the total.

# Canada
Canada covers about 10 million km², second only to Russia. Yet it has only about 41 million people, fewer than Spain, because most of it is cold forest, tundra and Arctic islands.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "area, million km² (about)", "items": [{"l": "Russia", "v": 17.1}, {"l": "Canada", "v": 10.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "USA", "v": 9.8}, {"l": "China", "v": 9.6}, {"l": "Brazil", "v": 8.5}]}
### Hugging the border
Most Canadians live in a narrow strip near the US border, where the climate is mildest and farmland best. Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver alone hold over a third of the population. The border with the USA, about 8,900 km, is the longest between two countries.
### Two languages
French settlers founded Quebec in 1608, before Britain took over in 1763. Today about a fifth of Canadians speak French as a first language, mostly in Quebec, and federal services must be offered in both English and French.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Canada", "items": ["~41 M people", "parliament, king as head", "public health system"]}, "r": {"t": "USA", "items": ["~340 M people", "president as head", "mostly private insurance"]}}
### A nation of immigrants
Canada admits a high share of immigrants for its size, and around a quarter of residents were born abroad. About half a million people of Portuguese descent live there, mostly around Toronto.
!? Canada has the longest coastline of any country, over 200,000 km, and more lakes than the rest of the world combined.
> Canada is vast on the map but small in people, with almost everyone living in a thin band along its southern edge.

# Spanish and Portuguese America
Spain and Portugal's colonies became today's **Latin America**. Spanish is the main language in 18 countries of the Americas; Portuguese in Brazil. Mexico, with about 130 million people, is the largest Spanish-speaking country on Earth.

### Same language, different flavours
Like British and American English, the Iberian languages changed differently on each side of the Atlantic.

### Why the spellings met
The 1990 Orthographic Agreement aimed to unify Portuguese spelling across Portugal, Brazil and the African countries. It was adopted gradually, in Portugal from 2009, and remains controversial with some writers.
### Growing in the USA
About 40 million people in the USA speak Spanish at home, making it one of the largest Spanish-speaking populations anywhere.

# The Andes and the Amazon
The Andes run about 7,000 km through seven countries, from Venezuela to southern Chile. They exist because the Pacific's **Nazca plate** slides beneath South America, crumpling the edge upwards and feeding volcanoes.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ocean plate sinks", "Continent edge crumples", "Mountains and volcanoes", "Strong earthquakes"]}
### Life up high
Aconcagua, about 6,960 m, is the highest peak outside Asia. La Paz in Bolivia sits at about 3,600 m, and millions live on the high plateau, the Altiplano, where potatoes and quinoa were first farmed. The largest earthquake ever recorded struck Chile in 1960, at magnitude 9.5.
### The great forest
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~5.5 M km²", "l": "of Amazon rainforest"}, {"v": "~20%", "l": "of river water reaching seas"}, {"v": "~10%", "l": "of known species"}]}
The Amazon River carries more water than the next several largest rivers combined. Trees release huge amounts of water vapour, forming "flying rivers" that bring rain to farms in southern Brazil and Argentina.
### Under pressure
Roughly a fifth of the original forest has been cleared, mainly for cattle and soy. Scientists warn that beyond a certain point, parts could dry out and turn to savanna.
!! The Amazon produces 20% of the world's oxygen => It uses almost as much as it makes; it matters for carbon and rain
> The Andes and the Amazon are linked: one makes the rain-catching wall, the other the world's greatest river system.

# The Panama Canal
The French, led by Ferdinand de Lesseps of Suez fame, tried first in the 1880s and failed. Tropical diseases like malaria and yellow fever killed over 20,000 workers. The USA took over in 1904, controlled the mosquitoes, and opened the canal on 15 August 1914.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1881", "l": "French attempt begins"}, {"y": "1904", "l": "USA takes over"}, {"y": "1914", "l": "Canal opens"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "Handed to Panama"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "Larger locks open"}]}
### How it works
Instead of cutting to sea level, engineers dammed a river to create Gatún Lake, about 26 m above the sea. Locks lift ships up to the lake and lower them on the other side.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ship enters lock", "Water fills chamber", "Ship rises 26 m", "Crosses Gatún Lake", "Locks lower it down"]}
### Water is the limit
Each crossing uses about 200 million litres of fresh water, which flows out to sea. A drought in 2023–24 lowered Gatún Lake so much that daily crossings were cut, causing queues and higher shipping costs.
ex: New York to San Francisco round Cape Horn ≈ 21,000 km | Through the canal ≈ 8,000 km | Saving ≈ 13,000 km
!? The canal runs roughly north-west to south-east, so ships going from the Atlantic to the Pacific actually end up further east.
> The Panama Canal turned a dangerous voyage round South America into a crossing of about ten hours.

# Food from the Americas
After 1492, crops and animals moved between the hemispheres in the **Columbian Exchange**. Portuguese ships were key carriers, taking American crops to Africa and Asia within a few decades.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Maize", "b": "Mexico, ~9,000 years ago"}, {"a": "Potato", "b": "Andes, ~8,000 years ago"}, {"a": "Tomato, chilli", "b": "Mexico and Andes"}, {"a": "Cocoa, vanilla", "b": "Central America"}]}
### Changing the world's diet
Maize is now the most produced grain on Earth, used for food, animal feed and biofuel. Potatoes fed Europe's population boom, but dependence on them also led to the Irish famine of the 1840s, when blight destroyed the crop.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "world harvest, million tonnes (about)", "items": [{"l": "Maize", "v": 1200.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Wheat", "v": 800.0}, {"l": "Rice (paddy)", "v": 800.0}, {"l": "Potatoes", "v": 380.0}]}
### A Portuguese twist
• Portuguese traders spread chillies to Africa and Asia; **piri-piri** and many Indian curries depend on them
• Goan **vindaloo** comes from the Portuguese "carne de vinha d'alhos"
• Portugal planted cocoa on São Tomé, which around 1900 was among the world's top producers
• Tomatoes were first grown in Europe as ornamental plants, as many feared they were poisonous

# Oceania
Oceania is less a continent than an ocean with land scattered across it. It covers roughly a sixth of Earth's surface, yet holds only about 45 million people, more than half of them in Australia. There are 14 independent countries, from Australia (about 27 million people) to Tuvalu (about 11,000).
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Oceania", "items": ["Australia", "Melanesia", "Micronesia", "Polynesia"]}
### Three island regions
The names were coined by a French explorer, Jules Dumont d'Urville, in the 1830s, and they are still used today, although the boundaries are cultural rather than strict.
• **Melanesia** ("black islands"): Papua New Guinea, Fiji, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia
• **Micronesia** ("small islands"): Palau, Guam, Kiribati, the Marshall Islands, Nauru
• **Polynesia** ("many islands"): a vast triangle with Hawaii, New Zealand and Easter Island at its corners
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Melanesia", "b": "large, mountainous islands"}, {"a": "Micronesia", "b": "tiny islands and atolls"}, {"a": "Polynesia", "b": "Hawaii–NZ–Easter Island"}]}
### Languages and peoples
Papua New Guinea alone has about 840 living languages, more than any other country on Earth. Its rugged mountains kept valleys isolated for thousands of years, so languages split and multiplied. Across the Pacific, many islands are still shared between their own nations and outside powers: Hawaii is a US state, French Polynesia and New Caledonia belong to France, and Easter Island to Chile.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~45 million", "l": "people in all Oceania"}, {"v": "~840", "l": "languages in PNG"}, {"v": "14", "l": "independent countries"}]}
!? Kiribati's islands are spread over about 3.5 million km² of ocean, more than India's area, but its land adds up to only about 800 km², similar to the Madeira archipelago.
> Oceania is mostly water: its countries are defined as much by the sea between islands as by the land.

# Australia
Australia is about 7.7 million km², roughly 84 times the size of Portugal, but it has only about 27 million people. Around 70% of the land is arid or semi-arid, so people cluster on the wetter coasts, especially in the southeast. Sydney and Melbourne each have about 5 million inhabitants; the capital, Canberra, was purpose-built in between to settle their rivalry.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "84×", "l": "bigger than Portugal"}, {"v": "~27 million", "l": "people"}, {"v": "~85%", "l": "live near the coast"}]}
### Why the animals are so different
Australia broke away from Antarctica about 35–45 million years ago and drifted north alone. Its mammals evolved in isolation, which is why it is dominated by **marsupials**, whose young finish developing in a pouch.
• Kangaroos and wallabies: hop efficiently, using elastic tendons like springs
• Koalas: eat eucalyptus leaves, which are toxic to most animals, and sleep up to 20 hours a day
• Platypus and echidnas: **monotremes**, mammals that lay eggs
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Mammals", "items": ["Placental (most)", "Marsupial (pouch)", "Monotreme (eggs)"]}
### A tough land
Australia is the driest inhabited continent and has some of the oldest, most weathered soils on Earth. Bushfires, droughts and floods are part of its normal rhythm; the 2019–20 "Black Summer" fires burned over 20 million hectares, about twice the size of Portugal.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Coast", "items": ["most cities", "rain and farms"]}, "r": {"t": "Outback", "items": ["desert and scrub", "very few people"]}}
!! Australia is mostly desert sand => Much of the outback is scrub, grassland and rocky plains; sandy dune deserts are only part of it
> Australia is huge but empty: geography, not size, decides where its people live.

# Aboriginal Australians
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples arrived by sea from Southeast Asia, crossing at least 90 km of open water even when sea levels were lower. Archaeological sites show people in Australia at least 50,000 years ago, and some researchers argue for about 65,000, though that date is debated.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~50,000 BP", "l": "people in Australia"}, {"y": "1788", "l": "British First Fleet"}, {"y": "1967", "l": "referendum on census"}, {"y": "1992", "l": "Mabo land ruling"}]}
### Knowledge carried in songs
Before 1788 there were about 250 distinct languages and hundreds of nations, each with its own country (territory). Knowledge of water holes, seasons, food and law was passed down in stories, songs, dance and art. **Songlines** are routes across the land that are remembered as sung narratives, a kind of oral map.
• Fire was used deliberately to manage land and encourage new growth
• Rock art sites in places like Kakadu and the Kimberley are tens of thousands of years old
• Torres Strait Islanders, from islands between Australia and New Guinea, are culturally distinct, with Melanesian links
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Songs and stories as maps", "Fire to manage the land", "Rock art over many millennia", "About 250 languages in 1788"]}
### After colonisation
British settlement brought disease, violence and the loss of land. The legal idea of **terra nullius** ("nobody's land") was only overturned by the High Court's Mabo decision in 1992. Today Indigenous Australians are about 4% of the population, and gaps in health and life expectancy remain a national issue.
!? A 2023 referendum to give Indigenous Australians a formal advisory "Voice" to Parliament was rejected by about 60% of voters.
> Aboriginal cultures kept knowledge alive for tens of thousands of years without writing, through land, song and story.

# The Great Barrier Reef
The Great Barrier Reef is not one reef but about 2,900 separate reefs and 900 islands, spread over about 344,000 km² of protected sea, an area larger than Italy. It is built by tiny animals called coral polyps, which lay down limestone skeletons layer upon layer over thousands of years.
### How coral lives
Coral polyps host microscopic algae inside their tissues. The algae photosynthesise and pass sugars to the coral; the coral gives them shelter and nutrients. This partnership is why reefs thrive in clear, shallow, sunlit, nutrient-poor tropical water.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Coral polyp", "items": ["shelter", "nutrients"]}, "r": {"t": "Algae", "items": ["sugars from sunlight", "colour"]}}
### What bleaching is
When water stays about 1 °C above the usual summer maximum for several weeks, the partnership breaks down. The coral expels its algae and turns white. If the heat passes quickly, coral can recover; if it lasts, the coral starves and dies.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sea stays too warm", "Coral expels algae", "Coral turns white", "Recovers or dies"]}
• Mass bleaching hit the reef in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, 2024 and 2025
• Other pressures: crown-of-thorns starfish, cyclones, and farm run-off carrying sediment
• Recovery between events takes about 10 years for fast-growing corals, longer for slow ones
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1981", "l": "World Heritage site"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "first mass bleaching"}, {"y": "2016–17", "l": "back-to-back events"}, {"y": "2024", "l": "most widespread yet"}]}
!! The reef is already dead => Large parts still have live coral, but bleaching events now come too often for full recovery
> Coral reefs depend on a partnership with algae that breaks down when the sea gets too hot.

# New Zealand
New Zealand, or **Aotearoa** in Māori, has about 5.3 million people on an area about three times that of Portugal. It sits right on the boundary between the Pacific and Australian plates, which is why it has so much dramatic scenery packed into a small space.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "North Island", "items": ["volcanoes, hot springs", "most people"]}, "r": {"t": "South Island", "items": ["Southern Alps", "glaciers, fjords"]}}
### Shaped by plates and ice
• The **Alpine Fault** runs along the South Island and pushes the Southern Alps upward; Aoraki/Mount Cook reaches 3,724 m
• The Taupō volcano erupted massively around 230 CE; its crater is now Lake Taupō
• Ice-age glaciers carved Fiordland's steep fjords, such as Milford Sound
### The last major land settled
Polynesian voyagers arrived around 1250–1300 CE, making New Zealand the last large landmass people reached. They became the Māori. Within about 150 years, the giant flightless **moa** birds, some over 3 m tall with neck stretched, had been hunted to extinction.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~1300", "l": "Māori settle"}, {"y": "1642", "l": "Abel Tasman sights it"}, {"y": "1840", "l": "Treaty of Waitangi"}, {"y": "1893", "l": "women get the vote"}]}
The Treaty of Waitangi, signed in 1840 between the British Crown and Māori chiefs, is still the country's founding document, though its English and Māori versions differ in meaning, which has caused disputes ever since.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~5.3 million", "l": "people"}, {"v": "3,724 m", "l": "Aoraki/Mount Cook"}, {"v": "1893", "l": "first nation to let women vote"}]}
!? In 1893 New Zealand became the first self-governing country to give all women the right to vote in national elections.
> New Zealand is young land on a busy plate boundary, and one of the last places on Earth humans reached.

# Polynesian navigators
Polynesian voyaging is one of humanity's great achievements. Starting from islands near New Guinea about 3,000 years ago, people sailed east into an ocean where the next island might be thousands of kilometres away, settling almost every habitable speck within the Polynesian triangle.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~1000 BCE", "l": "Tonga and Samoa"}, {"y": "~1000 CE", "l": "Society Islands"}, {"y": "~1200", "l": "Hawaii, Easter Island"}, {"y": "~1300", "l": "New Zealand"}]}
### Reading the ocean
Navigators carried no instruments. They memorised where dozens of stars rose and set on the horizon, using them like a compass. They felt ocean swells through the hull, and could detect land beyond the horizon by its effect on the waves.
• **Stars:** a mental "star compass" of rising and setting points
• **Swells:** steady swells keep their direction for days
• **Birds:** some species fly out to sea by day and return to land at dusk
• **Clouds:** green-tinged or still clouds can sit above unseen islands
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Finding land", "items": ["Star compass", "Ocean swells", "Birds at dusk", "Clouds over islands"]}
### Proving it again
In 1976 the double canoe Hōkūleʻa sailed about 4,000 km from Hawaii to Tahiti without instruments, guided by Mau Piailug, a navigator from Micronesia. It settled a long debate over whether Polynesians had sailed deliberately or simply drifted.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~4,000 km", "l": "Hawaii to Tahiti, 1976"}, {"v": "0", "l": "instruments used"}]}
!? The sweet potato, a South American plant, was grown across Polynesia before Europeans arrived, strong evidence that Polynesians reached the Americas and came back.
> Polynesians treated the ocean as a map that could be read, centuries before European ships crossed the Pacific.

# The Arctic
The Arctic is the opposite of Antarctica: an ocean almost surrounded by land, rather than land surrounded by ocean. Eight countries have territory there: Canada, the USA, Russia, Norway, Denmark (through Greenland), Iceland, Sweden and Finland. They cooperate through the Arctic Council, founded in 1996.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Arctic", "items": ["ocean ringed by land", "floating sea ice"]}, "r": {"t": "Antarctica", "items": ["land ringed by ocean", "ice up to 4 km thick"]}}
### Why it warms so fast
Since 1979 the Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the planet as a whole. The main reason is a feedback loop: white ice reflects most sunlight, while dark open water absorbs it.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Arctic warms", "Sea ice melts", "Dark water absorbs heat", "More warming"]}
### Shrinking summer ice
Sea ice reaches its smallest extent each September. Satellite records since 1979 show the September minimum has fallen by roughly 40%, with a record low in 2012. The ice is also thinner and younger than before.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "September sea ice, million km² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1980", "v": 7.7}, {"l": "2000", "v": 6.3}, {"l": "2012", "v": 3.4, "hi": true}, {"l": "2024", "v": 4.3}]}
• Melting sea ice barely changes sea level, because it is already floating
• Melting land ice on Greenland does raise seas: the whole sheet holds about 7 m of sea-level rise
• Less ice opens new shipping routes and access to oil, gas and minerals, raising geopolitical tension
!! Melting Arctic sea ice is raising sea levels => Floating ice is already displacing water; it's melting land ice, like Greenland's, that raises seas
> The Arctic is warming fastest because losing ice makes it absorb even more of the Sun's heat.

# Polar day and night
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4°. As Earth orbits the Sun, each pole spends half the year leaning towards it and half leaning away. The Arctic and Antarctic Circles, at about 66.5° latitude, mark where this produces at least one full day without sunset and one without sunrise.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earth tilted 23.4°", "Pole leans to the Sun", "Sun circles, never sets", "Six months later: dark"]}
### How it varies with latitude
The further you go towards the pole, the longer the midnight sun and the polar night last. On the Arctic Circle itself it is about one day each; in Tromsø, Norway (about 70° N), the Sun stays up from about late May to late July. At the North Pole, there is one sunrise in March and one sunset in September.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "longest day, hours (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Lisbon 39°N", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "Oslo 60°N", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "Arctic Circle", "v": 24.0, "hi": true}]}
### Living with it
• Polar night is rarely pitch black: there is twilight at midday and often the aurora
• People in the far north report sleep problems and winter low mood; light therapy lamps are common
• In summer, sports matches and festivals run past midnight in full daylight
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Midnight sun", "b": "Sun stays above the horizon"}, {"a": "Polar night", "b": "Sun stays below the horizon"}, {"a": "Arctic Circle", "b": "about 66.5° N"}]}
!? Because the atmosphere bends sunlight, the Sun appears above the horizon a little before it really is, so polar "day" lasts slightly longer than polar "night".
> Polar day and night are caused by Earth's tilt, not by changing distance from the Sun.

# Life in the cold
Humans have lived in the Arctic for thousands of years. The ancestors of today's Inuit spread across Alaska, Canada and Greenland about 1,000 years ago. The Sámi have herded reindeer and fished across northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia for much longer.
### Human ingenuity
• Layered clothing of caribou and seal skin trapped warm air; the word "anorak" comes from Greenlandic
• Snow houses were temporary winter hunting shelters; snow is an excellent insulator
• The **kayak** was invented in the Arctic for hunting seals
• Diets rich in fat from seals, whales and fish provided energy where plants were scarce
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Layered skins trap warm air", "Snow walls insulate", "Kayaks for seal hunting", "Fat-rich food for energy"]}
### Animal tricks
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Polar bear", "b": "fat up to 10 cm, dense fur"}, {"a": "Arctic tern", "b": "migrates pole to pole"}, {"a": "Emperor penguin", "b": "thousands huddle together"}, {"a": "Icefish", "b": "antifreeze in its blood"}]}
Antarctic icefish live in water at about −1.9 °C, colder than the normal freezing point of blood. Special antifreeze proteins stick to tiny ice crystals and stop them growing. Many Arctic animals also follow a pattern: larger, rounder bodies lose heat more slowly than small, thin ones.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Keep heat in", "items": ["fat and fur", "huddling"]}, "r": {"t": "Avoid the cold", "items": ["migration", "hibernation"]}}
!? An Arctic tern can fly about 70,000 km a year between the Arctic and Antarctic, so it sees more daylight than any other animal.
!! Polar bears have white skin => Their skin is black; their hairs are clear and hollow and only look white
> Life in the cold succeeds by holding on to heat or escaping when it's too cold, and people learned both.

# Pacific islands and the sea
Many Pacific nations are made of **atolls**: rings of coral islands around a lagoon, sitting on top of sunken volcanoes. Tuvalu's highest point is under 5 m above sea level, and most of Kiribati lies less than 3 m above the sea. For them, a few tens of centimetres of sea-level rise is a national emergency.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "<5 m", "l": "Tuvalu's highest point"}, {"v": "~20 cm", "l": "global sea rise since 1900"}, {"v": "~11,000", "l": "people in Tuvalu"}]}
### The threats are not just flooding
• **King tides** and storm surges wash over land more often
• Salt water seeps into the thin layer of fresh groundwater that islands depend on
• Coastal erosion eats away at homes, roads and graves
• Warmer seas bleach the reefs that protect the shore and feed people
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Seas rise", "More flooding at high tide", "Salt spoils fresh water", "Land harder to live on"]}
### Responses
Islands are building sea walls and raising land, but they are also planning for the worst. Kiribati bought about 20 km² of land in Fiji in 2014 for food security. In 2023 Tuvalu signed a treaty with Australia allowing up to 280 citizens a year to migrate there. Vanuatu led the push for a 2025 International Court of Justice opinion, which said states have legal duties to tackle climate change.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Emissions", "items": ["Pacific islands: tiny", "big economies: most"]}, "r": {"t": "Exposure", "items": ["Pacific islands: extreme", "others: lower"]}}
!? Some studies show many atoll islands have kept or even grown their area over recent decades, as waves pile up new coral sand, but the fresh water and people on them are still at risk.
> The Pacific islands have done least to cause climate change and are among the first to face its consequences.

== wo-economy

# GDP
GDP can be measured three ways, and in theory they all give the same answer: add up everything produced, everything spent, or everything earned. The spending approach is the one most people learn.
= GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "GDP = C + I + G + (X−M)", "items": [{"s": "C", "m": "household spending"}, {"s": "I", "m": "business investment"}, {"s": "G", "m": "government spending"}, {"s": "X−M", "m": "exports minus imports"}]}
Portugal's GDP was about €285 billion in 2024. The modern measure was developed in the 1930s, partly by the economist Simon Kuznets, who himself warned that it shouldn't be read as a measure of welfare.
### Nominal or real?
Nominal GDP uses current prices, so it rises with inflation. **Real GDP** removes price changes, showing whether the economy actually produced more. When news says "the economy grew 2%", it means real growth.
ex: Nominal GDP up 6% | Prices up 4% | Real growth ≈ 6% − 4% = 2%
### What GDP misses
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Counted", "items": ["paid work", "repairing crash damage", "building a motorway"]}, "r": {"t": "Not counted", "items": ["unpaid care at home", "clean air", "free time"]}}
GDP also ignores how income is shared and whether growth is sustainable. That's why statisticians publish alternatives such as the UN's Human Development Index, which adds health and education.
!! A higher GDP means people are better off => GDP measures output; wellbeing also depends on health, inequality, free time and the environment
> GDP tells you how big an economy is, not how good life is inside it.

# The biggest economies
In 2025 the US economy was worth roughly $30 trillion and China's roughly $19 trillion, together about 40% of the world's output. Germany and Japan follow at about $4–5 trillion each, with India close behind Japan and expected to pass it. The world economy as a whole is around $115 trillion.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "GDP 2025, trillion US$ (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "USA", "v": 30.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "China", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "Germany", "v": 4.7}, {"l": "Japan", "v": 4.2}, {"l": "India", "v": 4.2}]}
### Purchasing power parity
Exchange rates don't capture what money buys locally. A haircut or a bus ride costs far less in Delhi than in Lisbon. **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** adjusts for these price differences. On that basis China has been the largest economy since the mid-2010s, and India is third.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Market rates", "items": ["USA first", "China second"]}, "r": {"t": "PPP", "items": ["China first", "USA second, India third"]}}
### Where Europe fits
• The EU's 27 economies together are worth about $19–20 trillion, similar to China at market rates
• Portugal's economy is about 0.3% of the world total
• Big populations matter: India's GDP is large but spread over 1.4 billion people
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~$115 tn", "l": "world GDP"}, {"v": "~40%", "l": "USA + China share"}, {"v": "~0.3%", "l": "Portugal's share"}]}
!? Measured at market exchange rates, the ranking can shift just because a currency rises or falls, even if nothing real has changed.
> Which economy is "biggest" depends on whether you count in dollars or in what money actually buys.

# GDP per person
Dividing GDP by population gives an average, and it changes the picture completely. China's economy is huge, but per person it is below Portugal's. Small, wealthy countries jump to the top.
= GDP per person = GDP ÷ population
### Why the leaders are odd
• **Luxembourg:** nearly half its workers commute from France, Belgium and Germany; they add to GDP but aren't counted as residents
• **Ireland:** many multinationals book profits there for tax reasons, inflating GDP; Ireland publishes an adjusted measure, "GNI*", to correct for it
• **Switzerland:** genuinely high wages, plus finance and pharmaceuticals

### Portugal's position
Measured in purchasing power standards, a Eurostat unit that evens out price levels, Portugal sits at roughly 80% of the EU average. It has been overtaken in recent decades by several central and eastern European countries, such as Czechia, Slovenia, Lithuania and Estonia, which joined the EU in 2004.
ex: Portugal GDP ≈ €285 billion | Population ≈ 10.7 million | €285,000 m ÷ 10.7 m ≈ €26,600 per person

!! GDP per person is what an average person earns => It includes company profits and taxes; median household income is usually much lower
> Per-person figures reveal living standards better than totals, but small countries with commuters or tax-driven profits can mislead.

# Sectors
Economists divide work into sectors. As countries grow richer, workers move from fields to factories and then to offices, shops and hotels. This shift has happened in almost every wealthy country.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Tertiary: services", "Secondary: industry, building", "Primary: farming, mining"]}
### Portugal's shift
In 1960 more than 40% of Portuguese workers were in agriculture. Today it's only a few per cent, while services account for about three-quarters of economic output. Industry still matters: car parts, the Autoeuropa factory in Palmela, moulds, footwear and textiles in the north.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of Portugal's output, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Services", "v": 75.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Industry & building", "v": 22.0}, {"l": "Farming & fishing", "v": 2.0}]}
### Tourism as an export
When a German visitor pays for a hotel in the Algarve, money flows into Portugal just as if a Portuguese product had been sold abroad. That's why tourism counts as a **service export**. Tourism revenue reached about €27 billion in 2024, and depending on how indirect effects are counted, tourism-related activity is roughly 12–16% of GDP.
• Upside: jobs, foreign income, regional development
• Downside: seasonal, low-wage jobs, vulnerable to shocks like the 2020 pandemic, pressure on housing
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Tourism boost", "items": ["foreign income", "jobs across regions"]}, "r": {"t": "Tourism risk", "items": ["seasonal, low wages", "hit hard by shocks"]}}
!? Some economists add a quaternary sector for knowledge work like research, software and consulting.
> Rich economies are mostly service economies, and for Portugal, tourists are one of the biggest export markets.

# Trade
The classic explanation of trade uses Portugal as its example. In 1817 the economist David Ricardo imagined England and Portugal making cloth and wine. Even if Portugal were better at both, both countries gain if each specialises in what it is *relatively* best at and trades. This is **comparative advantage**.
ex: Portugal: 1 unit wine costs 80 hours, cloth 90 | England: wine 120, cloth 100 | Portugal's wine is relatively cheaper, so Portugal makes wine and England cloth
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Absolute adv.", "items": ["better at making it", "in raw terms"]}, "r": {"t": "Comparative adv.", "items": ["lowest sacrifice", "of other goods"]}}
### Portugal today
Exports of goods and services are now close to half of Portugal's GDP, up from about a quarter in the 1990s. Spain is by far the largest market, taking roughly a quarter of goods exports.
• Cars and car parts, machinery, refined fuels from Sines
• Clothing and footwear, cork (Portugal produces about half the world's supply), wine
• Services: tourism, transport, IT and business services
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of goods exports, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Spain", "v": 26.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "France", "v": 12.0}, {"l": "Germany", "v": 11.0}, {"l": "USA", "v": 7.0}]}
### Trade's costs
Opening up trade makes countries richer on average, but some workers and regions lose out when factories move abroad. Tariffs, like those the USA raised sharply in 2025, push up prices and invite retaliation.
!! Trade is a contest with a winner and loser => Both sides usually gain overall; the losses fall on particular industries within each country
> Countries get richer by doing what they are relatively best at and trading for the rest.

# Inflation
Inflation is measured by tracking the price of a "basket" of typical goods and services over time. The euro area uses the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP), so countries can be compared. In Portugal, the INE publishes the figure every month.
### Why 2%?
The European Central Bank (ECB) targets 2% inflation over the medium term. A little inflation gives a buffer against **deflation**, falling prices, which can freeze spending as people wait for things to get cheaper. It also lets real wages adjust without nominal pay cuts.
ex: Prices rise 2% a year | Rule of 70: 70 ÷ 2 = 35 | Prices double in about 35 years
### What drives it
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Causes of inflation", "items": ["Demand outruns supply", "Costs rise (energy)", "Expectations of rises"]}
### The 2022 spike
After the pandemic, demand bounced back faster than supply. Then Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent gas, electricity and food prices soaring. Euro-area inflation peaked at 10.6% in October 2022, the highest since the euro began. Higher interest rates and falling energy prices brought it back near 2% by 2025.

!! When inflation falls, prices go back down => Lower inflation only means prices rise more slowly; the earlier rises remain
> Inflation quietly shrinks what each euro buys, which is why central banks aim to keep it low and steady.

# Interest rates
An interest rate is the price of borrowing money. The ECB sets the rates at which banks deposit and borrow from it. Those rates ripple out to the rates banks charge each other, then to mortgages, business loans and savings accounts.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["ECB raises its rate", "Euribor rises", "Loans cost more", "Less spending, slower prices"]}
### Euribor and Portuguese mortgages
**Euribor** is the average rate at which big European banks lend to each other. Most Portuguese home loans have historically been variable: the rate is Euribor plus a fixed bank spread, reset every 3, 6 or 12 months. When the ECB raised rates from −0.5% in mid-2022 to 4% in 2023, family budgets felt it fast.
ex: Loan €150,000 over 30 years | Total rate 1%: about €480 a month | Total rate 4%: about €715 a month
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "ECB deposit rate, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Mid 2022", "v": 0.0, "t": "−0.5"}, {"l": "Late 2023", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Mid 2025", "v": 2.0}]}
### The trade-off
• Higher rates cool inflation, but slow growth and can raise unemployment
• Lower rates help borrowers and investment, but can fuel inflation and house-price bubbles
• Savers gain when rates rise; borrowers lose
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Rates up", "items": ["cools prices", "loans dearer"]}, "r": {"t": "Rates down", "items": ["boosts growth", "savings earn less"]}}
!? Since 2023 more Portuguese buyers have chosen fixed or mixed-rate mortgages, after the shock of variable payments jumping.
> Interest rates are the central bank's main lever: up to cool prices, down to support growth.

# Supply and demand
In a market, buyers want to pay less and sellers want to receive more. The price settles where the quantity people want to buy equals the quantity offered: the **equilibrium**. If anything shifts either side, the price moves to a new balance.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Demand up", "b": "price rises"}, {"a": "Supply up", "b": "price falls"}, {"a": "Supply down", "b": "price rises"}, {"a": "Demand down", "b": "price falls"}]}
### Why food prices swing so much
People still need to eat when bread costs more, so demand for staple food is **inelastic**: it barely changes with price. That means even a small shortfall in supply must be cleared by a big price rise.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Drought cuts harvest", "Supply falls a little", "Buyers still need food", "Price jumps a lot"]}
### Real shocks
• **2022:** Russia's invasion of Ukraine threatened about a quarter of world wheat exports; prices hit records within weeks
• **2024:** disease and bad weather in West Africa sent cocoa above $10,000 a tonne, about four times its usual level
• **2010:** a Russian drought and export ban pushed wheat prices up sharply
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "cocoa price, US$ per tonne (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "2022", "v": 2400.0}, {"l": "Late 2024", "v": 11000.0, "hi": true}]}
High prices also send a signal: farmers plant more, which eventually brings prices back down. This cycle of booms and busts is common in commodity markets.
!! High prices are always caused by greedy sellers => Usually they reflect shortages or rising demand, though market power can add to them
> When supply and demand shift, price is how the market rations what's scarce.

# Why some countries are rich
In 1960 South Korea was about as poor as Ghana. Today it is one of the world's richest countries. North and South Korea share a language, history and climate, yet the South is many times richer. Differences like these point beyond geography to how societies are organised.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Long-run prosperity", "items": ["Good institutions", "Education and skills", "Infrastructure and tech", "Openness and stability"]}
### Institutions
The 2024 Nobel Prize in economics went to Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson for showing how **institutions** shape prosperity. Where property is protected, contracts are enforced and power is checked, people invest and innovate. Where elites can seize wealth, they rarely do.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Inclusive", "items": ["secure property", "fair courts"]}, "r": {"t": "Extractive", "items": ["elites take wealth", "little incentive"]}}
### The resource curse
Oil, diamonds or gold can actually hold a country back. Big resource earnings can strengthen the exchange rate, crowd out other industries, and encourage corruption and conflict over who controls them.
• **Norway** saved its oil money in a sovereign fund now worth about $2 trillion
• **Venezuela** has the world's largest proven oil reserves, yet its economy collapsed in the 2010s
• **Singapore** has almost no natural resources and is one of the richest places on Earth
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Norway", "items": ["oil wealth saved", "strong institutions"]}, "r": {"t": "Venezuela", "items": ["oil wealth spent", "economic collapse"]}}
!! Countries are rich because they have natural resources => Institutions, skills and stability matter far more; many resource-rich countries remain poor
> Wealth is built more by the rules people live under than by what lies beneath the ground.

# Inequality
The Gini index is drawn from the **Lorenz curve**: line up everyone from poorest to richest and plot the share of income they hold. If income were shared equally, it would be a straight line. The Gini measures how far the real curve sags below it.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 0.7, "unit": "Gini", "items": [{"l": "Slovakia", "v": 0.24}, {"l": "Portugal", "v": 0.32, "hi": true}, {"l": "Brazil", "v": 0.52}, {"l": "S. Africa", "v": 0.63}]}
### What brings it down
Market incomes before tax are unequal everywhere. The difference between countries comes largely from taxes and benefits: progressive income tax, pensions, child benefits and free public services narrow the gap. Education, wage bargaining and minimum wages matter too.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Market income", "Minus progressive taxes", "Plus pensions, benefits", "Disposable income"]}
### Income vs wealth
• **Income** is what you earn each year
• **Wealth** is what you own: houses, savings, shares
• Wealth is always much more unequal than income; in most rich countries the top 10% own around half of it or more
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Income", "items": ["flow each year", "Gini ~0.3 in EU"]}, "r": {"t": "Wealth", "items": ["stock you own", "far more unequal"]}}
!? Global inequality between countries fell after about 1990, mainly because China and India grew fast, even as inequality within many countries rose.
> Inequality depends less on what markets pay than on how a country taxes, spends and educates.

# The big crops
People have eaten thousands of plant species, yet today just three cereals, maize, rice and wheat, provide about half of all the calories humanity eats directly. Add soybeans, sugar cane and palm oil, and a handful of crops underpins the world's food system.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "world harvest, million tonnes (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Maize", "v": 1200.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Wheat", "v": 790.0}, {"l": "Rice (milled)", "v": 530.0}, {"l": "Soybeans", "v": 420.0}]}
### Different crops, different jobs
• **Rice** is the staple for more than half the world, especially in Asia; most of it is eaten close to where it's grown
• **Wheat** is the most traded grain, made into bread, pasta and noodles
• **Maize** is the biggest by weight, but most of it feeds animals or becomes ethanol fuel, not food on a plate
• **Soybeans** and **palm oil** supply protein meal and the world's most-used cooking oils
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Rice", "b": "staple for Asia"}, {"a": "Wheat", "b": "bread, pasta, most traded"}, {"a": "Maize", "b": "mostly animal feed, fuel"}, {"a": "Palm oil", "b": "most-used vegetable oil"}]}
### Why so few?
Cereals store well for months, carry lots of energy per hectare and can be grown on a huge scale with machines. Centuries of breeding have made them far more productive than their wild ancestors. The cost is fragility: a disease or drought hitting one crop can ripple through prices everywhere.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strength", "items": ["cheap calories", "easy to store, ship"]}, "r": {"t": "Risk", "items": ["little diversity", "one pest, big shock"]}}
!? Sugar cane is the world's largest crop by weight, at nearly 2 billion tonnes a year, but most of that weight is water and fibre.
> A handful of crops feeds the world, which makes them efficient and vulnerable at the same time.

# Where they grow
Production is concentrated in a few breadbaskets with good soil, reliable rain or irrigation, and flat land for machines. That concentration is what makes world prices sensitive to weather in places like Iowa, Mato Grosso or the Black Sea steppe.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Maize", "b": "USA, China, Brazil"}, {"a": "Rice", "b": "China, India"}, {"a": "Wheat", "b": "China, India, Russia"}, {"a": "Soybeans", "b": "Brazil, USA, Argentina"}]}
### Producers vs exporters
The biggest producers aren't always the biggest sellers. China and India grow huge amounts of wheat and rice but eat almost all of it. What matters for world prices is the **exportable surplus**.
• Russia is the largest wheat exporter, at about a fifth of world trade
• India supplies roughly 40% of world rice exports, so its export bans in 2022–23 shook Asian markets
• Brazil now produces about 40% of the world's soybeans; China buys roughly 60% of all soybeans traded
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "soybean harvest, million tonnes (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Brazil", "v": 170.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "USA", "v": 120.0}, {"l": "Argentina", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "China", "v": 20.0}]}
### Two hemispheres, two harvests
Northern farms harvest maize and soybeans around September–November; Brazil and Argentina harvest theirs around February–June. Traders watch both seasons, so a dry spell in either can move prices.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "North harvest", "items": ["USA, Europe, Russia", "Sept to Nov"]}, "r": {"t": "South harvest", "items": ["Brazil, Argentina", "Feb to June"]}}
!? Brazil often grows two crops a year on the same field: soybeans first, then a "safrinha" (little harvest) of maize straight afterwards.
> Prices are set by the few countries with grain to spare, so their weather becomes everyone's problem.

# Most soy feeds animals
A soybean is about 20% oil and 80% protein-rich solids. Crushing plants press out the oil and grind the rest into **soybean meal**, which is about 45% protein. That meal is the world's most important protein feed for farm animals.
ex: Crush 1 tonne of soybeans | About 0.79 t meal for feed | About 0.19 t oil for food and fuel
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Soybeans", "Crushing plant", "Meal → animal feed", "Oil → cooking, biodiesel"]}
### Who eats it
• Chickens are the largest users, followed by pigs, farmed fish and dairy cattle
• Only about 7% of soy is eaten directly by people, as tofu, soy milk, edamame or tempeh
• Soy oil is used for frying, margarine and in Europe and the Americas as biodiesel
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of world soy, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Animal feed", "v": 77.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Oil, fuel, industry", "v": 16.0}, {"l": "Beans, tofu, milk", "v": 7.0}]}
### The forest link
Soy expansion has driven deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon and especially the Cerrado savanna. Since 2006 the **Amazon Soy Moratorium** has stopped big traders buying soy from newly cleared Amazon land, but the Cerrado was left out. The EU has adopted a law to block imports linked to deforestation, though its start has been delayed repeatedly.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Tofu-eaters drive soy farming", "r": "Meat, eggs and milk drive soy demand"}]}
!! Vegetarians are the main reason for soy farming => Most soy ends up feeding livestock for meat, eggs and dairy
> When you eat chicken or pork, you are indirectly eating soybeans, often grown in South America.

# Commodity trading
Between a farm in Mato Grosso and a bakery in Lisbon there are silos, trucks, trains, ports, ships and mills. Global traders link them. A small group of firms, often called **ABCD** after ADM, Bunge, Cargill and Louis Dreyfus, has handled a large share of world grain trade for decades, now joined by others such as COFCO International.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Buy from farmers", "Store and dry", "Ship by rail and sea", "Sell to millers, crushers"]}
### How traders make money
Margins on each tonne are thin, so profit comes from volume and from three kinds of **arbitrage**:
• **Space:** buy where grain is cheap, sell where it's scarce
• **Time:** store grain after harvest and sell later when prices are higher
• **Form:** process it, for example crushing soybeans into meal and oil
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Trader's arbitrage", "items": ["Space: where", "Time: when", "Form: processing"]}
### Managing risk
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Price risk", "b": "market moves before you sell"}, {"a": "Freight risk", "b": "shipping rates jump"}, {"a": "Quality risk", "b": "moisture, protein off-spec"}, {"a": "Political risk", "b": "export bans, sanctions"}]}
Traders also face currency risk (buying in reais, selling in dollars) and counterparty risk (a buyer who can't pay). Much of the business is about managing these risks carefully, hedging the price while keeping the physical flow running.
!? Grain is graded on details like moisture, protein and broken kernels; a shipment a fraction off-spec can be discounted or rejected.
> Commodity traders turn scattered harvests into a steady global supply, and earn their living by managing risk.

# Futures and hedging
A **futures contract** is a standardised promise to buy or sell a set amount of a commodity at an agreed price on a future date. It's traded on an exchange, which guarantees both sides. Most futures are closed out with an opposite trade before delivery; they're used to manage price, not to move grain.
### How a hedge works
A farmer who will harvest soybeans in autumn worries prices may fall. By selling futures now, she locks in today's price: if the market falls, her loss on the crop is offset by a gain on the futures.
ex: Spring: sell futures at $400/t | Autumn: price falls to $350/t | Crop sells $50 lower, futures gain $50 | Net: about $400/t
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Hedger", "items": ["farmer, trader, miller", "reduces risk"]}, "r": {"t": "Speculator", "items": ["investor, fund", "takes on risk"]}}
### The benchmarks
The **Chicago Board of Trade**, founded in 1848 and now part of CME Group, sets world reference prices for corn, soybeans and wheat. Other key exchanges:
• Euronext in Paris for European milling wheat
• ICE in New York and London for coffee, cocoa and sugar
• Dalian in China for soybean meal and other products
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1848", "l": "CBOT founded"}, {"y": "1865", "l": "standard contracts"}, {"y": "2007", "l": "merges into CME"}]}
### Basis
The local cash price rarely equals the futures price. The difference is called **basis** and reflects transport costs, local supply and quality.
= Basis = local cash price − futures price
!! Futures are just gambling => They exist mainly so producers and buyers can lock in prices; speculators take the other side and add liquidity
> Futures let farmers and traders swap an uncertain future price for a known one.

# Shipping routes
Grain crosses oceans on **dry bulk carriers**, ships whose holds are loaded like giant buckets. A typical "Panamax" carries about 60,000–80,000 tonnes, enough wheat for roughly 100 million loaves. Freight can be a large share of the final cost of cheap commodities.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Panama Canal: US Gulf to Asia", "Bosphorus: Black Sea grain out", "Suez Canal: Europe to Asia", "Strait of Malacca: into East Asia"]}
### When chokepoints fail
• **2022:** war closed Ukraine's ports; a UN-brokered Black Sea deal shipped about 33 million tonnes before it ended in 2023
• **2023–24:** drought lowered the lakes that feed the Panama Canal, cutting daily transits by about a third
• **Late 2023 onward:** attacks on ships in the Red Sea pushed many vessels around Africa's Cape of Good Hope
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Via Suez", "items": ["shorter", "Red Sea risk"]}, "r": {"t": "Via Cape", "items": ["10–14 days longer", "more fuel"]}}
### Rivers matter too
Most US grain reaches the coast by barge on the Mississippi. In dry autumns like 2022 and 2023, low water forced barges to carry lighter loads, raising costs. Brazil moves much of its soy by truck over long distances to ports like Santos and Paranaguá, which adds cost.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Farm", "Truck, rail or barge", "Export port", "Bulk carrier", "Import port"]}
!? Portugal's grain imports arrive mostly by sea at ports such as Lisbon, Leixões and Aveiro, where large silos store them for millers and feed makers.
> Food prices depend not only on harvests but on a few narrow waterways staying open.

# Coffee and cocoa
Both are tropical tree crops grown mostly by smallholders and consumed mostly in rich countries. Coffee grows in a "bean belt" between the tropics; cocoa needs hot, humid lowlands within about 20° of the equator.

### Two kinds of coffee

### Why prices jump
Trees take three to five years to produce, so supply can't respond quickly. Brazil's frosts in 2021 and droughts since lifted arabica to record highs above $4 per pound in 2025. In West Africa, swollen shoot virus, ageing trees, heavy rain then drought cut cocoa harvests, and prices roughly tripled during 2024.

• Climate change is pushing suitable land uphill or out of production

# Food waste
The "one third" figure comes from a 2011 UN Food and Agriculture Organization estimate. Newer UN data split it into two parts: about 13% of food is **lost** between harvest and shops, and about 19% is **wasted** in shops, restaurants and homes.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Poorer countries", "items": ["loss on the farm", "poor storage, roads"]}, "r": {"t": "Richer countries", "items": ["waste at the end", "shops and homes"]}}
### Where it happens
Households are the biggest single source of waste, responsible for about 60% of what's thrown away at the retail and consumer end. In the EU, about 130 kg of food per person is wasted each year across the whole chain.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of end-of-chain waste, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Households", "v": 60.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Food service", "v": 28.0}, {"l": "Retail", "v": 12.0}]}
### The hidden costs
Wasted food carries all the land, water, fertiliser and fuel used to make it. Food loss and waste produce about 8–10% of global greenhouse gas emissions, partly as methane from food rotting in landfill.
• Plan meals and check the fridge before shopping
• Understand dates: "use by" is about safety, "best before" is about quality
• Freeze leftovers and bread; use apps that sell surplus food cheaply
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Best before = throw away", "r": "Often fine after; use your senses"}]}
!? If food waste were a country, it would be the third-largest greenhouse gas emitter after China and the USA.
> Wasting less food is one of the cheapest ways to cut emissions and feed more people.

# Fertilisers
Plants need three main nutrients in large amounts: **nitrogen (N)**, **phosphorus (P)** and **potassium (K)**. Nitrogen gas makes up 78% of the air, but plants can't use it directly; it has to be "fixed" into compounds like ammonia.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "N: nitrogen", "b": "made from air + natural gas"}, {"a": "P: phosphorus", "b": "mined rock, mostly Morocco"}, {"a": "K: potassium", "b": "potash: Canada, Russia"}]}
### The Haber–Bosch process
In 1909 the German chemist Fritz Haber made ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen. Carl Bosch scaled it up at BASF by 1913. It runs at about 400–500 °C and 150–300 times atmospheric pressure, and it still produces almost all synthetic nitrogen today.
= N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Natural gas → hydrogen", "Nitrogen from the air", "High heat and pressure", "Ammonia → fertiliser"]}
### The downside
• Making ammonia uses about 1–2% of the world's energy and emits a similar share of CO₂
• Crops typically take up only about half the nitrogen applied; the rest washes into water or escapes to air
• Runoff feeds algal blooms that create oxygen-starved "dead zones", as in the Gulf of Mexico
• Soil microbes turn some into nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas about 270 times stronger than CO₂ over a century
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Benefit", "items": ["doubles yields", "feeds billions"]}, "r": {"t": "Cost", "items": ["fossil gas", "water pollution"]}}
!? When gas prices spiked in 2022, several European fertiliser plants shut down, showing how closely food and energy are linked.
> Nitrogen fertiliser made from air and gas is one of the inventions that most shaped the modern world.

# Portugal's food
Portugal produces only about a fifth of the cereals it consumes, and much less of its wheat, so it imports wheat, maize and soybeans, largely by sea. It is strong in Mediterranean crops suited to its hot, dry summers.

### Alqueva and the olive boom
The Alqueva dam on the Guadiana river, completed in 2002, created the largest artificial lake in Western Europe, about 250 km². Its irrigation network now covers well over 100,000 hectares. With reliable water, the Alentejo has planted vast intensive olive groves in hedgerow rows harvested by machine, plus almonds and vines.

• Concerns include water use in dry years, soil health, pesticides and loss of traditional landscapes

# Primary energy mix
**Primary energy** is energy in its raw form: crude oil, coal, gas, uranium, wind, sunlight and flowing water, before it's converted into petrol or electricity. The world uses about 600 exajoules a year, roughly the energy in 14 billion tonnes of oil.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of world primary energy, % (2024, approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Oil", "v": 34.0}, {"l": "Coal", "v": 26.0}, {"l": "Gas", "v": 23.0}, {"l": "Renewables", "v": 14.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Nuclear", "v": 4.0}]}
### Why fossil fuels still dominate
They are dense, storable and easy to transport, and the whole world is built around them: engines, boilers, furnaces and pipelines. Even as clean energy grows quickly, total energy demand keeps growing too, so the fossil share falls only slowly.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Renewables, nuclear: ~17%", "Gas: ~23%", "Coal: ~26%", "Oil: ~34%"]}
### A measuring trap
Burning fuel in a power station wastes about 60% as heat. Wind and solar have no such loss. So comparing them in primary energy can either overstate fossil fuels or require an adjustment that inflates renewables. Different statistics use different methods, which is why published shares vary.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "1 kWh of coal = 1 kWh of power", "r": "About 2.5 kWh of coal per kWh"}]}
!? Hydropower is still the largest renewable source worldwide, though wind and solar together now produce more electricity than it.
> The energy transition is happening fastest in electricity; the overall mix still runs mostly on fossil fuels.

# Electricity vs energy
**Final energy** is what users actually receive: petrol in a tank, gas in a boiler, electricity at a socket. Electricity is only about a fifth of that worldwide. Most energy is still burned directly in vehicles, furnaces and heating systems.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of final energy, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Fuels burned directly", "v": 80.0}, {"l": "Electricity", "v": 20.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why electrifying saves energy
Electric machines waste far less energy than engines and burners. Switching a car or a boiler to electricity often cuts the energy needed by a factor of three or more, even before the electricity gets cleaner.
ex: Petrol car: about 6 L/100 km ≈ 55 kWh | Electric car: about 16 kWh/100 km | Roughly 3 times less energy
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Combustion", "items": ["engine ~25–30% efficient", "boiler makes 1 unit heat"]}, "r": {"t": "Electric", "items": ["motor ~90% efficient", "heat pump: 3–4 units"]}}
### Portugal's grid
Portugal produces most of its electricity from renewables, around 70% in recent years, mainly wind, hydro and fast-growing solar. Dry years cut hydro output, so the share varies. Yet oil products still supply a large part of the country's final energy, mostly for transport.
• Electric cars, heat pumps and electric industrial heat are the main ways to bring clean power into the rest of the economy
• More electricity demand means grids, storage and connections must grow
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clean electricity", "Electric cars and heat pumps", "Less oil and gas burned"]}
!! Clean electricity means a clean energy system => Electricity is only about a fifth of final energy; transport, heat and industry must switch too
> Making electricity clean is half the job; the other half is using it for things that now burn fuel.

# Solar's rise
Solar panels turn light directly into electricity using silicon cells. In 2010 they were an expensive niche; by the 2020s, new solar farms were the cheapest source of electricity ever built in sunny places. Panel prices fell about 90% in a decade.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "world solar capacity, gigawatts (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "2014", "v": 180.0}, {"l": "2019", "v": 600.0}, {"l": "2024", "v": 2200.0, "hi": true}]}
### Why it got so cheap
Solar follows a **learning curve**: each time cumulative production doubles, the price falls by roughly 20%. Mass manufacturing, mostly in China, which makes about 80% of the world's panels, drove costs down year after year.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["More panels made", "Lower costs", "More buyers", "Factories scale up"]}
### Portugal's sunshine
Portugal gets some of the strongest sunshine in Europe. A 2020 Portuguese auction set a then world-record low price of about €11 per megawatt-hour for solar power. Large solar farms are spreading across the Alentejo, and rooftop panels on homes and businesses have boomed.
### The catch
• Solar only works in daylight, so output peaks at midday and stops at night
• Too much midday solar can push wholesale prices to zero or below
• Batteries, flexible demand and grids are needed to shift energy into the evening
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Strengths", "items": ["cheapest new power", "quick to build"]}, "r": {"t": "Limits", "items": ["no power at night", "needs storage"]}}
!? Solar now makes up about three-quarters of all the new renewable power capacity the world adds each year.
> Solar became cheap because making more of it kept making it cheaper.

# Oil
The world uses about 103 million barrels of oil a day; a barrel is 159 litres. Oil is so useful because liquid fuels pack a lot of energy into a small, easily moved volume. Cars, trucks, ships and planes run on it, and it's also the raw material for plastics and chemicals.
ex: 103 million barrels × 159 L | ≈ 16 billion litres a day | about 2 litres per person on Earth daily
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "oil output, million barrels/day (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "USA", "v": 20.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Saudi Arabia", "v": 11.0}, {"l": "Russia", "v": 10.0}, {"l": "Canada", "v": 6.0}]}
### OPEC and OPEC+
OPEC was founded in Baghdad in 1960 by five producers. In 2016 it formed **OPEC+** with Russia and other exporters. By agreeing to pump less or more, they influence world prices, though high prices also encourage US shale drillers to produce more.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1960", "l": "OPEC founded"}, {"y": "1973", "l": "oil embargo, prices x4"}, {"y": "2010s", "l": "US shale boom"}, {"y": "2016", "l": "OPEC+ formed"}]}
### Portugal's oil
Portugal has no oil production of its own, so all crude is imported. Galp refines it at Sines; its Matosinhos refinery closed in 2021. Fuel prices at the pump follow the world price of Brent crude, plus taxes, which make up a large part of the price.
• Electric vehicles are starting to cut oil demand for cars, especially in China and Europe
• Trucks, ships, planes and petrochemicals are harder to switch
!! The world is about to run out of oil => Reserves keep being found; the bigger question is how much we can afford to burn
> Oil runs the world's transport, which is why its price and its producers have so much power.

# Gas and Europe
Natural gas is mostly methane. It heats homes, fuels industry and generates electricity. Until 2021 Russia supplied around 40% of the EU's imported gas, mainly by pipeline. After Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, flows were cut, the Nord Stream pipelines were blown up, and prices spiked to about ten times normal levels.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "Russian share of EU gas imports, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "2021", "v": 45.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "2023", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "2024", "v": 19.0}]}
### LNG to the rescue
**Liquefied natural gas (LNG)** is gas cooled to −162 °C, which shrinks it about 600 times so it can travel by ship. Europe built floating import terminals in months and bought LNG from the USA, Qatar, Norway and others. The USA is now the world's largest LNG exporter.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Gas field", "Cooled to −162 °C", "LNG tanker", "Terminal warms it", "Into the grid"]}
### Portugal and Sines
Portugal took little Russian gas, getting most of its supply as LNG through the Sines terminal, mainly from Nigeria and the USA. Spain and Portugal also won a temporary EU exception in 2022–23 to cap the gas price used in power plants, the "Iberian exception".
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Pipeline gas", "items": ["cheap, fixed route", "one supplier"]}, "r": {"t": "LNG", "items": ["any seller", "costlier, flexible"]}}
!? Gas transit through Ukraine to Europe ended on 1 January 2025, and the EU has agreed to phase out Russian gas imports entirely by the end of 2027.
> Europe learned in 2022 that cheap energy from one supplier can become a weapon.

# Coal
Coal is ancient plant matter, compressed and heated underground for hundreds of millions of years. It powered the Industrial Revolution and still produces about a third of the world's electricity, more than any other single source. Global use hit a record of nearly 9 billion tonnes in 2024.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "CO₂ per kWh of electricity, grams (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Coal", "v": 1000.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Gas", "v": 450.0}, {"l": "Solar", "v": 40.0}, {"l": "Wind", "v": 12.0}]}
### Who burns it
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of world coal use, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "China", "v": 56.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "India", "v": 15.0}, {"l": "USA", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Rest", "v": 24.0}]}
China and India rely on coal because they have large domestic reserves and fast-growing demand. Yet China is also building more solar and wind than the rest of the world combined, and its coal use may be near its peak.
### Portugal goes coal-free
Portugal had two coal plants, Sines and Pego. Sines closed in January 2021 and Pego in November 2021, years ahead of the original plan. Portugal was one of the first EU countries to stop burning coal for power, after Belgium, Austria and Sweden.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1985", "l": "Sines coal plant opens"}, {"y": "Jan 2021", "l": "Sines closes"}, {"y": "Nov 2021", "l": "Pego closes"}]}
!? Besides CO₂, coal smoke releases fine particles, sulphur dioxide and mercury; air pollution from coal causes hundreds of thousands of early deaths every year.
> Coal is the dirtiest major fuel, and phasing it out is the single biggest step in cleaning up power.

# Energy per person
Energy use per person is one of the clearest signs of how people live: heated and cooled homes, cars, flights, factories and goods. The differences are enormous. An average American uses roughly three times as much energy as an average Portuguese, and several dozen times as much as an average Ethiopian.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "primary energy per person, MWh/yr (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "USA", "v": 77.0}, {"l": "EU", "v": 34.0}, {"l": "Portugal", "v": 24.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "India", "v": 7.0}, {"l": "Ethiopia", "v": 1.4}]}
### Why the gaps?
• **Climate:** cold or very hot countries spend more on heating or cooling
• **Distances:** large, car-based countries like the USA and Canada use more for transport
• **Industry:** places with heavy industry, like Iceland's aluminium smelters, rank very high
• **Wealth:** richer people buy more goods, travel more and live in bigger homes
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Energy per person", "items": ["Climate", "Distances", "Industry", "Wealth"]}
### Energy poverty
About 730 million people, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, have no electricity at home. Around 2 billion still cook on wood, charcoal or dung, and the indoor smoke kills millions each year. For them, more energy means better health, education and income.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~730 million", "l": "without electricity"}, {"v": "~2 billion", "l": "lack clean cooking"}]}
!? Portugal uses less energy per person than most EU countries, partly thanks to its mild climate, but many Portuguese homes are poorly insulated and cold in winter.
> The energy challenge is two-sided: rich countries must use less fossil energy, while billions still need more energy.

# Hydrogen
Hydrogen burns or reacts with oxygen to give energy, leaving only water. But hydrogen doesn't exist freely on Earth; it must be made. Today about 100 million tonnes a year are produced, almost all from natural gas or coal, mainly for oil refining and fertiliser.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Grey hydrogen", "b": "from gas, CO₂ released"}, {"a": "Blue hydrogen", "b": "from gas, CO₂ captured"}, {"a": "Green hydrogen", "b": "from water + clean power"}]}
### How green hydrogen is made
An **electrolyser** passes electricity through water, splitting it into hydrogen and oxygen. It takes about 50–55 kWh of electricity to make one kilogram of hydrogen, losing roughly a third of the energy on the way.
= 2H₂O + electricity → 2H₂ + O₂
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Solar or wind power", "Electrolyser splits water", "Hydrogen stored", "Used in steel, fertiliser"]}
### Where it makes sense
Because making hydrogen wastes energy, it's best kept for jobs that electricity can't do directly. Using it in cars or home boilers usually loses out to batteries and heat pumps.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Good uses", "items": ["green steel", "fertiliser, shipping"]}, "r": {"t": "Weak uses", "items": ["cars", "home heating"]}}
### Portugal's plans
Portugal published a national hydrogen strategy in 2020, betting on cheap solar and wind. Sines, with its port, refinery and grid links, is the hub: Galp is building a 100 MW electrolyser at its refinery. A planned H2Med corridor would link Iberia's hydrogen to France and beyond. Many projects worldwide have been delayed, however, because green hydrogen still costs much more than grey.
!! Hydrogen will power most of our cars => Battery cars are far more efficient; hydrogen fits heavy industry and shipping better
> Green hydrogen is a precious fuel for the hardest jobs, not a replacement for electricity everywhere.

# Energy security
The International Energy Agency was created in 1974 after the Arab oil embargo showed how vulnerable importers were. Its members must hold emergency oil stocks equal to at least 90 days of net imports. The 2022 gas crisis taught Europe the same lesson again.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Energy security", "items": ["Affordable", "Reliable", "Diverse suppliers", "Resilient grid"]}
### Ways to improve it
• **Domestic renewables:** sunshine and wind can't be embargoed
• **Storage:** batteries, pumped hydro and gas storage bridge gaps
• **Interconnections:** cables and pipes to neighbours share supply
• **Efficiency:** energy you don't need can't be cut off
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old risks", "items": ["oil, gas embargoes", "price shocks"]}, "r": {"t": "New risks", "items": ["minerals from one country", "cyber attacks"]}}
### Iberia: an energy island
Spain and Portugal have weak electricity links to France: interconnection capacity is only around 3% of their generating capacity, far below the EU target of 15% by 2030. A new cable under the Bay of Biscay is being built. On 28 April 2025 a blackout shut down almost the entire Iberian grid for many hours; investigations pointed to problems controlling voltage, and the event sparked a debate about grid stability and storage.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "90 days", "l": "IEA oil stock rule"}, {"v": "~3%", "l": "Iberia–France link"}, {"v": "15%", "l": "EU target for 2030"}]}
!? China refines most of the world's lithium, graphite and rare earths, so clean energy brings a new kind of supply risk.
> Energy security means never depending too heavily on one fuel, one supplier or one wire.

# Energy transition
Past energy transitions were slow: coal took about a century to overtake wood worldwide, and oil took decades to overtake coal. Today's shift is deliberate and faster, but it means rebuilding the system that powers almost everything.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1900", "l": "coal overtakes wood"}, {"y": "1960s", "l": "oil overtakes coal"}, {"y": "2020s", "l": "clean power surges"}]}
### What it takes
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Clean power: solar, wind, hydro", "Grids, cables and storage", "Electric cars, heat pumps", "Minerals: lithium, copper", "New skills and industries"]}
Global investment in clean energy reached about $2 trillion in 2024, about twice the amount invested in fossil fuels. Electric cars made up more than a fifth of new car sales worldwide, and about a fifth of new cars in Portugal are fully electric.
### The bottlenecks
• **Grids:** connections to new solar and wind farms often take years to approve and build
• **Minerals:** an electric car needs several times more minerals than a petrol car, mostly in its battery
• **Fairness:** coal and refinery workers, like those at Sines and Pego, need new jobs; the EU funds a "just transition"
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Going well", "items": ["cheap solar and wind", "EV sales rising"]}, "r": {"t": "Lagging", "items": ["grids and storage", "heavy industry"]}}
!! The transition means less energy and lower living standards => Electrified systems waste less, so the same comfort needs much less primary energy
> The energy transition is less about inventing new technology than about building and connecting it at enormous scale.

# Fresh water is scarce
Earth holds about 1.4 billion km³ of water, but about 97.5% of it is salty seawater. Of the small fresh share, roughly two-thirds is locked in ice sheets and glaciers, mainly in Antarctica and Greenland, and almost a third lies underground. Lakes, rivers, soil moisture and the air hold only around 1%.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "where fresh water is, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Ice and glaciers", "v": 69.0}, {"l": "Groundwater", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "Lakes, rivers, air", "v": 1.0, "hi": true}]}
### Shrink it to a bucket
ex: All Earth's water = a 10-litre bucket | Fresh water ≈ 250 mL, one glass | Lakes and rivers ≈ less than 1 mL, a few drops
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Lakes and rivers: a few drops", "Groundwater: about a third", "Ice: about two-thirds", "All fresh water: 2.5%"]}
### Unevenly shared
The water that matters for people is the renewable flow, the rain that refills rivers and aquifers each year. That is shared very unequally. Canada, Brazil and Russia have vast amounts per person; much of the Middle East and North Africa has almost none.
• Lake Baikal in Siberia holds about a fifth of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water
• In Portugal, rainfall falls sharply from north to south: parts of the Minho and Gerês get over 2,000 mm a year, while parts of the Alentejo and Algarve get under 500 mm
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "North Portugal", "items": ["over 2,000 mm rain", "rivers full in winter"]}, "r": {"t": "South Portugal", "items": ["under 500 mm rain", "frequent drought"]}}
!! There's plenty of water because Earth is a blue planet => Almost all of it is salty or frozen; usable fresh water is tiny
> Earth is wet, but the fresh water we can easily reach is only a few drops in the bucket.

# The water cycle
The water cycle is powered by the Sun and pulled along by gravity. About 500,000 km³ of water evaporates every year, around 86% of it from the oceans. Plants add more by **transpiration**, releasing water vapour through their leaves.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Evaporation", "Condensation into clouds", "Rain or snow", "Runoff and groundwater"]}
### How long water stays put
A water molecule spends very different times in each part of the cycle. That's why polluted rivers can recover quickly, but contaminated groundwater can take centuries.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Atmosphere", "b": "about 9 days"}, {"a": "Rivers", "b": "weeks to months"}, {"a": "Large lakes", "b": "decades"}, {"a": "Deep groundwater", "b": "up to 10,000 years"}]}
### Energy in the cycle
Evaporating water absorbs heat; when vapour condenses into clouds it releases that heat again. This moves huge amounts of energy from the tropics towards the poles and powers storms. Warmer air can hold about 7% more water vapour for every 1 °C of warming, which is one reason heavy downpours are getting heavier.
• Atlantic weather fronts bring most of Portugal's rain between October and April
• Mountains force moist air to rise and cool, so the north and west get far more rain than the interior and south
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Atlantic air arrives", "Rises over mountains", "Cools, clouds form", "Heavy rain in the north"]}
!? Water molecules you drink today have been around the cycle countless times; some may once have fallen as rain on dinosaurs.
> The water cycle is a solar-powered machine that keeps recycling the same water, moving heat and life around the planet.

# Farming uses most
Worldwide, about 70% of fresh water withdrawn from rivers, lakes and aquifers goes to agriculture, about 20% to industry and about 12% to homes and cities. Irrigated land is only about a fifth of cropland, yet it grows around 40% of the world's food.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of world withdrawals, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Agriculture", "v": 70.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Industry", "v": 19.0}, {"l": "Homes and cities", "v": 11.0}]}
### Irrigation methods
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Flood or furrow", "items": ["cheap, simple", "half may be lost"]}, "r": {"t": "Drip", "items": ["water at the roots", "up to 90% efficient"]}}
Drip irrigation delivers water through pipes with small emitters right at each plant's roots, often with fertiliser mixed in. Little evaporates or runs off. Sensors in the soil and weather forecasts can schedule watering precisely. In the Alentejo, olive groves, almonds and vineyards irrigated from Alqueva use drip systems almost everywhere.
### The rebound trap
Efficiency doesn't always save water overall. When farmers can grow more with each litre, they often expand the irrigated area or switch to thirstier, more profitable crops. Total water use can stay the same or even rise. Real savings need limits on total extraction as well as better technology.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Drip saves water", "Farming more profitable", "More land irrigated", "Total use may not fall"]}
!? It takes roughly 1,000 tonnes of water to grow one tonne of wheat.
> Farming is the world's biggest water user, so the biggest savings must come from the fields.

# Virtual water
**Virtual water** is the water used to produce something, all along its supply chain. The idea was proposed by the British geographer Tony Allan in the 1990s, and the related **water footprint** was developed by the Dutch researcher Arjen Hoekstra.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "litres per kg (global averages, approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Beef", "v": 15400.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Chicken", "v": 4300.0}, {"l": "Bread", "v": 1600.0}, {"l": "Tomatoes", "v": 210.0}]}
### Three colours of water
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Green water", "b": "rain stored in the soil"}, {"a": "Blue water", "b": "irrigation from rivers, wells"}, {"a": "Grey water", "b": "water needed to dilute pollution"}]}
Most of beef's footprint is green water, rain falling on pasture and feed crops, which would fall anyway. Blue water, pumped from rivers and aquifers, is the scarcer kind. A tomato irrigated in a dry region may put more pressure on local water than its small number suggests.
### Trading water without pipes
When a dry country imports wheat, it's effectively importing the water used to grow it. Much of the Middle East relies on this. Portugal is a net importer of virtual water, mainly through cereals and soy for animal feed.
ex: 1 cup of coffee ≈ 7 g of roasted beans | Growing and processing those beans ≈ 140 L | About 1,000 cups' worth per day for a busy café
!! A product's water footprint tells you how much harm it does => Rain-fed water on pasture differs from irrigation pumped from a stressed river
> Every product carries hidden water, and where that water comes from matters as much as how much.

# Access to safe water
**Safely managed** drinking water means water at home, available when needed and free from contamination. By that standard, about 2 billion people still go without, and about 3.4 billion lack safely managed sanitation such as a toilet connected to safe treatment.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Safely managed: at home, clean", "Basic: within 30 min", "Limited: longer trip", "Unimproved or surface water"]}
### Why it matters
• Unsafe water and sanitation cause around a million deaths a year, mostly from diarrhoeal disease, mostly young children
• Women and girls do most of the water-carrying, often for hours a day, time lost from school and work
• Clean water and toilets were among the biggest causes of rising life expectancy in Europe after the 1850s
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~2 billion", "l": "lack safe drinking water"}, {"v": "~3.4 billion", "l": "lack safe sanitation"}]}
### Progress and gaps
Since 2000, billions of people have gained access to better water. But progress is slowest in rural sub-Saharan Africa and in fragile, conflict-hit countries. The UN's goal of safe water for all by 2030 is far off track.
### Portugal's turnaround
In the early 1990s many Portuguese homes still lacked reliable treated water and sewage connections. With heavy investment, much of it EU-funded, about 99% of tap water now meets quality standards, according to the water regulator ERSAR.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Portugal 1990", "items": ["patchy treatment", "many septic tanks"]}, "r": {"t": "Portugal today", "items": ["~99% safe tap water", "wide sewage networks"]}}
!? Clean water and sewers did more to extend human lives in the 19th and early 20th centuries than almost any medicine.
> Safe water and sanitation are among the cheapest, most powerful public health tools ever invented.

# Drought in Iberia
Southern Iberia has a Mediterranean climate: wet winters, long dry summers, and big swings from year to year. Climate change is making hot, dry years more common. 2022 was one of the driest years on record in Portugal, and the Algarve faced a severe drought in 2023–24.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Less winter rain", "Hotter summers", "More evaporation", "Reservoirs run low"]}
### The Algarve crunch
By early 2024, Algarve reservoirs were around a quarter full. The government ordered cuts of about 15% for urban use and 25% for farms. The region's water demand is pushed up by tourism in summer, golf courses and expanding avocado and citrus orchards.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Storage", "b": "Alqueva and other reservoirs"}, {"a": "New sources", "b": "desalination at Albufeira"}, {"a": "Reuse", "b": "treated wastewater for golf"}, {"a": "Demand", "b": "fix leaks, smarter farming"}]}
### The toolkit
• **Storage:** Alqueva holds over 4,000 hm³, the largest reservoir in Western Europe
• **Desalination:** a plant near Albufeira is planned to supply part of the Algarve's needs
• **Reuse:** treated wastewater can irrigate golf courses, parks and some crops
• **Leaks:** some Portuguese water networks lose a quarter or more of their water before it reaches taps
• **Connections:** pipelines can move water between basins, for example from the Guadiana
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Supply side", "items": ["dams, desalination", "reuse, transfers"]}, "r": {"t": "Demand side", "items": ["fix leaks", "suitable crops"]}}
!? Spain's Tagus–Segura transfer has piped water from central Spain to Murcia's farms since 1979, a long-running source of conflict between regions.
> In southern Iberia, drought is no longer a rare event but a condition to plan around.

# Desalination
Seawater contains about 35 grams of salt per litre. Modern plants mostly use **reverse osmosis**: seawater is pushed at high pressure through membranes with pores so fine that water molecules pass but salt doesn't. Older plants, still common in the Gulf, boil seawater and condense the steam.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pump in seawater", "Filter out sand, algae", "High-pressure membranes", "Fresh water + brine"]}
### The costs
• **Energy:** about 3–4 kWh per cubic metre with reverse osmosis, more than other sources but far less than in the past
• **Brine:** for each litre of fresh water, about 1.5 litres of extra-salty brine go back to the sea and must be spread out to protect marine life
• **Money:** usually around €0.5–1 per m³, which is affordable for cities but too much for most farming

# Shared rivers
There are more than 300 river and lake basins shared by two or more countries, holding about 60% of the world's river flow. Upstream countries can build dams or take water; downstream countries depend on what's left. This can cause tension, but shared rivers more often lead to treaties than to wars.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Nile", "b": "Ethiopia's dam vs Egypt"}, {"a": "Mekong", "b": "Chinese dams upstream"}, {"a": "Indus", "b": "India–Pakistan 1960 treaty"}, {"a": "Tagus, Douro", "b": "Spain upstream of Portugal"}]}
### Portugal downstream
Five major rivers flow from Spain into or along Portugal: the Minho, Lima, Douro, Tagus and Guadiana. Their basins cover about two-thirds of mainland Portugal, and much of their water originates in Spain. That makes Portugal heavily dependent on Spanish decisions about dams, irrigation and transfers.
### The Albufeira Convention
Signed in Albufeira in 1998, the convention commits both countries to share information, protect water quality and guarantee flows. A 2008 protocol added more detailed seasonal and weekly minimum flows. In drought years, Spain can invoke exceptions, which has led to disputes over the Tagus in particular.

# Groundwater
An **aquifer** is a layer of rock, sand or gravel whose pores and cracks hold water, like a sponge. Rain soaks down and slowly recharges it, sometimes over years or centuries. Groundwater supplies about half the world's drinking water and about 40% of irrigation.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Soil and roots", "Unsaturated zone", "Water table", "Aquifer: saturated rock"]}
### Overpumping
When water is pumped out faster than rain replaces it, the water table drops. Wells must go deeper, pumping costs rise and shallow wells dry up.
• **India** is the world's largest user of groundwater, and tables are falling in Punjab and elsewhere
• The **Ogallala aquifer** under the US Great Plains is being drained far faster than it refills
• **Mexico City** is sinking by up to tens of centimetres a year in places as its aquifer is emptied
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Pumping exceeds rain", "Water table drops", "Coast: seawater seeps in", "Wells turn salty"]}
### Seawater intrusion
Near coasts, fresh groundwater floats on denser seawater. Pump too hard and salt water moves inland underground. The Algarve's Querença–Silves aquifer, its largest, is closely watched for this risk.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Advantages", "items": ["stored, no evaporation", "naturally filtered"]}, "r": {"t": "Risks", "items": ["slow to refill", "hard to clean up"]}}
!! Groundwater is an underground river or lake => Mostly it's water held in the tiny pores and cracks of rock and sand
> Aquifers are savings accounts of water: easy to withdraw from, slow to refill.

# Saving water at home
Household water is only about a tenth of total use, but it's the part we control directly, and treating, pumping and heating it uses a lot of energy. Hot water for showers is one of the biggest energy uses in many homes.

• **Toilets:** dual-flush cisterns use 3 or 6 litres instead of 9–12
• **Machines:** run the dishwasher and washing machine only when full; a modern dishwasher uses about 10 litres, often less than washing by hand

# NATO
The North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington on 4 April 1949 by 12 countries, including Portugal, which was a founding member despite being a dictatorship at the time. Its core is Article 5: an armed attack on one member is considered an attack on all.

# International courts
The two courts in The Hague are often confused, but they judge different things. One settles arguments between countries; the other puts individuals on trial.

### The International Court of Justice
The ICJ has 15 judges elected for nine years. It sits in the Peace Palace and hears cases only when states accept its jurisdiction. # Limits of cooperation
International organisations are only as strong as their members let them be. There's no world government to enforce decisions; countries cooperate when they see benefit, and can walk away when they don't.

### Why it's hard
• **Free riders:** every country benefits from a stable climate or safe seas, even if it doesn't pay its share
• **Sovereignty:** governments resist rules that limit their freedom
• **Power:** strong countries can ignore rulings or withdraw, as the USA did from WHO and the Paris Agreement in 2025
### Why it still works

> Portugal remains culturally Catholic, even as fewer people practise regularly.

# Calendars
A calendar is a way of fitting human counting to the sky. The trouble is that the Sun, the Moon and the day don't divide into each other neatly.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "days per year (about)", "items": [{"l": "Solar year", "v": 365.24, "hi": true}, {"l": "Julian year", "v": 365.25}, {"l": "12 lunar months", "v": 354.4, "t": "354"}]}
### Why Pope Gregory changed it
The old Julian calendar was 11 minutes a year too long, so by the 1500s Easter was drifting. In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII dropped 10 days: in Portugal, Spain and Italy, Thursday 4 October was followed by Friday 15 October. Britain only switched in 1752 and Russia in 1918.
= Leap year: divisible by 4, except century years not divisible by 400
ex: 2024 ÷ 4 → leap year | 1900 ÷ 400 not exact → not leap | 2000 ÷ 400 exact → leap
### The Islamic calendar
It counts 12 lunar months of 29 or 30 days, about 354 days in all. With no correction, Ramadan comes about 11 days earlier each year and travels through all the seasons in about 33 years. Year 1 begins with the Hijra in 622 CE.
### Lunisolar calendars
Chinese and Jewish calendars use lunar months but add an extra month about 7 times every 19 years to stay in step with the seasons. That's why Chinese New Year always falls between late January and late February.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lunar", "items": ["Islamic", "drifts through seasons"]}, "r": {"t": "Lunisolar", "items": ["Chinese, Jewish", "leap month keeps seasons"]}}
!? Ethiopia uses its own calendar, about seven to eight years behind the Gregorian one, with 13 months.
> Every calendar is a compromise between the Sun, the Moon and tradition.

# Festivals of light
In the northern hemisphere, the darkest weeks come around the winter solstice, about 21 December, when Lisbon gets only about 9.5 hours of daylight. Lighting lamps against the darkness is one of humanity's oldest instincts.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Diwali (Hindu)", "b": "Oct–Nov; oil lamps, fireworks"}, {"a": "Hanukkah (Jewish)", "b": "Dec; 8 nights of candles"}, {"a": "Christmas", "b": "25 Dec; lights and candles"}, {"a": "Lantern Festival", "b": "end of Lunar New Year"}]}
### The stories behind them
• **Diwali** celebrates the victory of light over darkness, often the god Rama's return home; families clean houses, light small clay lamps called diyas and honour Lakshmi, goddess of wealth
• **Hanukkah** remembers the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem in the 2nd century BCE, when a little oil is said to have burned for eight days
• **Christmas** marks the birth of Jesus, with candles, lights and the idea of Christ as "light of the world"
• The **Lantern Festival** closes Lunar New Year celebrations on the first full moon
### Borrowed customs
Festivals mix over time. The Christmas tree came from Germany, Father Christmas from several European folk figures, and Diwali is now celebrated by Sikhs and Jains too, each with their own story.

# Greetings around the world
Greetings signal respect, closeness and status in a second or two. Getting them wrong rarely causes offence for a visitor, but getting them right builds trust fast.

# Time and punctuality
The anthropologist Edward T. Hall described two broad attitudes to time. In **monochronic** cultures, time is a line of slots: one thing at a time, on schedule. In **polychronic** cultures, time is flexible: several things at once, with relationships taking priority over the clock.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Monochronic", "items": ["schedules are firm", "one task at a time", "late = disrespect"]}, "r": {"t": "Polychronic", "items": ["schedules are flexible", "many tasks at once", "people over plans"]}}
### Examples
• Switzerland, Germany and Japan prize punctuality; Japanese bullet trains are on average late by under a minute
• Spain, Portugal, Italy, Brazil and much of the Arab world tend to treat social times more loosely
• In Brazil, arriving exactly on time for a party at a friend's home may find the hosts still getting ready
### It depends on the setting
Most cultures mix both. A Portuguese dinner invitation for 20:00 may start at 20:30, but a job interview, a flight or a doctor's appointment still expects you on time.

!! People in flexible cultures don't care about time => They value different things, like relationships and adapting to events
> Being "on time" is a cultural agreement, not a universal rule, so ask what it means where you are.

# High and low context
Edward T. Hall also introduced this idea, in his 1976 book Beyond Culture. **Context** means everything around the words: shared history, tone, status, silence and setting.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0.0, "hi": 10.0, "unit": "context (illustrative)", "items": [{"l": "Netherlands", "v": 1.5}, {"l": "USA", "v": 3.0}, {"l": "Portugal", "v": 6.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Japan", "v": 9.5}]}
### How it sounds
In a low-context culture, "This plan won't work" means exactly that. In a high-context culture, the same message might be "That's an interesting idea; it could be difficult", and listeners understand it as a polite no.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "They said 'maybe', so it's yes", "r": "In high context, 'maybe' often means no"}, {"w": "Blunt means rude", "r": "In low context, blunt means clear"}]}
### Why it matters at work
• Low-context teams write detailed contracts, agendas and emails
• High-context teams rely on trust built over meals and long relationships
• Mixed teams misread each other: one side feels insulted, the other feels confused
### Where Portugal sits
Portugal is somewhere in the middle, more indirect than Germany or the Netherlands, more direct than Japan. Portuguese people often soften criticism and value personal relationships before doing business.
!? The writer Erin Meyer found that people from high-context cultures often read more into a message than was meant, while low-context listeners miss the hints entirely.
> Low-context cultures put the meaning in the words; high-context cultures put it in the situation.

# An urban planet
For almost all of history, most people lived on farms. In 1800 perhaps 1 in 10 people lived in towns. By 1950 it was about 30%, and around 2007 the world became majority urban for the first time.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "% of people living in cities (UN est.)", "items": [{"l": "1950", "v": 30.0}, {"l": "2007", "v": 50.0}, {"l": "2018", "v": 55.0}, {"l": "2050 (proj.)", "v": 68.0, "hi": true}]}
### Where the growth is
Europe and the Americas are already about three-quarters urban. Nearly all new urban growth to 2050 will be in Asia and Africa. India, China and Nigeria alone are expected to add hundreds of millions of city dwellers.
### What counts as "urban"?
Every country uses its own definition, from 2,000 people in a settlement to 50,000. In 2025 the UN adopted a common method based on population density, which counts cities, towns and rural areas separately. Numbers shift with the method, but the direction is the same.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Farming needs fewer hands", "People move for jobs", "Cities grow and densify", "Most people live urban"]}
### Portugal
About two-thirds of Portuguese live in urban areas, mostly along the coast between Braga and Setúbal and in the Algarve. Many inland villages have lost half their population since 1960.
!? The UN estimates that the urban population grows by roughly 200,000 people a day, the size of a city like Braga.
> Humanity became an urban species only in the 21st century, and the shift is still accelerating in Africa and Asia.

# Megacities
The UN calls a city with more than 10 million inhabitants a **megacity**. In 1950 there were only two, New York and Tokyo. By 2018 there were 33, and over 40 are expected by 2030.

### Beyond Asia
• **São Paulo** and **Mexico City** each have over 20 million people in their urban areas
• **Cairo** is the largest city in the Middle East and North Africa
• **Lagos** and **Kinshasa** are among the fastest growing, adding hundreds of thousands each year
• Europe's largest are **Istanbul**, **Moscow** and **Paris**

### Size brings problems
Megacities struggle with traffic, air pollution, water supply and housing. Many in poorer countries grow faster than governments can build pipes, roads and schools. Some, like Lagos and Jakarta, also face flooding as land sinks and seas rise.
!! Megacities are mostly in rich countries => Most are in middle-income countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America

# Why cities grow
People move to cities for higher wages, more kinds of work, better schools and hospitals, and simply more choice. On average, urban workers are more productive than rural ones, even in the same country.
### Agglomeration
Economists call the benefits of clustering **agglomeration economies**. They come from three things:

### Bigger is more productive
Studies of many cities found that output and patents grow slightly faster than population, while roads and cables grow slower. That's why big cities are both richer per person and more efficient in infrastructure.
ex: City doubles in size | Output per person rises about 15% | Road length per person falls about 15%
### Costs of growth
The same density raises rents, congestion and pollution, which can push people back out to the suburbs.

> Cities grow because putting people close together makes them more productive and gives them more choice.

# Lisbon and Porto
The **Lisbon Metropolitan Area** has 18 municipalities and about 2.9 million people; the **Porto Metropolitan Area** has 17 municipalities and about 1.8 million. The cities themselves are much smaller: about 550,000 in Lisbon and 230,000 in Porto.

### A coastal country
Since the 1960s, people have moved from the interior to the coast and abroad. Most growth went to the suburbs: Sintra, Amadora, Vila Nova de Gaia and Matosinhos are now among the most populous municipalities.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Lisbon", "items": ["capital, government", "ports, tourism, tech"]}, "r": {"t": "Porto", "items": ["industry and trade", "port wine, tourism"]}}
### Different histories
Lisbon's centre, the **Baixa**, was rebuilt on a grid after the 1755 earthquake, using an early anti-earthquake wooden frame, the gaiola. Porto's medieval Ribeira grew around its river trade and has been a World Heritage site since 1996.
### Strengths and strains
• Both now attract foreign students, remote workers and tech firms
• Both suffer from high rents and long commutes for those priced out
• Inland regions keep losing people, ageing and closing services

# Informal settlements
UN-Habitat estimates that about 1.1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements. A household counts as a slum household if it lacks at least one of: clean water, decent sanitation, durable housing, enough living space or secure tenure.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["No safe water", "No proper sanitation", "Fragile building", "Overcrowding", "No legal tenure"]}
### Why they form
When cities grow faster than formal housing, newcomers build their own homes on vacant or risky land: hillsides, riverbanks, rail edges. Without legal title, they can be evicted, and authorities often don't provide water, sewers or rubbish collection.
### Famous examples
• **Dharavi** in Mumbai: about a million people on roughly 2 km², with thriving workshops and recycling
• **Kibera** in Nairobi: one of Africa's largest informal settlements
• Brazil's 2022 census counted about 16 million people in favelas, about 8% of the population
### What works
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Secure tenure", "Water and sewers", "Paved paths, lighting", "Residents improve homes"]}
Upgrading settlements in place usually works better than demolishing them and moving people far from their jobs. Medellín in Colombia linked hillside barrios to the centre with cable cars and libraries.
!? Portugal had its own "bairros de lata": in the early 1990s tens of thousands still lived in shacks around Lisbon and Porto until a large rehousing programme, the PER, began in 1993.
> Informal settlements are the housing of last resort for growing cities, and they improve fastest when residents are secure.

# The 15-minute city
The term was popularised by the Franco-Colombian researcher Carlos Moreno around 2016. Anne Hidalgo made it the centre of her 2020 campaign for mayor of Paris.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Living", "Working", "Shopping", "Health and education", "Leisure and green space"]}
### Old idea, new name
In the 1920s, planners designed "neighbourhood units" around a school. In the 1960s, Jane Jacobs praised lively mixed streets. Car-centred planning after the Second World War separated homes, offices and shops, and the 15-minute city tries to mix them again.
### In practice
• **Paris** added hundreds of km of bike lanes, closed some riverbanks to cars and opened schoolyards as neighbourhood parks
• **Barcelona** created "superblocks", groups of nine city blocks where through traffic is diverted and streets become squares
• **Melbourne** plans "20-minute neighbourhoods"
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Car-centred", "items": ["zones far apart", "drive for everything"]}, "r": {"t": "15-minute", "items": ["mixed neighbourhoods", "walk or cycle daily"]}}
### Limits
It needs enough density to support local shops and services. Rising house prices in popular neighbourhoods can push out the people it was meant to help.
!! 15-minute cities stop people leaving their area => No one is restricted; the aim is to make travel optional, not forbidden
> The 15-minute city aims to bring daily life back within walking distance, rather than banning travel.

# Heat in cities
The **urban heat island** effect makes cities warmer than the countryside around them, especially at night, when the difference in big cities can reach 5–10 °C.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Dark surfaces absorb sun", "Buildings store heat", "Little evaporation", "Heat released at night"]}
### Why concrete heats up
• Asphalt and dark roofs reflect little sunlight, so they absorb most of it
• Stone and concrete store heat by day and release it slowly
• There are few plants and little soil, so less water evaporates to cool the air
• Cars, air conditioners and factories add waste heat
### Why it matters
Hot nights stop bodies recovering, so heatwaves are most dangerous in cities, especially for older people. A study estimated that Europe's summer heat in 2022 caused over 60,000 deaths.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Street trees", "b": "shade plus evaporation"}, {"a": "Parks", "b": "often 1–2 °C cooler"}, {"a": "Light roofs", "b": "reflect sunlight"}, {"a": "Fountains, rivers", "b": "evaporative cooling"}]}
### Old wisdom
Southern cities long used whitewashed walls, narrow shaded streets, courtyards and fabric shades. Seville and Córdoba still stretch awnings over streets in summer, and many Alentejo towns are painted white.
!? On a sunny summer day, dark asphalt can exceed 60 °C on its surface, while grass nearby stays close to air temperature.
> Cities make their own heat, but trees, water and lighter surfaces can take several degrees off.

# Housing pressure
Housing prices follow supply and demand, and in Portugal demand has grown much faster than supply. House prices more than doubled between 2015 and the mid-2020s, while wages rose far more slowly.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Demand grows fast", "Few new homes built", "Prices and rents rise", "Locals priced out"]}
### Why demand rose
• Tourism: short-term rentals (alojamento local) took many central homes off the long-term market
• Foreign buyers, digital nomads and, until 2023, "golden visas" for property investment
• Low interest rates in the 2010s and more people living alone
### Why supply lagged
After the 2008 crisis construction collapsed, many builders closed, and licensing remained slow. Portugal also has a small public housing stock, about 2% of homes, compared with over 15% in the Netherlands and Austria.

**Vienna** owns or funds a large share of its housing, keeping rents moderate. **Barcelona** plans to end tourist flat licences. Each tool has trade-offs: strict rent caps can discourage new building, while building takes years.

> Housing gets expensive when demand outruns supply; lasting fixes need more homes, not only rules.

# Smart cities
A smart city uses sensors, networks and data to run services better. The idea is simple: measure what is happening, analyse it, then act, often automatically.

# The greenhouse effect
Sunlight arrives mostly as visible light, which passes easily through the air and warms the ground and oceans. The warm surface then gives off energy as invisible **infrared** radiation. Gases such as carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and water vapour absorb some of that infrared and send part of it back down, so the surface ends up warmer than it would otherwise be.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sunlight warms the surface", "Surface gives off infrared", "Greenhouse gases absorb it", "Some heat sent back down"]}
### A natural blanket, thickened
Without any greenhouse gases, Earth's average temperature would be about −18 °C. With them it is about 15 °C, a difference of roughly 33 °C that makes liquid oceans and life possible. The problem is not the effect itself but adding more gas, which thickens the blanket.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "−18 °C", "l": "Earth with no greenhouse gas"}, {"v": "15 °C", "l": "Earth's actual average"}, {"v": "33 °C", "l": "warming from the effect"}]}
### Not all gases are equal
• Water vapour is the biggest greenhouse gas, but it acts as a feedback: warmer air holds more of it, which amplifies warming caused by other gases
• Methane traps about 80 times more heat than CO₂ over 20 years, but breaks down in about a decade
• CO₂ is weaker per molecule, but a large share of it stays in the air for centuries
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1824", "l": "Fourier: air traps heat"}, {"y": "1856", "l": "Foote: CO₂ warms air"}, {"y": "1859", "l": "Tyndall measures gases"}, {"y": "1896", "l": "Arrhenius: CO₂ warming"}]}
!! A real greenhouse works the same way => Glasshouses stay warm mainly by stopping warm air escaping; the atmosphere traps infrared radiation
> Greenhouse gases don't create heat; they slow its escape, so the surface warms until balance returns.

# CO₂ is rising
Since 1958, CO₂ has been measured continuously at Mauna Loa in Hawaii, a project started by Charles David Keeling. The **Keeling Curve** shows a steady climb with a yearly zigzag: CO₂ falls each northern summer as plants grow, and rises in winter as leaves decay.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "CO₂, parts per million", "items": [{"l": "Pre-industrial", "v": 280.0}, {"l": "1958", "v": 315.0}, {"l": "1990", "v": 354.0}, {"l": "2024", "v": 424.0, "hi": true}]}
### How we know about the past
Bubbles of ancient air are trapped in Antarctic ice. Drilling cores up to about 3 km deep gives a record going back about 800,000 years. Through all the ice-age cycles, CO₂ swung between about 180 and 300 ppm. Today's level is far outside that range, and it is rising about 2–3 ppm a year.
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 150.0, "hi": 450.0, "unit": "CO₂, ppm", "items": [{"l": "Ice age low", "v": 180.0}, {"l": "Warm periods", "v": 290.0}, {"l": "Today", "v": 425.0, "hi": true}]}
### Where the CO₂ goes
Humans emit roughly 40 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year. Only about half stays in the air. Roughly a quarter dissolves in the oceans and a quarter is taken up by plants and soils.
• We know the extra CO₂ comes from fossil fuels: it carries a chemical fingerprint of old plant carbon, with almost no carbon-14 left
• The ocean's share makes seawater more acidic; surface pH has fallen by about 0.1 since 1850, which harms shellfish and corals
ex: 1 ppm of CO₂ ≈ 7.8 billion tonnes of CO₂ in the air | Yearly rise of about 2.5 ppm | ≈ 20 billion tonnes added to the air each year
!? The last time CO₂ was this high, probably around 3 million years ago, sea levels were many metres higher than today.
> CO₂ has risen by half since industry began, and the rise is still speeding up rather than slowing.

# How much warmer?
Global temperature is calculated by averaging thousands of measurements from weather stations, ships and buoys, compared with the 1850–1900 average. Several independent teams, in Europe, the US and Japan, get almost the same answer.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "°C above 1850–1900 (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "1980", "v": 0.3}, {"l": "2000", "v": 0.6}, {"l": "2016", "v": 1.3}, {"l": "2024", "v": 1.55, "hi": true}]}
### Small number, big change
A degree sounds tiny next to daily swings of 10 °C or more. But this is an average over the whole planet, all day and all year. During the last ice age, about 20,000 years ago, the world was only about 5–6 °C colder, and ice sheets covered northern Europe.
• Land has warmed faster than the oceans, about 1.6 °C on average
• The Arctic has warmed about three to four times faster than the global average
• Each of the last ten years has been among the ten warmest on record
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "One hot year", "items": ["2024: about 1.55 °C", "boosted by El Niño"]}, "r": {"t": "Long-term average", "items": ["about 1.3 °C", "what Paris measures"]}}
### Why 2024 doesn't break Paris yet
The Paris limits refer to the average over about 20 years, not single years. Natural swings, such as El Niño warming the Pacific, add or subtract a tenth of a degree or two. At current rates, the long-term average is expected to pass 1.5 °C around 2030.
!! One cold winter disproves warming => Weather varies day to day; climate is the long-term average, and that keeps rising
> About 1.3 °C of global warming is already a large shift; the ice-age difference was only about five degrees.

# Where emissions come from
About three-quarters of greenhouse gas emissions come from burning energy: coal, oil and gas. The rest comes mostly from farming and land, chemical reactions in industry such as making cement, and rotting waste in landfills.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "share of world emissions, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Energy use", "v": 73.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Farming and land", "v": 18.0}, {"l": "Industry processes", "v": 5.0}, {"l": "Waste", "v": 3.0}]}
### Inside the energy slice
• Industry, such as steel, chemicals and cement kilns: about a quarter of all emissions
• Buildings, mainly heating and electricity used in homes and offices: about a sixth
• Transport: about a sixth, three-quarters of it from road vehicles
• Leaks of methane from oil, gas and coal production add several per cent more
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Energy emissions", "items": ["Industry, about 24%", "Buildings, about 17%", "Transport, about 16%", "Leaks and other, about 16%"]}
### Who emits
China emits about 30% of the world's CO₂, the US about 13%, India and the EU about 7% each. Per person the picture changes: an average American emits about 14 tonnes of CO₂ a year, a Portuguese person about 4 tonnes. Over history, the US and Europe have emitted the most in total.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "tonnes CO₂ per person per year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "USA", "v": 14.0}, {"l": "China", "v": 8.5}, {"l": "EU", "v": 6.0}, {"l": "Portugal", "v": 4.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "India", "v": 2.0}]}
!? Cement alone causes about 8% of world CO₂, half from the chemical reaction that turns limestone into lime, not from the fuel.
> Most emissions come from how we make energy, so changing the energy system is the heart of the solution.

# Impacts
Extra heat in the climate system shows up in many ways. More than 90% of it goes into the oceans, which expand as they warm. Together with melting glaciers and ice sheets, this has raised global sea level by about 20–25 cm since 1900, and the rate has roughly doubled since the 1990s to about 4 mm a year.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "More heat", "items": ["Heatwaves and drought", "Heavier downpours", "Rising seas", "Melting ice"]}
### Heavier rain and fiercer heat
Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour for each degree, so when it rains hard, it rains harder. At the same time, higher temperatures dry soils and vegetation faster, making droughts and wildfires worse. A heatwave that would have happened about once in 50 years before industry now happens almost five times as often.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Warmer air", "More evaporation", "Drier soils, more fire", "Heavier rain when it falls"]}
### Ice and ecosystems
• Arctic sea ice in late summer covers about 40% less area than in 1979
• Swiss glaciers lost about half their volume between the 1930s and 2016
• Warmer seas cause coral bleaching; mass bleaching events now hit most of the world's reefs
• Species are shifting towards the poles and up mountains, and spring arrives earlier
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "20–25 cm", "l": "sea rise since 1900"}, {"v": "≈ 4 mm", "l": "sea rise per year now"}, {"v": "≈ 60,000", "l": "European heat deaths, 2022"}]}
!! Climate change is a problem for the future => Its effects, such as record heat deaths and sea-level rise, are already measured today
> Each fraction of a degree makes the extremes more frequent and more severe, which is why every tenth matters.

# Portugal's risks
The Iberian Peninsula sits on the boundary between temperate Europe and the dry subtropics. As the climate warms, the dry subtropical belt is expanding northwards. For Portugal this means hotter, longer summers and less rain, especially in the south and interior.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["More and hotter heatwaves", "Less rain in the south", "Longer, fiercer fire seasons", "Coastal erosion and flooding"]}
### Water and heat
The Alentejo and Algarve are most exposed. In 2022 almost the whole country was in severe or extreme drought, and in 2023–24 the Algarve imposed water restrictions as reservoirs fell very low. Portugal's highest recorded temperature, over 47 °C at Amareleja in 2003, was nearly matched by 47 °C in Pinhão in July 2022.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "North and coast", "items": ["milder summers", "floods from storms"]}, "r": {"t": "South and interior", "items": ["extreme heat", "drought, desert risk"]}}
### The coast and the forests
Much of Portugal's sandy coast is retreating. Rising seas combine with fewer sediments, because dams trap sand that rivers once carried to the sea. Places like Costa da Caparica, Espinho and Ofir need repeated sand replenishment and sea walls. Inland, hotter and drier summers lengthen the fire season; in 2017 over 500,000 hectares burned.
• The 2017 fires killed over 100 people
• Wine regions are already harvesting earlier
!? Some winemakers in the Douro are planting at higher altitudes and on north-facing slopes to escape rising heat.
> Portugal faces climate change from both sides: heat and drought inland, and a rising, eroding sea on the coast.

# The Paris Agreement
The Paris Agreement was adopted at the COP21 climate conference in December 2015 and came into force in November 2016. Almost every country joined. Unlike the earlier Kyoto Protocol, which set targets only for rich countries, Paris asks every country to set its own goals.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1992", "l": "UN climate treaty"}, {"y": "1997", "l": "Kyoto Protocol"}, {"y": "2015", "l": "Paris Agreement"}, {"y": "2023", "l": "First global stocktake"}]}
### How it works
Each country submits a **nationally determined contribution** (NDC): its own plan for cutting emissions. Every five years, plans must be updated and should become more ambitious, a process called the ratchet. A global stocktake checks collective progress.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Countries set plans", "Report progress", "Global stocktake", "Stronger new plans"]}
### Strengths and weaknesses
• Targets are not legally binding, but reporting is; it relies on transparency and peer pressure
• Rich countries pledged climate finance to help poorer ones; at COP29 in 2024 the goal was raised to at least $300 billion a year by 2035
• The EU submits one shared plan for all its members, including Portugal
• The United States has left the agreement twice, most recently with a withdrawal announced in 2025
Before Paris, the world was heading for about 3.5–4 °C of warming by 2100. Current policies point to roughly 2.5–3 °C: better, but still far above the goal.
!! Paris failed because warming continues => It bent the curve down, but current plans still fall well short of 1.5 °C
> Paris is a framework that relies on countries raising their ambition every five years.

# Net zero
Warming stops roughly when CO₂ emissions reach **net zero**: no more is added to the air than is removed. That's because CO₂ accumulates; temperature tracks the total amount ever emitted. Cutting emissions by half slows warming, but only net zero stops it.
= net emissions = emissions − removals
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Emissions", "items": ["fossil fuels", "farming, cement"]}, "r": {"t": "Removals", "items": ["growing forests", "direct air capture"]}}
### Removals are limited
Forests and soils can absorb carbon, but fires, droughts and logging can release it again. Machines that capture CO₂ directly from air exist but are small and costly, often several hundred euros per tonne. So removals should balance only the hardest-to-cut emissions, such as some from farming, aviation and cement.
### The targets
• The EU aims for climate neutrality by 2050, with a cut of at least 55% by 2030 compared with 1990
• Portugal's Climate Law of 2021 set neutrality by 2050, with the government aiming to bring it forward to 2045
• Portugal closed its last coal power station, Pego, in 2021
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2021", "l": "Portugal ends coal"}, {"y": "2030", "l": "EU: 55% cut"}, {"y": "2045", "l": "Portugal's goal"}, {"y": "2050", "l": "EU climate neutral"}]}
ex: Think of a bath | Emissions = the tap, removals = the plug hole | The level stops rising only when inflow = outflow
!? To keep a 50% chance of staying under 1.5 °C, the world has only a few years of current emissions left.
> Net zero means the CO₂ level stops rising: whatever we still emit, we must also take back.

# Solutions
Most of the technology needed already exists, and much of it is cheap. Solar power costs fell by about 90% between 2010 and 2020, and wind by about 70%. In most countries new solar or wind is now the cheapest electricity to build.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clean up electricity", "Electrify cars and heat", "Use less energy", "Cut farm and land emissions"]}
### Electricity first, then electrify
Once electricity is clean, switching other uses to it cuts emissions twice over. Electric motors and heat pumps are also far more efficient than burning fuel.
• An electric car turns about 80–90% of its battery energy into motion; a petrol car about 25–30% of its fuel
• A heat pump delivers about 3–4 units of heat for each unit of electricity
• Portugal got about 70% of its electricity from renewables in 2024, mainly wind, hydro and solar
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "energy turned into motion, % (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Electric car", "v": 85.0, "hi": true}, {"l": "Petrol car", "v": 28.0}]}
### Food and land
About a third of food produced is lost or wasted, and food waste causes about 8–10% of global emissions. Beef and lamb produce many times more emissions per gram of protein than beans, poultry or tofu. Stopping deforestation, especially in the tropics, protects one of the largest carbon stores.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Clean power", "b": "solar, wind, hydro, storage"}, {"a": "Electrify", "b": "EVs, heat pumps"}, {"a": "Efficiency", "b": "insulation, better industry"}, {"a": "Land and food", "b": "forests, less waste"}]}
!! Individual choices don't matter => Policies matter most, but choices like heating, travel and diet shape demand and markets
> No single fix exists: the big wins come from clean electricity, then using it for almost everything.

# Adaptation
Even if emissions stopped tomorrow, some change is locked in. Oceans react slowly, and sea level will keep rising for centuries. **Adaptation** means reducing harm from changes we can no longer avoid, while **mitigation** means cutting emissions to limit how bad they get. Both are needed.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Mitigation", "items": ["cut emissions", "limits future change"]}, "r": {"t": "Adaptation", "items": ["prepare for impacts", "reduces the harm"]}}
### What adaptation looks like
• Heat-health warning systems, cooled public spaces and shade trees; Portugal has run a heat-warning system for over two decades
• Fire-resistant landscapes: clearing fuel around houses, grazing, and a mosaic of farmland and mixed native forest
• Water: fixing leaky pipes, reusing treated wastewater, storing winter rain, and desalination planned for the Algarve
• Flood defences, drainage tunnels in Lisbon, and not building on flood plains
• Crops and varieties that need less water or tolerate heat
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Heat", "b": "warnings, shade, cool rooms"}, {"a": "Fire", "b": "fuel breaks, mosaic land"}, {"a": "Drought", "b": "reuse, storage, less leakage"}, {"a": "Sea rise", "b": "dunes, sand, planned retreat"}]}
### Limits to adaptation
Adaptation gets harder and costlier as warming rises. Some coral reefs and glaciers cannot be saved, and some coastal places may eventually need to be abandoned. That's why adaptation cannot replace cutting emissions.
!? The Netherlands, with about a quarter of its land below sea level, already plans its dykes and storm barriers for sea levels expected around 2100.
> Adaptation buys safety against the change already coming; only cutting emissions limits how much more arrives.

# Earthquakes
Earth's outer shell is broken into tectonic plates that creep a few centimetres a year. Where plates meet, rocks grind and lock together. Stress builds for decades or centuries until the rock suddenly slips along a **fault**, releasing energy as seismic waves. Geologists call this elastic rebound.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Plates keep moving", "Fault stays locked", "Stress builds up", "Sudden slip: earthquake"]}
### Waves through the ground
The point where slip starts underground is the focus; the spot directly above it is the epicentre. Fast **P-waves** (pressure waves) arrive first as a jolt. Slower **S-waves** and surface waves follow with stronger side-to-side and rolling motion, which does most damage.
• Instruments record about 20,000 earthquakes a year worldwide
• About 15 are magnitude 7 or more
• The Pacific "Ring of Fire" has most of the biggest ones
### Portugal's earthquakes
South of Portugal, the African plate pushes slowly against the Eurasian plate along the Azores–Gibraltar zone. The great Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755, estimated around magnitude 8.5, was followed by a tsunami and fires; tens of thousands died. Marquês de Pombal rebuilt the Baixa with an early anti-seismic wooden frame, the **gaiola pombalina**.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1755", "l": "Great Lisbon quake"}, {"y": "1909", "l": "Benavente quake"}, {"y": "1969", "l": "M7.9 off the Algarve"}, {"y": "1980", "l": "Terceira, Azores"}]}
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "≈ 20,000", "l": "quakes recorded per year"}, {"v": "≈ 15", "l": "magnitude 7+ per year"}]}
!! Big earthquakes only happen in faraway places => Portugal has had several large ones and will have more; only the date is unknown
> Earthquakes are stored-up strain released in seconds, and Portugal sits close to a plate boundary that does it.

# Magnitude
**Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source. Charles Richter created the first scale in 1935. Seismologists now use the moment magnitude scale, based on the area of fault that slipped and how far it moved, but it gives similar numbers.
@viz {"k": "terms", "f": "+1 magnitude", "items": [{"s": "×10", "m": "ground shaking (amplitude)"}, {"s": "×32", "m": "energy released"}, {"s": "+2", "m": "about ×1,000 energy"}]}
ex: Magnitude 6 to 7 = 32 × the energy | 7 to 8 = 32 × again | 32 × 32 ≈ 1,000 × the energy of a magnitude 6
### Magnitude versus intensity
Magnitude is one number for the whole earthquake. **Intensity** describes how strong the shaking felt at a particular place, using the Mercalli scale from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). A moderate quake under a city can be deadlier than a huge one offshore.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Magnitude", "items": ["energy at source", "one number per quake"]}, "r": {"t": "Intensity", "items": ["shaking at a place", "varies by distance"]}}
### The biggest
• Chile, 1960: magnitude 9.5, the largest ever recorded
• Alaska, 1964: 9.2
• Sumatra, 2004: about 9.1
• Japan, 2011: about 9.1
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "magnitude", "items": [{"l": "Chile 1960", "v": 9.5, "hi": true}, {"l": "Japan 2011", "v": 9.1}, {"l": "Lisbon 1755 (est.)", "v": 8.5}, {"l": "Algarve 1969", "v": 7.9}]}
!? A magnitude 9 releases about as much energy as a million magnitude 5 earthquakes.
> Each step up the magnitude scale means about 32 times more energy, so the top of the scale is vastly more powerful.

# What to do in an earthquake
Most injuries in earthquakes come from falling objects and collapsing furniture or walls, and from people trying to move while the ground shakes. The aim is to protect your head and neck and avoid being thrown about.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Drop to hands and knees", "Cover under a sturdy table", "Hold on until it stops"]}
### Indoors, outdoors, driving
• Indoors: stay inside; don't run for the exit, as glass and masonry fall outside walls
• No table: crouch by an inside wall, away from windows, and cover your head with your arms
• In bed: stay there and protect your head with a pillow
• Outdoors: move away from buildings, trees and power lines
• Driving: stop away from bridges and overpasses and stay in the car
### After the shaking
Expect aftershocks. Check for injuries and gas leaks; if you smell gas, open windows and leave. Don't use lifts. Use the phone only for emergencies, so lines stay free.
### Near the coast
If shaking is strong or lasts more than about 20 seconds, or the sea suddenly draws back, move quickly to high ground or inland. Don't wait for an official warning: in 1755 the tsunami reached Lisbon within about half an hour.
@viz {"k": "fix", "items": [{"w": "Stand in a doorway", "r": "Get under a sturdy table"}, {"w": "Run outside", "r": "Stay in until it stops"}]}
!! The safest place is a doorway => Modern doorways are no stronger than other walls, and doors can swing and injure you
!? Portugal's civil protection holds an annual "A Terra Treme" exercise: one minute when schools, offices and families practise drop, cover and hold on.
> When the ground shakes, drop, cover and hold on; on the coast, then head for high ground.

# Tsunamis
A **tsunami** is a series of waves made when a large volume of water is suddenly displaced, usually by an undersea earthquake lifting or dropping the sea floor. Landslides and volcanic eruptions can also cause them. The name is Japanese for "harbour wave".
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Sea floor jolts up", "Whole water column lifted", "Waves race across ocean", "Slow and grow at the coast"]}
### Fast and hidden
In deep water a tsunami can be less than a metre high, but its wavelength is hundreds of kilometres. Its speed depends on depth. As it reaches shallow water it slows down, and the water piles up into a wall or a fast-rising flood several metres high.
= speed ≈ √(g × depth)
ex: Ocean depth 4,000 m | √(9.8 × 4,000) ≈ 200 m/s | ≈ 700 km/h, as fast as a jet
### Deadly examples
• 26 December 2004: a magnitude 9.1 quake off Sumatra sent waves across the Indian Ocean, killing about 230,000 people in 14 countries
• 2011, Japan: waves over 10 m high killed about 18,000 people and flooded the Fukushima nuclear plant
• 1755: the Lisbon tsunami struck the Tagus, the Algarve and Morocco
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1755", "l": "Lisbon and Algarve"}, {"y": "2004", "l": "Indian Ocean"}, {"y": "2011", "l": "Japan, Fukushima"}]}
### Warning signs
A strong quake near the coast, a roar from the sea, or the sea suddenly withdrawing all mean: go to high ground now. There were no warning systems in the Indian Ocean in 2004; one has operated since 2006. IPMA monitors Portugal's coast.
!! A tsunami is one giant wave => It's a series of waves, often over hours, and the first is not always the largest
> A tsunami can cross an ocean in hours; the natural warning signs are often your best alarm.

# Volcanoes
A **volcano** forms where molten rock, called magma, rises from deep underground and erupts at the surface. Once it erupts it is called lava. About 1,350 volcanoes on land are considered potentially active, and around 50 to 70 erupt each year.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Where volcanoes form", "items": ["Plates pulling apart", "One plate sinking under another", "Hotspots, mid-plate"]}
### Gentle or explosive
The style of eruption depends on the magma. Runny, low-silica basalt lets gas escape easily, producing lava fountains and flows, as in Hawaii and Iceland. Sticky, silica-rich magma traps gas until it blasts out in ash clouds, as at Mount St Helens in 1980.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Runny magma", "items": ["lava flows", "Hawaii, Iceland"]}, "r": {"t": "Sticky magma", "items": ["explosive ash", "Andes, Japan"]}}
### The Azores
The Azores lie where three plates meet, with a hotspot underneath. São Miguel, Terceira, Faial, Pico and São Jorge all have active volcanic systems. Pico's summit, at 2,351 m, is the highest point in Portugal.
• Capelinhos on Faial erupted from September 1957 to October 1958, adding about 2.5 km² of new land
• Thousands of Azoreans emigrated to the US afterwards under a special law
• Hot springs at Furnas are used to cook "cozido" in the ground
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1957", "l": "Capelinhos erupts"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "Serreta, undersea"}, {"y": "2022", "l": "São Jorge quake swarm"}]}
!? The 1815 eruption of Tambora in Indonesia threw so much ash and gas into the sky that 1816 became "the year without a summer" in Europe.
> Volcanoes are where Earth's interior reaches the surface; in the Azores they built the islands themselves.

# Hurricanes and typhoons
A **tropical cyclone** is a giant heat engine. Warm sea water evaporates, the moist air rises, and as the vapour condenses into clouds it releases heat, which makes the air rise faster and pulls in more moist air. Earth's rotation makes the whole system spin.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Warm sea evaporates", "Moist air rises", "Condensing releases heat", "Pulls in more air"]}
### What it needs
• Sea surface above about 26.5 °C, warm to a depth of about 50 m
• Light winds high up, so the storm isn't torn apart
• Distance from the equator, at least about 500 km, so the spin from Earth's rotation can start the swirl
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Hurricane", "b": "Atlantic, NE Pacific"}, {"a": "Typhoon", "b": "Northwest Pacific"}, {"a": "Cyclone", "b": "Indian Ocean, S Pacific"}]}
### Wind, rain and surge
Storms are rated 1 to 5 on the Saffir–Simpson scale; category 5 means sustained winds above about 252 km/h. But the biggest killer is often the **storm surge**, sea water pushed onto land by wind and low pressure. In 2005 Hurricane Katrina's surge flooded about 80% of New Orleans.
### Portugal's encounters
The Azores are sometimes hit: Hurricane Lorenzo in 2019 destroyed the port of Lajes das Flores. On the mainland, storms usually arrive weakened. Leslie struck near Figueira da Foz in October 2018 with gusts around 175 km/h. As seas warm, such storms may reach Iberia more often.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "minimum winds, km/h", "items": [{"l": "Category 1", "v": 119.0}, {"l": "Category 3", "v": 178.0}, {"l": "Category 5", "v": 252.0, "hi": true}]}
!! A hurricane's category tells you how dangerous it is => Rain and storm surge often kill more than wind, even in low categories
> Tropical cyclones are fuelled by warm oceans, so their power rises as the seas heat up.

# Wildfires
A fire needs heat, fuel and oxygen. How fast it spreads depends on weather, fuel and terrain. Firefighters in Portugal talk of the **30-30-30 rule**: when it's above 30 °C, humidity is below 30% and wind is over 30 km/h, fires can quickly become uncontrollable.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Fire spreads fast", "items": ["Hot, dry weather", "Strong wind", "Dense fuel", "Steep slopes"]}
### Why Portugal burns
• About a quarter of Portugal's forest is eucalyptus, grown for paper pulp; along with maritime pine, it burns readily
• Rural depopulation has left fields and woods unmanaged and full of scrub
• The vast majority of fires are started by people, through carelessness or arson
• Hotter, drier summers make the fuel more flammable
### 2017
On 17 June, a fire at Pedrógão Grande killed 66 people, many trapped in cars on a road. On 15 October, winds from storm Ophelia fanned hundreds of fires across the centre and north, killing about 50 more. That year over 500,000 hectares burned, the worst in Portugal's history.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "> 100", "l": "people killed in 2017"}, {"v": "> 500,000", "l": "hectares burned in 2017"}]}
### Reducing the risk
Owners must clear vegetation within 50 m of houses and 100 m around villages. Grazing goats, controlled burning in winter and replacing plantations with a mosaic of farmland and native oaks and chestnuts all slow fires.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Clear 50 m around homes", "Break up the fuel", "Watch alerts on hot days", "Leave early if told to"]}
!? Large fires can create their own weather, building towering pyrocumulus clouds and winds that spread flames even faster.
> Fire risk comes from weather, fuel and people; we can't control the weather, but we can manage fuel and behaviour.

# Floods
Floods happen when water arrives faster than the ground, rivers and drains can carry it away. There are several kinds, each with different warning times.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "River flood", "b": "days of rain; rises slowly"}, {"a": "Flash flood", "b": "intense storm; minutes"}, {"a": "Urban flood", "b": "drains overwhelmed"}, {"a": "Coastal surge", "b": "storm pushes the sea in"}]}
### Flash floods are deadliest
In steep valleys and dry riverbeds, water can rise metres in minutes. In Madeira on 20 February 2010, torrential rain sent floods and debris down ravines into Funchal, killing about 45 people. In October 2024, extreme rain near Valencia in Spain killed over 220 people.
### Concrete makes it worse
In a forest most rain soaks in. Roads, roofs and car parks are impermeable, so water rushes straight into drains and streams. Building on flood plains puts homes exactly where rivers naturally spread.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Natural land", "items": ["rain soaks in", "slow runoff"]}, "r": {"t": "City", "items": ["concrete, tarmac", "fast, high peaks"]}}
### Staying safe
• Never walk or drive through floodwater: about 15 cm of fast water can knock you over, and about 30 cm can float a car
• Move to higher floors, not cellars or underground car parks
• Lisbon is building large drainage tunnels to protect its low-lying Baixa
!? In November 1967, a night of torrential rain caused floods around Lisbon that killed about 500–700 people, Portugal's deadliest flood.
> Floods are water with nowhere to go, made worse when we pave the ground and build where rivers spread.

# Heatwaves
Heatwaves kill more people in Europe than any other weather hazard, yet the deaths are often unseen: they happen quietly at home and show up later as excess deaths in statistics. The summer of 2003 caused about 70,000 extra deaths across Europe, and 2022 about 60,000.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "extra deaths in Europe (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "2003", "v": 70000.0, "t": "70,000", "hi": true}, {"l": "2022", "v": 61000.0, "t": "61,000"}, {"l": "2023", "v": 47000.0, "t": "47,000"}]}
### How heat harms the body
The body cools itself by sweating; evaporation carries heat away. When air is very hot or humid, this works less well and core temperature rises. The heart must pump harder to send blood to the skin, which strains people with heart or lung disease.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Body heats up", "Sweat and blood to skin", "Heart works harder", "Overheating risk"]}
### Who is most at risk
• Older people, who feel thirst less and sweat less
• Babies and young children
• People with chronic illness or on some medicines
• Outdoor workers and people living alone or in hot top-floor flats
### Keeping cool
Keep shutters and curtains closed by day and open windows at night when it's cooler. Drink water regularly before feeling thirsty, avoid the sun between about 11:00 and 17:00, and check daily on neighbours who live alone. Never leave a child or pet in a parked car, which can exceed 50 °C inside.
!! Only very hot days are dangerous => Warm nights matter too: without cooler nights the body can't recover
> Heat is a silent killer; the most important protection is keeping vulnerable people cool and checked on.

# Preparedness
Emergency services can't reach everyone at once in a big disaster. Being ready to look after yourself for the first few days makes a real difference. The EU advises households to be able to manage on their own for at least 72 hours.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Know your risks", "Make a plan", "Prepare a kit", "Follow alerts"]}
### An emergency kit
• Water: a few litres per person per day, for three days
• Food that keeps and needs no cooking
• Torch, battery or wind-up radio, spare batteries, power bank
• Medicines, first aid kit, copies of documents, some cash
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Water and food for 3 days", "Torch and battery radio", "Medicines and first aid", "Documents and cash"]}
### A family plan
Agree where to meet if you are separated and phone lines are down. Choose a contact person outside the area. Know how to turn off gas, water and electricity. Learn your local risks: coastal tsunami zone, flood plain or forest edge.
