# Oracle bones
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Ask the ancestors","Heat the bone","Read the cracks","Carve the answer"]}
China's oldest writing survives on bones. Around 1200 BCE, kings of the Shang dynasty asked their ancestors about harvests, wars and illness by heating ox shoulder blades and turtle shells until they cracked.
Diviners read the cracks and carved the question, and sometimes the result, into the bone. Those signs are the direct ancestors of today's Chinese characters.
---
The Shang ruled the Yellow River valley from roughly 1600 to 1046 BCE. Their last capital, near modern Anyang in Henan province, has produced more than 150,000 inscribed fragments, the earliest large body of Chinese writing.
### How a divination worked
Diviners carved rows of small hollows into the back of a bone or shell. A heated rod pressed into a hollow made the surface crack with a sharp pop, and the shape of the crack was read as yes or no, lucky or unlucky.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Ox shoulder blade","b":"large, flat, easy to get"},{"a":"Turtle plastron","b":"prized belly shell"},{"a":"Drilled hollows","b":"control where cracks run"},{"a":"Inscription","b":"date, question, outcome"}]}
### What kings worried about
• Will it rain? Will the millet harvest be good?
• Should we attack this enemy?
• Will the queen's childbirth be lucky?
• Why does the king have toothache? Which ancestor is angry?
One name that appears often is Fu Hao, a royal consort who also led armies. Her tomb, found intact at Anyang in 1976, held hundreds of bronzes and jades.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"150,000+","l":"inscribed fragments"},{"v":"~4,500","l":"different characters"},{"v":"~1/3","l":"of them deciphered"}]}
!? The bones were rediscovered around 1899, when scholars noticed carvings on "dragon bones" sold in pharmacies to be ground into medicine.
!! Chinese characters have not changed in 3,000 years => The writing system is continuous, but the shapes of characters changed greatly and thousands of new ones were added
> Chinese writing began as a conversation between kings and their ancestors, scratched into bone.

# The First Emperor
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"475 BCE","l":"Warring States begin"},{"y":"221 BCE","l":"Qin unites China"},{"y":"210 BCE","l":"First Emperor dies"},{"y":"206 BCE","l":"Qin dynasty falls"}]}
After centuries of war between rival states, King Zheng of Qin conquered them all and in 221 BCE declared himself Qin Shi Huang, the "First Emperor".
He ruled through appointed officials rather than nobles, and imposed one script, one currency and one system of weights. His dynasty collapsed soon after his death, but the idea of a single China lasted.
---
Zheng became king of Qin at about 13 and spent some 25 years conquering the six other major states. The title he invented, huangdi, combined words used for legendary sages and gods, and every Chinese ruler used it until 1912. Many scholars think the Western name "China" comes from Qin.
### One standard for everything
@viz {"k":"list","items":["One script for official writing","Round coins with square holes","Standard weights and lengths","Same axle width for carts","Districts run by officials"]}
Standard axles mattered because carts wore deep ruts into dirt roads, and a cart of the wrong width could not use them. The old noble domains were replaced by commanderies run by officials the emperor could appoint and dismiss.
### Harsh rule
Qin followed **Legalism**, a philosophy that kept people in line with strict laws and severe punishments. According to the historian Sima Qian, writing about a century later, the emperor ordered books burned in 213 BCE and scholars killed. Some historians think the account is exaggerated, but the regime was certainly feared. Hundreds of thousands of conscripts built roads, canals, walls and his tomb.
### The Terracotta Army
In 1974 farmers digging a well near Xi'an found pieces of a clay soldier. Excavations revealed pits holding an estimated 8,000 life-size soldiers, plus horses and chariots, guarding the emperor in death.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Found so far","items":["~8,000 soldiers (est.)","horses and chariots","bronze weapons"]},"r":{"t":"Still unknown","items":["tomb never opened","how to save the paint","rivers of mercury?"]}}
!? Sima Qian wrote that the tomb held rivers of mercury. Soil tests over the mound have found unusually high mercury levels, one reason it has not been opened.
> The Qin dynasty lasted only 15 years, but its standards and its idea of one emperor over one China lasted more than 2,000.

# The Great Walls
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"600s BCE","l":"States build first walls"},{"y":"221 BCE","l":"Qin links northern walls"},{"y":"~100 BCE","l":"Han push into desert"},{"y":"1500s","l":"Ming brick walls"}]}
The Great Wall is not one wall but many, built over about 2,000 years by different states and dynasties to guard farmland against horsemen from the northern steppe.
The famous stone and brick sections near Beijing are fairly recent: most were built by the Ming dynasty, mainly in the 1500s. Older walls were mostly made of rammed earth.
---
A Chinese government survey published in 2012 counted every wall, trench and natural barrier from all periods and arrived at about 21,000 km, more than half the distance around the Equator. Many stretches are now low earth ridges that are easy to miss.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"length in km (approx.)","items":[{"l":"All walls (2012)","v":21000,"t":"~21,000","hi":true},{"l":"Ming walls","v":8850,"t":"~8,850"},{"l":"Equator","v":40000,"t":"~40,000"}]}
### Building materials
Builders used whatever was nearby.
• Rammed earth: soil pounded in layers inside wooden frames
• In the Gobi desert: layers of gravel with reeds or tamarisk branches
• Ming sections: fired bricks and cut stone, bonded with lime mortar
Studies of Ming mortar found sticky rice soup mixed into the lime, which made it stronger and more water-resistant.
### What walls were for
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Slow down raiders on horseback","Signal with smoke and fire","Control trade and taxes","Watch who comes and goes"]}
A wall could not stop a determined army, but it slowed raids, funnelled traffic through gates and let watchtowers pass warnings with fire at night and smoke by day. It also marked where settled farming China ended. In 1644 the Manchus came through the Shanhai Pass at its eastern end after a Ming general opened the gate to them.
!! The Great Wall is visible from space with the naked eye => It is very long but only a few metres wide and the colour of the land; China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei, said in 2003 that he could not see it
> The Great Wall is a 2,000-year collection of frontiers, each one a snapshot of where China's rulers drew their line.

# The Han and the Silk Road
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Went west","items":["silk","lacquer and iron","paper (later)"]},"r":{"t":"Came east","items":["horses","grapes and alfalfa","glass, Buddhism"]}}
The Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) ruled about as many people as the Roman Empire, at the same time. Most Chinese people still call themselves Han.
Han armies and envoys opened routes through Central Asia. Along them, silk travelled west, while horses, new crops and, later, Buddhism came east.
---
In 138 BCE Emperor Wu sent an officer called Zhang Qian west to find allies against the Xiongnu, a nomadic confederation that raided Han lands. He was captured by the Xiongnu and held for about ten years, escaped, and returned around 125 BCE without an alliance but with something more valuable: detailed reports on the kingdoms of Central Asia.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"138 BCE","l":"Zhang Qian sets out"},{"y":"~125 BCE","l":"He returns with reports"},{"y":"101 BCE","l":"Han win Ferghana horses"},{"y":"97 CE","l":"Envoy sent towards Rome"}]}
### Heavenly horses
Zhang Qian described tall, fast horses in the Ferghana valley, in today's Uzbekistan. Han cavalry needed them to fight the steppe riders, so the emperor sent armies thousands of kilometres to get them. Garrisons and watchtowers were then built along the Gansu corridor to protect the caravans.
### Two empires that never met
The Romans knew the Chinese only as the Seres, the "silk people", and the Han knew Rome vaguely as Daqin. In 97 CE an envoy, Gan Ying, was sent to reach it; he got as far as the Persian Gulf or the Black Sea region and turned back. Goods, not people, made the whole journey, passing through Parthian and other middlemen.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~57 million","l":"people in the Han census of 2 CE"},{"v":"~400","l":"years of Han rule"}]}
!? A Chinese chronicle records that in 166 CE visitors claiming to represent the Roman emperor Andun, probably Marcus Aurelius, arrived by sea. They were most likely enterprising merchants.
### Ideas on the road
Buddhism reached China from India along these routes in the 1st century CE. Monks, translators and pilgrims followed the merchants, and cave temples grew up at oasis towns such as Dunhuang.
> The Han turned a military search for horses and allies into a lasting link between East and West.

# The imperial exams
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Palace exam: the emperor","Metropolitan exam: capital","Provincial exam","Local exams"]}
For about 1,300 years, from 605 to 1905, China chose many of its officials through written exams on the Confucian classics, rather than by birth alone.
Candidates memorised hundreds of thousands of characters of ancient text and wrote essays on how to govern. Passing brought prestige, power and wealth to a whole family.
---
Confucius (551–479 BCE) taught that society works when rulers govern by moral example and everyone respects their duties: children to parents, subjects to rulers, friends to friends. Han emperors made his teachings state orthodoxy, and the Sui dynasty began formal exams in 605 CE. The system matured under the Song, when printed books made study more widely possible.
### Days in a cell
At the provincial level, candidates were locked into tiny brick cells for about three days, with one board as a desk and another as a bed. The examination hall in Nanjing had about 20,000 cells. Answers were recopied by clerks before marking, so examiners could not recognise handwriting.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Four Books","b":"Confucius, Mencius and more"},{"a":"Five Classics","b":"poetry, history, rites"},{"a":"Eight-legged essay","b":"rigid essay form, from Ming"},{"a":"Jinshi","b":"top degree"}]}
### Very few winners
Pass rates at the higher levels were often only a few per cent. Men sat the exams again and again, some into old age. Cheating was a constant battle: museums hold silk undershirts covered in thousands of tiny handwritten passages from the classics.
!! Anyone could rise through the exams => In theory most men could sit them, but women were excluded and years of study cost money, so wealthy families had a big advantage
### Copied abroad
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Korea and Vietnam: own exams","Europe: admired by Voltaire","Britain: civil-service exams","China today: the gaokao"]}
Korea and Vietnam adopted similar exams. European writers admired the idea of officials chosen by merit, and Britain's competitive civil-service exams, introduced from the 1850s, were partly inspired by the Chinese example.
> China's exams made learning the road to power for over a thousand years, an early form of government by merit.

# Four great inventions
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Paper","b":"Han dynasty, ~100 CE"},{"a":"Printing","b":"woodblocks by the 700s"},{"a":"Compass","b":"used at sea by ~1100"},{"a":"Gunpowder","b":"Tang alchemists, 800s"}]}
Paper, printing, the magnetic compass and gunpowder are often called China's "four great inventions". Each one later spread west and changed the world.
In 1620 the English philosopher Francis Bacon wrote that printing, gunpowder and the compass had transformed the world more than any empire. He did not know where they came from.
---
### Paper
Early paper fragments date from the 2nd century BCE. In 105 CE a court official, Cai Lun, presented an improved recipe: mulberry bark, hemp, rags and old fishing nets, pulped, spread on a screen and dried. Papermaking reached Samarkand in the 700s, Baghdad soon after, and Europe through al-Andalus, where Xàtiva near Valencia was making paper by the 1100s.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["China, ~100 CE","Samarkand, 700s","Baghdad, ~800","Al-Andalus, 1100s","Rest of Europe"]}
### Printing
Woodblock printing carved a whole page into one block. The oldest dated printed book, a Buddhist text called the Diamond Sutra, was printed in 868 and found in a cave at Dunhuang. Around 1040 an artisan called Bi Sheng made movable type from baked clay, but with thousands of characters to cast, whole-page blocks often stayed cheaper.
### Compass
Chinese thinkers knew that lodestone points south and first used it for divination. In 1088 the scholar Shen Kuo described a magnetised needle, and by about 1100 sailors used one to navigate in cloudy weather.
### Gunpowder
Alchemists searching for an elixir of long life noticed that mixing saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal could burn a house down. Fire arrows, bombs and rockets followed.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"~850","l":"Text warns of fire risk"},{"y":"1044","l":"First written formula"},{"y":"1200s","l":"Early metal guns"},{"y":"1300s","l":"Cannon in Europe"}]}
!? China produced many other inventions too, including cast iron, the wheelbarrow, porcelain and an earthquake detector built by Zhang Heng in 132 CE.
> The four inventions spread knowledge, guided ships and changed warfare, and every one crossed Asia before reaching Europe.

# Tang and Song
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Tang 618–907","items":["cosmopolitan Chang'an","golden age of poetry","Silk Road trade"]},"r":{"t":"Song 960–1279","items":["paper money","huge trading cities","landscape painting"]}}
The Tang dynasty (618–907) ruled from Chang'an, perhaps the world's largest city, full of merchants, monks and envoys from across Asia. It was the golden age of Chinese poetry.
The Song (960–1279) was weaker militarily but richer: rice farming boomed, cities grew, and the government issued the world's first paper money.
---
Tang Chang'an had perhaps a million people in and around its walls, laid out on a grid of wide avenues with two great markets. Persians, Sogdians, Arabs, Koreans and Japanese lived there, and Buddhist temples stood near Zoroastrian, Manichaean and Christian ones. Japan copied its layout for its capitals at Nara and Kyoto.
### Poets and an empress
Li Bai and Du Fu, still memorised by Chinese schoolchildren, wrote in the 700s. Wu Zetian became the only woman in Chinese history to rule as emperor in her own name, from 690 to 705. Then the An Lushan rebellion (755–763) devastated the empire, and the Tang never fully recovered.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"618","l":"Tang founded"},{"y":"690","l":"Wu Zetian rules"},{"y":"755","l":"An Lushan rebellion"},{"y":"960","l":"Song founded"},{"y":"1279","l":"Song falls to Mongols"}]}
### The Song economy
• Fast-ripening rice from Champa (now in Vietnam) allowed two harvests a year in the south
• China's population passed 100 million
• Sichuan merchants used paper notes; the state took over issuing them in 1024
• Kaifeng and later Hangzhou may each have held around a million people
@viz {"k":"cycle","steps":["More rice","More people","More trade","Bigger cities"]}
### Painters and scholars
Song scholar-officials, chosen by exam, also painted, wrote poetry and practised calligraphy. The long scroll *Along the River During the Qingming Festival*, painted in the 1100s, shows more than 800 people going about city life in minute detail.
!? Despite this wealth, the Song paid large yearly sums of silver and silk to northern neighbours to keep the peace, and still lost the north to the Jurchen in 1127.
> Tang brought power and openness; Song brought wealth, cities and paper money that later amazed Marco Polo.

# Zheng He's fleets
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"7","l":"voyages, 1405–1433"},{"v":"~300","l":"ships in the first fleet"},{"v":"~28,000","l":"sailors and soldiers"}]}
Between 1405 and 1433, the Ming admiral Zheng He led seven huge fleets across the Indian Ocean, reaching India, Arabia and East Africa.
The voyages showed off Ming power and collected tribute. Then they stopped: the court turned inward, and China left the oceans to others, decades before the Portuguese arrived.
---
Zheng He was born around 1371 into a Muslim family in Yunnan. Captured as a boy when Ming armies conquered the province, he was made a eunuch and served Prince Zhu Di. When the prince seized the throne as the Yongle Emperor, Zheng He became one of his most trusted commanders.
### Treasure ships
Ming records describe the largest "treasure ships" as about 120–135 m long. Many historians think that is exaggerated and that 60–75 m is more likely, but even that dwarfed European ships of the time.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"ship length in m (approx.)","items":[{"l":"Recorded claim","v":127,"t":"~120–135"},{"l":"Likely size","v":67,"t":"~60–75","hi":true},{"l":"Santa María","v":20,"t":"~20"}]}
### Where they went
• Champa, Java, Sumatra and Malacca
• Sri Lanka and Calicut in India
• Hormuz, Aden and the Red Sea
• Mogadishu and Malindi in East Africa
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Zheng He, 1405","items":["~300 ships","~28,000 men","show of power"]},"r":{"t":"Da Gama, 1497","items":["4 ships","~170 men","trade and forts"]}}
!? In 1414 the fleet brought a giraffe from East Africa, via Bengal, to the Ming court. Courtiers presented it as a qilin, a mythical creature said to appear only under a wise ruler.
### Why it stopped
After Yongle died in 1424, officials argued that the voyages were hugely expensive and brought back mainly luxuries. The real threat was the Mongols in the north, so money went to walls and to the new capital in Beijing. Later Ming governments even restricted private sea trade.
!! Zheng He reached America in 1421 => This popular claim from a 2002 book has no evidence behind it and is rejected by historians
> In the early 1400s China had the greatest navy in the world, and chose to stop using it.

# The Qing dynasty
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1644","l":"Manchus take Beijing"},{"y":"1757","l":"Trade limited to Canton"},{"y":"1842","l":"First Opium War ends"},{"y":"1864","l":"Taiping revolt crushed"}]}
In 1644 the Manchus, from the north-east, took Beijing and founded the Qing, China's last dynasty. Under emperors such as Kangxi and Qianlong it nearly doubled in size and grew rich.
In the 1800s it faced rising Western powers, two Opium Wars, unequal treaties and a devastating civil war, the Taiping Rebellion.
---
The Manchus were a small minority ruling a vast Han majority. They kept many Ming institutions, including the exams, but required Chinese men to shave the front of the head and wear a long braid, the queue, as a sign of loyalty.
### The high Qing
Kangxi (ruled 1661–1722) and his grandson Qianlong (1735–1796) had two of the longest reigns in Chinese history. Their campaigns brought Taiwan, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet under Qing control, roughly setting the borders of modern China.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"population of China, millions (approx.)","items":[{"l":"1700","v":150},{"l":"1800","v":300},{"l":"1850","v":430,"hi":true}]}
### Trade on China's terms
From 1757 Western merchants could trade only at Canton (Guangzhou), through licensed Chinese firms. Europeans paid for tea, silk and porcelain mostly in silver. In 1793 a British embassy under Lord Macartney asked for wider access; Qianlong replied that China had no need of British goods.
### Opium and unequal treaties
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Britain pays silver for tea","Sells Indian opium instead","China bans opium","Opium War, 1839–42","Hong Kong ceded"]}
The treaties that followed opened more ports, gave foreigners legal privileges and fixed low tariffs. Chinese historians call the era that began then the "century of humiliation".
The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), led by a man who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus, became one of the deadliest wars in history, with an estimated 20–30 million deaths.
!? Portugal had a presence in Macau from 1557, long before Britain took Hong Kong. It was handed back to China on 20 December 1999, the last European colony in Asia.
> The Qing built China's largest empire, then struggled to adapt when industrial powers arrived by sea.

# The end of empire, 1912
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1900","l":"Boxer uprising"},{"y":"1905","l":"Exams abolished"},{"y":"1911","l":"Wuchang uprising"},{"y":"1912","l":"Last emperor abdicates"}]}
After defeats by Western powers and by Japan, reforms came too late. In October 1911 an army uprising in Wuchang spread across the provinces.
On 1 January 1912 the Republic of China was proclaimed, and in February the six-year-old emperor Puyi abdicated. More than 2,000 years of imperial rule, begun by the First Emperor in 221 BCE, were over.
---
For almost half a century real power lay with the Empress Dowager Cixi, who dominated the court from 1861 until her death in 1908. She backed some modernisation, such as arsenals and shipyards, but in 1898 she crushed a burst of reforms by her nephew, the Guangxu Emperor, after only about a hundred days.
### Shocks from abroad
@viz {"k":"list","items":["1895: defeat by Japan","1898: reforms crushed","1900: Boxers besiege embassies","1901: huge indemnity imposed"]}
The Boxers, a movement that blamed foreigners and Christians for China's troubles, besieged the foreign legations in Beijing in 1900. An army of eight foreign powers took the city, and China was forced to pay a vast indemnity.
### Revolution
Revolutionary groups, many organised by Sun Yat-sen from exile, staged a series of failed uprisings. The one that worked began almost by accident: on 9 October 1911 a bomb went off by mistake in neighbouring Hankou, police found membership lists, and plotters in the Wuchang army rose the next day before they could be arrested. Province after province then declared independence from the Qing.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Sun Yat-sen","b":"first provisional president"},{"a":"Yuan Shikai","b":"general, arranged abdication"},{"a":"Puyi","b":"last emperor, aged six"}]}
### The last emperor
Puyi was allowed to live on in the Forbidden City until 1924. Japan later made him puppet ruler of Manchukuo, in north-east China. After years in Soviet and Chinese prisons he lived as an ordinary citizen in Beijing until his death in 1967, a story told in the 1987 film *The Last Emperor*.
!? Portugal had become a republic on 5 October 1910, so both countries ended their monarchies within about 16 months of each other.
> The fall of the Qing did not end China's troubles: it opened decades of warlords, invasion and civil war.

# Temüjin becomes Genghis Khan
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"~1162","l":"Temüjin is born"},{"y":"~1171","l":"Father poisoned"},{"y":"1206","l":"Proclaimed Genghis Khan"},{"y":"1227","l":"Dies on campaign"}]}
Temüjin was born around 1162 on the Mongolian steppe. After his father was poisoned, his family was abandoned and lived in poverty, hunting marmots and gathering roots.
Through alliances, loyalty and war he united the feuding steppe tribes. In 1206 a great assembly proclaimed him Genghis Khan, ruler of all the "people of the felt tents".
---
Our main source for his early life is *The Secret History of the Mongols*, written in the 13th century by someone close to the ruling family. It tells of a hard youth: a captured boy who escaped from a wooden collar, a young husband whose wife Börte was kidnapped by a rival clan and rescued with the help of allies.
### Breaking the old tribes
Steppe politics had been built on clans and tribes that changed sides constantly. Temüjin replaced them with an army and society organised in units of ten, mixing people from different tribes so that loyalty went to the khan, not to a clan chief.
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Tumen: 10,000 riders","Mingghan: 1,000","Zuun: 100","Arban: 10"]}
### Rule by merit and law
• Commanders were promoted for loyalty and ability, not birth; some former enemies became generals
• A body of laws, the Great Yasa, governed the army and nobility, though no full text survives
• Mongolian was first written in a script borrowed from the Uyghurs, still used in Inner Mongolia
• Religious leaders of many faiths were often exempt from taxes
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Kurultai","b":"assembly that chose a khan"},{"a":"Khan","b":"ruler"},{"a":"Ordu","b":"camp, source of 'horde'"}]}
!? The meaning of "Chinggis", usually written Genghis in English, is still debated: suggestions include "firm", "fierce" and "universal".
His burial place was kept secret and has never been found. According to tradition, the ground was ridden over by horses to hide it.
> Genghis Khan's first conquest was of the steppe itself: he turned quarrelling clans into one disciplined people.

# Horse archers
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Composite bow of horn and wood","Several horses per rider","Shooting at full gallop","Feigned retreats"]}
The Mongol army was made of mounted archers. Each rider carried a powerful composite bow and could shoot accurately at a gallop, standing in the stirrups.
Each warrior had several horses and changed mounts to keep them fresh. Fast, disciplined units could encircle an enemy, retreat on purpose to draw it into a trap, and wheel round again.
---
The Mongol bow was a composite: a wooden core, horn on the belly and sinew on the back, glued together and curved against its natural bend. That stored far more energy than a simple wooden bow of the same size, which mattered on horseback, where a long bow would be clumsy.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Composite bow","items":["horn, wood, sinew","short: fits a rider","very powerful"]},"r":{"t":"Simple wooden bow","items":["one piece of wood","long to be strong","awkward on a horse"]}}
### Born in the saddle
Mongol children learned to ride at about three or four and hunted from horseback as they grew. Great organised hunts, in which thousands of riders slowly closed a ring around game, trained the same skills as war: keeping formation over huge distances and closing in on command.
### Tactics
• Light cavalry harassed with arrows; heavier cavalry with lances finished the job
• Flags, drums and later lanterns passed signals between units
• The feigned retreat drew enemy knights out of formation, into waiting archers
• Captured Chinese and Persian engineers built catapults for sieges
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Arrows break ranks","Fake retreat","Enemy chases, spreads out","Mongols turn and encircle"]}
!! The Mongols won through huge numbers => They were often outnumbered; speed, discipline, scouting and planning mattered more
!? A stone inscription from about 1225 records a shot by Genghis Khan's nephew of more than 500 m, though how it was measured is uncertain.
> The horse archer turned the open steppe into a weapon: fast, hard to catch and able to strike from a distance.

# An army on the move
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Dried curd","b":"light, keeps for months"},{"a":"Dried meat","b":"powdered, carried in bags"},{"a":"Mare's milk","b":"fermented into airag"},{"a":"Herds","b":"food that walks"}]}
Mongol armies carried little and lived off their animals and the land. Riders had dried milk curd, dried meat and fermented mare's milk, and herds travelled with them.
Without slow supply wagons they could move much faster than their enemies, cross deserts and mountains, and appear where nobody expected them.
---
Each soldier was expected to bring his own horses, weapons and food. Officers inspected kit before campaigns, and a missing item could be punished. Because the horses grazed on grass, even in winter by pawing through snow, the army needed no fodder carts, though it did need good pasture and often campaigned in the seasons that offered it.
### Planning ahead
Campaigns were prepared years in advance. Merchants and spies gathered information on roads, rivers, rulers and rivalries, and scouts rode far ahead of the main army. Several columns would march separately over hundreds of kilometres and meet at an agreed place and time.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Spies and merchants report","Scouts ride ahead","Columns march apart","They meet for battle"]}
### Surrender or else
The Mongols used terror as a weapon. Cities that surrendered usually paid tribute and were spared; cities that resisted could be destroyed and their people killed, as at Nishapur and Merv in 1221 and Baghdad in 1258. Reports of these massacres made other cities think twice.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"City surrenders","items":["pays tribute","keeps its rulers","craftsmen recruited"]},"r":{"t":"City resists","items":["siege and sack","mass killings","used as a warning"]}}
Skilled workers were often spared and sent east, which is how Persian weavers and Chinese engineers ended up far from home.
!? In an emergency, a rider could open a small cut in his horse's vein, drink some blood and close it again, as Marco Polo and others reported.
> Speed was the Mongols' secret weapon, and speed came from an army that carried almost nothing.

# The largest land empire
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"area at peak, million km² (approx.)","items":[{"l":"Mongol","v":24,"hi":true},{"l":"Russian","v":22.8},{"l":"Roman","v":5}]}
At its peak, around 1270, the Mongol Empire stretched from Korea to Hungary and from Siberia to the Persian Gulf, about 24 million km².
It is the largest continuous land empire in history. Only the British Empire, spread across oceans, was larger in total. Yet the Mongols themselves may have numbered only about a million people.
---
Genghis Khan's armies took northern China's capital, near today's Beijing, in 1215. After a Central Asian ruler killed Mongol envoys and merchants, they destroyed the Khwarazmian Empire in 1219–1221. His sons and grandsons kept going.

### Europe's narrow escape
In 1241 Mongol armies crushed a Polish-German force at Legnica and the Hungarian army at Mohi. Then they withdrew. News had arrived that the Great Khan Ögedei had died, and the princes rode home to choose a successor. Whether they would have pushed further west remains one of history's great "what ifs".
### Where the advance stopped
• 1260: defeated by the Mamluks of Egypt at Ain Jalut, in today's Israel
• 1274 and 1281: two invasions of Japan wrecked by storms, later called kamikaze, "divine wind"
• Jungle and heat in Vietnam and Java defeated steppe cavalry
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Limits of conquest","items":["Mamluk Egypt","Japan's storms","Tropical jungles","Death of khans"]}
The human cost was enormous. Estimates of deaths caused by the conquests run into the tens of millions, though all such figures are uncertain.
!! The Mongol Empire was bigger than any other ever => The British Empire was larger in total, but the Mongol Empire is the largest single unbroken land empire
> A people of perhaps a million riders came to rule a huge part of Eurasia within about 70 years.

# The yam relay
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Rider with tablet","Station: fresh horse","Next station","Message arrives"]}
To rule such distances, the Mongols built the yam, a network of relay stations across the empire, with fresh horses, food and lodging for official riders.
Messengers carrying a metal tablet called a paiza could change horses at each station and ride on day and night, carrying news far faster than ever before.
---
The system was expanded and organised under Ögedei in the 1230s. Stations were set roughly a day's ride apart for ordinary travellers, with smaller posts in between for urgent riders. Local households were assigned to supply horses, food and labour, a heavy duty that was sometimes resented.
### The paiza
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Gold paiza","b":"highest rank"},{"a":"Silver paiza","b":"senior officials"},{"a":"Wood or iron","b":"lesser errands"}]}
A paiza was a metal or wooden tablet engraved with the khan's order. Its holder could demand horses, food and guides. In effect it was both a passport and a credit card, and misusing one was a serious crime.
### How fast?
Riders on urgent business wore bells so the next station could prepare a horse before they arrived. Reports suggest they could cover around 200 km or more in a day, though such figures are hard to check.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~200 km+","l":"urgent riders per day (reported)"},{"v":"~40 km","l":"between main stations (approx.)"}]}
### More than mail
The yam carried envoys, merchants with permits, tax records and military orders, and it let a khan in Mongolia keep track of governors thousands of kilometres away. Marco Polo described it with amazement, claiming there were tens of thousands of horses kept ready.
!? The Russian word "yamshchik", a coachman, comes from the Mongol yam, and the Russian postal relay system long kept its outline.
> The yam was the Mongol Empire's nervous system, carrying the khan's word across a continent.

# Pax Mongolica
@viz {"k":"cycle","steps":["Safe roads","More merchants","More taxes for khans","Khans protect roads"]}
Once the conquests settled, the Mongols ruled most of the Silk Road routes themselves. For about a century, roughly 1250 to 1350, merchants and travellers could cross Eurasia under one political umbrella.
Historians call this the Pax Mongolica, the "Mongol peace". Goods, ideas, technologies and people moved between China, Persia and Europe as never before.
---
The khans actively favoured trade. They lent money to merchant partnerships, cut tolls on main routes and protected caravans. In return, trade brought in taxes and luxury goods for the Mongol elite.
### Who travelled
• Franciscan friars sent by the Pope, such as John of Plano Carpini (1245) and William of Rubruck (1253)
• Rabban Bar Sauma, a Christian monk from China, who visited Rome, Paris and the English king in the 1280s
• Merchants from Venice and Genoa
• Muslim scholars, doctors and administrators in China
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Went west","items":["printing know-how","gunpowder","Chinese medicine"]},"r":{"t":"Went east","items":["Persian astronomy","cobalt blue pigment","Islamic maps"]}}
### Ideas in motion
In 1267 the Persian astronomer Jamal al-Din brought instruments and calendars to Kublai Khan. In Persia, the minister Rashid al-Din, a Jewish-born convert to Islam, compiled a *Compendium of Chronicles* with the help of Chinese, Mongol and European informants, often called the first attempt at a history of the whole world.
!? William of Rubruck described a religious debate in 1254 at the Mongol capital, Karakorum, between Christians, Muslims and Buddhists, organised by the Great Khan Möngke.

Ordinary people felt the effects too. Spices, silks and cotton became more widely available in Persian and Italian markets, and Italian merchants wrote handbooks for the long journey east, listing routes, costs and how to change money along the way.
> The Mongols conquered with great brutality, then made Eurasia's trade routes safer than they had ever been.

# Kublai Khan
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1260","l":"Becomes Great Khan"},{"y":"1271","l":"Founds Yuan dynasty"},{"y":"1279","l":"Song China conquered"},{"y":"1294","l":"Dies in Dadu"}]}
Kublai, a grandson of Genghis Khan, became Great Khan in 1260 and founded the Yuan dynasty in China in 1271. By 1279 he had conquered the Song, the first non-Chinese ruler of all China.
His capital, Dadu, grew into today's Beijing. He ruled through a mix of Mongol, Central Asian and Chinese officials and used paper money across the empire.
---
Kublai's claim to the throne was contested by his younger brother Ariq Böke, who had the support of many traditional Mongols. Kublai won the civil war in 1264, but parts of the family never accepted him, and the empire began to split.
### A Mongol emperor of China
Kublai adopted Chinese court rituals and gave his dynasty a Chinese name, Yuan, meaning "origin". He kept the Mongols on top, however, ranking his subjects in an unofficial hierarchy.
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Mongols","Central Asians and others","Northern Chinese","Southern Chinese"]}
### Two capitals
Dadu was the winter capital, built on a grand grid plan. Shangdu, the summer capital on the steppe to the north, was known in the West as Xanadu and became a byword for fabulous luxury.
### Projects and failures
• Extended the Grand Canal to supply Dadu with rice from the south
• Commissioned a new script for all the empire's languages, the 'Phags-pa script, in 1269
• Two invasions of Japan, in 1274 and 1281, ended in disaster
• Expeditions to Vietnam and Java also failed
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Successes","items":["unified China","Grand Canal extended","trade flourished"]},"r":{"t":"Failures","items":["Japan invasions","Vietnam and Java","costly inflation"]}}
After Kublai, the Yuan weakened through floods, famine, inflation of paper money and rebellions. In 1368 the Ming drove the Mongols back to the steppe.
!! The Yuan were a foreign dynasty China rejected entirely => China kept much of the Yuan's legacy, including Beijing as capital and the province system
> Kublai Khan was both a Mongol conqueror and a Chinese emperor, and his capital is still China's capital.

# Marco Polo
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1271","l":"Leaves Venice"},{"y":"~1275","l":"Reaches Kublai's court"},{"y":"1295","l":"Home again"},{"y":"1298","l":"Dictates his book"}]}
The Venetian merchant Marco Polo travelled to Kublai Khan's court with his father and uncle, stayed about 17 years and returned to Venice in 1295.
Later, as a prisoner of war in Genoa, he dictated his story to a writer of romances. *The Description of the World* amazed Europe with tales of paper money, huge cities and stones that burned.
---
Marco's father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo had already met Kublai on an earlier trip. The khan asked them to come back with Christian scholars. They returned in 1271 with the teenage Marco instead, travelling overland through Persia and Central Asia.
### What he described
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Paper money","b":"printed from mulberry bark"},{"a":"Black stones","b":"coal, used for heating"},{"a":"Quinsai","b":"Hangzhou, a vast city"},{"a":"The yam","b":"post-horse relay"}]}
According to his account he served the khan on missions around the empire. The family finally left by sea, escorting a Mongol princess to marry the ruler of Persia, and reached Venice in 1295 after about 24 years away.
### Did he really go?
Some scholars have doubted it. Chinese records do not mention him, and his book says nothing of tea, chopsticks or the Great Wall. But most historians now think he did go, because many details check out, such as the salt tax figures, paper money and the princess's voyage, which matches Persian records.

### A best-seller before printing
The book was copied by hand into Latin, French, Italian and other languages, and about 150 manuscripts survive, each slightly different. Many readers treated it as a collection of tall tales, and in Italian it was nicknamed *Il Milione*, perhaps mocking its "million" marvels.
!? Christopher Columbus owned a copy of Marco Polo's book and filled its margins with notes. It is still kept in Seville.
> Marco Polo's book gave Europe its first detailed picture of China, and inspired explorers looking for a way to reach it by sea.

# Plague on the trade routes
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Central Asia, 1338","Golden Horde lands","Caffa, Crimea, 1346","Ships to Sicily, 1347","Europe and Middle East"]}
The Black Death probably began in Central Asia in the 1330s. The same Mongol-era trade routes that carried silk and spices helped carry the plague bacterium west.
By 1346 it had reached the Black Sea, where Italian merchants traded with the Mongol Golden Horde. Their ships then brought it to the Mediterranean.
---
In 2022 scientists studied DNA from people buried in 1338–1339 in two Christian cemeteries near Lake Issyk-Kul, in today's Kyrgyzstan. Their gravestones mention a "pestilence", and the bacteria found were close to the ancestor of the Black Death strains. The disease almost certainly started in wild rodents in this region.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"1338","l":"earliest dated victims"},{"v":"~5,000 km","l":"to Crimea (approx.)"}]}
### The siege of Caffa
In 1346 a Golden Horde army besieged the Genoese trading port of Caffa in Crimea. An Italian writer, who was not there himself, claimed the attackers catapulted corpses over the walls to spread the disease. Whether or not that happened, Genoese ships leaving Caffa and other Black Sea ports carried infected rats, fleas and people.
!! The Mongols started the Black Death on purpose => Even if bodies were thrown at Caffa, plague spread along many routes with rodents, fleas and travellers
### A shock to the Mongol world
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Cities of the Golden Horde hit","Egypt and Syria devastated","Caravan trade collapses","Khanates weakened"]}
The plague killed huge numbers in the Middle East and the Mongol lands, not only in Europe. Trade fell, tax income shrank, and the open routes of the Pax Mongolica closed as the khanates fought each other and broke up.
Historians still debate whether the plague also struck China in the 1330s and 1340s. Chinese records describe terrible epidemics in those years, but they do not say clearly which disease it was.
> The connected world the Mongols created carried disease as easily as silk.

# Break-up and legacy
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Mongol Empire","items":["Yuan: China","Golden Horde: Russia","Chagatai: Central Asia","Ilkhanate: Persia"]}
After 1260 the empire split into four khanates ruled by different branches of Genghis Khan's family, often at war with each other.
They gradually broke up or were absorbed, but the Mongols left a deep mark: on Russia, China and Iran, on trade between East and West, and on the very idea of a world connected from end to end.
---
Each khanate adapted to the people it ruled. The Ilkhans of Persia became Muslims after Ghazan converted in 1295, and the Golden Horde followed under Özbeg in the early 1300s. The Yuan in China leaned towards Tibetan Buddhism.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1335","l":"Ilkhanate collapses"},{"y":"1368","l":"Yuan driven from China"},{"y":"1480","l":"Moscow ends Horde rule"},{"y":"1526","l":"Mughal Empire founded"}]}
### Successor states
• Timur (Tamerlane) built a new empire in Central Asia in the late 1300s, claiming Mongol links
• Babur, descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan, founded the Mughal Empire in India; "Mughal" means Mongol
• The Crimean Khanate, a Golden Horde successor, lasted until 1783
### What remained
Moscow rose as tax collector for the Golden Horde before throwing it off. Beijing stayed China's capital. Iran's Ilkhans encouraged Persian painting and history writing.
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Beijing as China's capital","The rise of Moscow","Trade and ideas across Eurasia","Words like 'horde'"]}
!? A 2003 genetic study suggested that about 0.5% of men worldwide share a Y-chromosome lineage that may trace back to Genghis Khan's family, though the link is debated.
In Mongolia today Genghis Khan is a national hero, on banknotes and the airport's name, while in Iran, Russia and China he is remembered far more darkly.
The Mongols also left new administrative habits behind: censuses, relay posts and the use of paper money spread through the lands they had ruled, and China's system of provinces dates from the Yuan.
> The Mongol Empire lasted barely a century as one state, but it rewired Eurasia for centuries afterwards.

# Built on maize and potatoes
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Mesoamerica","items":["maize","beans and squash","cacao and chillies"]},"r":{"t":"Andes","items":["potatoes","quinoa","llamas and alpacas"]}}
The civilisations of the Americas grew from crops domesticated there thousands of years earlier. Maize came from a wild grass in Mexico; potatoes from the high Andes.
These plants fed cities, armies and priests, just as wheat and rice did in Eurasia. Today maize and potatoes are among the most important foods on Earth.
---
Maize descends from teosinte, a wild grass of the Balsas river valley in south-west Mexico. About 9,000 years ago people began choosing seeds from the best plants. The earliest cobs were only a few centimetres long; over thousands of years farmers bred them into large ears that cannot even seed themselves without human help.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Wild teosinte","Farmers pick best seeds","Bigger cobs","Modern maize"]}
### The three sisters
Mesoamerican farmers often grew maize, beans and squash together in a field called a milpa. The maize stalk supports the climbing beans, the beans add nitrogen to the soil, and squash leaves shade out weeds and keep moisture in.
### Cooking with lime
Maize was soaked and cooked with lime or wood ash, a process now called **nixtamalisation**. It softens the grain for tortillas and releases niacin, a B vitamin. When maize spread to Europe without this step, poor people who lived mainly on it developed a disease called pellagra.
### Potatoes of the Andes
Potatoes were domesticated near Lake Titicaca, on today's border of Peru and Bolivia, perhaps 7,000–10,000 years ago. Andean farmers grow thousands of varieties at different altitudes. They also invented chuño: potatoes frozen at night, trodden to squeeze out water and dried in the sun, which keeps for years.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~9,000","l":"years since maize began"},{"v":"4,000+","l":"native potato varieties"}]}
!? In the Maya creation story, the *Popol Vuh*, the gods finally succeed in making human beings out of maize dough.
> Every great American civilisation rested on a crop that farmers had spent thousands of years shaping.

# Maya cities
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"~500 BCE","l":"Early cities: El Mirador"},{"y":"250 CE","l":"Classic period begins"},{"y":"~800","l":"Tikal at its height"},{"y":"~900","l":"Southern cities emptied"}]}
The Maya built hundreds of cities in what is now southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras, with stepped pyramids, palaces, plazas and ball courts.
They were never one empire. Rival city-states such as Tikal and Calakmul, each ruled by a divine king, fought and allied with each other for centuries.
---
Maya cities were not packed inside walls like European towns. A ceremonial centre of temples and palaces was surrounded by houses, gardens, fields and reservoirs spreading over many square kilometres.
### Hidden by the forest
In 2018 archaeologists used **lidar**, laser scanning from aircraft, to look through the forest of northern Guatemala. It revealed more than 60,000 previously unknown structures: houses, causeways, terraces, canals and defensive walls. Population estimates for the Maya lowlands at their peak rose to several million people.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Tikal","b":"Guatemala, Temple IV ~65 m"},{"a":"Palenque","b":"Mexico, King Pakal's tomb"},{"a":"Copán","b":"Honduras, carved stelae"},{"a":"Chichén Itzá","b":"Yucatán, later city"}]}
### The ballgame
Almost every city had a ball court. Players kept a heavy solid rubber ball in the air using their hips, never their hands. The game could be sport, ritual or both, and sometimes ended in sacrifice. Mesoamericans were making rubber from tree sap thousands of years before Europeans.
### The Classic collapse
In the 800s and 900s, many southern cities were abandoned. Lake sediments show a series of severe droughts, which came on top of warfare between cities, deforestation and growing populations. Northern cities such as Chichén Itzá flourished for a while longer.
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Repeated severe droughts","Endless wars between cities","Forest cleared, soil eroded","Kings lose authority"]}
!! The Maya disappeared => Cities were abandoned, but the people stayed; millions of Maya live in the region today
> The Maya world was a network of rival cities, much like ancient Greece, set in tropical forest.

# Maya writing
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Logograms","items":["a sign for a word","e.g. 'jaguar'"]},"r":{"t":"Syllables","items":["a sign for a sound","ba, ka, ma..."]}}
The Maya developed the most complete writing system in the ancient Americas, with signs for whole words and signs for syllables.
They carved it on stone monuments, painted it on pottery and wrote books on bark paper. After years of work, scholars can now read most surviving texts: they record kings, wars, births and rituals.
---
Maya scribes could write the same word in several ways: with one word sign, with syllable signs spelling it out, or with a mixture. This flexibility made the script beautiful but hard to crack.
### Burned books
Maya books, called codices, were folded screens of bark paper coated with fine plaster. Spanish missionaries saw them as works of idolatry. In 1562, at Maní in Yucatán, the friar Diego de Landa burned dozens of them.
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Dresden Codex","Madrid Codex","Paris Codex","Maya Codex of Mexico"]}
Only four codices are known to survive, named after the cities where they ended up. The fourth, in Mexico, was confirmed as genuine in 2016.
### Cracking the code
• 1950s: the Soviet scholar Yuri Knorozov argued many signs stood for syllables
• 1960: Tatiana Proskouriakoff showed that monuments recorded the lives of real rulers
• 1970s onwards: teams worked out the dynasties of Palenque and other cities
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1562","l":"Landa burns codices"},{"y":"1952","l":"Knorozov: syllables"},{"y":"1960","l":"Inscriptions = history"},{"y":"Today","l":"Most texts readable"}]}
### Who wrote
Scribes were high-ranking specialists, sometimes members of royal families, and painted vases show them at work with brushes and shell ink pots. Texts were meant to be seen as well as read: on a single monument, carving and writing form one design.
!? Landa, who burned the books, also wrote down what he thought was the Maya "alphabet". It was confused, but it later gave decipherers a vital clue.
> Maya inscriptions turned silent ruins into history, with names, dates and quarrels of real kings.

# The Long Count and zero
type: fact
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Dot","b":"one"},{"a":"Bar","b":"five"},{"a":"Shell","b":"zero"}]}
Maya numbers used just three signs: a dot for one, a bar for five and a shell for zero. They counted in twenties, not tens.
With them they kept several calendars, including the Long Count, which numbered days from a starting point in 3114 BCE. It needed a true zero, which the Maya invented independently of India.
---
The Maya tracked time with interlocking cycles. A sacred calendar of 260 days ran alongside a solar year of 365 days. The two came back into line only every 52 years, a period known as the Calendar Round.
@viz {"k":"cycle","steps":["260-day sacred count","365-day solar year","Both realign","Every 52 years"]}
### How the Long Count works
The Long Count counted days in place-value units, mostly in twenties. A date was written as five numbers, largest unit first.
• k'in: 1 day
• winal: 20 days
• tun: 360 days
• k'atun: 7,200 days, about 20 years
• b'ak'tun: 144,000 days, about 394 years
ex: Date 9.12.11.5.18 | 9×144,000 + 12×7,200 + 11×360 + 5×20 + 18 | = 1,386,478 days since the start, a date in 683 CE
### Zero
A place-value system needs a sign for "nothing in this place". The oldest known Long Count dates, from the 1st century BCE in southern Mexico, already imply a zero. Only a few cultures, including Babylon and India, ever invented such a placeholder.
!! The Maya predicted the end of the world in 2012 => 21 December 2012 simply completed a large cycle of 13 b'ak'tun; Maya texts mention dates thousands of years later
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"584","l":"days: Maya Venus cycle"},{"v":"583.9","l":"days: modern value"}]}
> Maya astronomers built a calendar of astonishing precision, with a zero at its heart.

# Tenochtitlan
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Island in a lake, ~1325","Causeways to the shore","Floating gardens grow","Great city by 1519"]}
The Mexica, better known as the Aztecs, founded Tenochtitlan around 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco. By 1519 it may have had over 200,000 people, larger than any city in Spain.
It was fed by chinampas: narrow fields built up from lake mud, separated by canals. They could give several harvests a year.
---
According to legend, the Mexica were told to settle where they saw an eagle on a cactus eating a snake. That scene is now on the flag of Mexico. In 1428 Tenochtitlan joined two neighbouring cities in a Triple Alliance that came to dominate central Mexico, collecting tribute from hundreds of towns.
### A city on water
• Three causeways linked the island to the shore
• An aqueduct brought fresh water from springs at Chapultepec
• Canals served as streets for thousands of canoes
• The Templo Mayor, a double pyramid, stood at the centre
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Crops: maize, beans, flowers","Rich lake mud","Mats of reeds and plants","Shallow lake bed"]}
### How chinampas worked
Farmers staked out long rectangles in the shallow lake, piled up layers of vegetation and mud, and planted willow trees at the edges whose roots held the soil. Canals kept the fields watered and brought fresh mud. Seedlings were raised in nurseries and transplanted.
### Sacrifice and markets
The Mexica believed the gods needed human blood to keep the sun moving, and they sacrificed prisoners of war. Excavations since 2015 near the Templo Mayor have uncovered racks and towers holding hundreds of skulls. Yet daily life centred on trade: Cortés wrote that the market of Tlatelolco drew tens of thousands of people a day, using cacao beans as small change.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Cacao beans","b":"small change"},{"a":"Cotton cloaks","b":"bigger payments"},{"a":"Tribute","b":"feathers, gold, maize"}]}
!? Chinampas are still farmed at Xochimilco in Mexico City, a UNESCO World Heritage site, while the rest of the city sits on the drained lake bed and is slowly sinking.
> Tenochtitlan was one of the world's great cities, built on water and fed by fields made by hand.

# The Inca road network
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~30,000 km","l":"of Inca roads"},{"v":"~4,000 km","l":"north to south"},{"v":"6","l":"countries today"}]}
The Inca Empire, ruled from Cusco, was the largest in the Americas, stretching along the Andes from Ecuador to Chile. A road network of about 30,000 km held it together.
The Inca had no wheeled transport and no horses. Roads carried people on foot, llama caravans and relay runners who passed messages from station to station.
---
The Inca called their empire Tawantinsuyu, "the four parts together". It expanded rapidly from the 1430s under Pachacuti and his successors, absorbing many peoples in less than a century.
### Roads through the mountains
The road system, the Qhapaq Ñan, had two main routes, one in the highlands and one along the coast, linked by cross-roads. Builders cut stairways into cliffs, paved wet ground and built drainage channels. Since 2014 it has been a UNESCO World Heritage site shared by Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, Colombia, Chile and Argentina.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Chasqui","b":"relay runner"},{"a":"Tambo","b":"way station with stores"},{"a":"Mit'a","b":"labour owed to the state"}]}
### Runners and storehouses
Chasqui runners waited at small posts a few kilometres apart and ran messages, quipus and light goods to the next post. Spanish chroniclers wrote that fresh fish could reach Cusco from the coast in about two days. Tambos along the roads stored food, cloth and weapons for travelling armies and officials.
### Grass bridges
Over deep gorges, villagers wove suspension bridges from grass ropes. Each community was responsible for its own bridge.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Families twist grass cord","Cords braided into cables","Cables stretched over gorge","Deck and handrails woven"]}
!? The Q'eswachaka bridge in southern Peru is still rewoven by local communities every year in a few days, a tradition recognised by UNESCO.
> The Inca ran an empire without money, wheels or writing, held together by roads, runners and labour.

# Quipu: knots that count
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Main cord","Pendant cords hang down","Knots set by position","Colours mark categories"]}
The Inca had no writing in the usual sense. Officials kept records with quipus: bundles of coloured strings tied with knots.
The position and type of each knot stood for a number, in a base-ten system. Quipus recorded censuses, taxes and storehouse contents across the empire. Whether some also recorded words is still debated.
---
A quipu usually has a thick main cord with dozens or even hundreds of thinner cords hanging from it, made of cotton or llama and alpaca wool. Specialists called quipucamayocs made and read them, and could report figures to officials from memory with their help.
### Reading the numbers
Knots near the bottom of a cord are units, higher up are tens, then hundreds. An empty space means zero. Long knots with several turns show units from 2 to 9.
ex: Top cluster: 3 single knots = 300 | Middle: no knot = 0 tens | Bottom: long knot of 5 turns = 5 | Cord reads 305
@viz {"k":"terms","f":"3 · 0 · 5 = 305","items":[{"s":"3","m":"knots at hundreds"},{"s":"0","m":"empty tens place"},{"s":"5","m":"long knot of 5 turns"}]}
### Cracked and uncracked
In 1912 the American scholar Leland Locke showed how quipus record numbers. Some cords add up to totals on other cords, like a spreadsheet. But some quipus do not fit a simple numerical pattern, and several researchers think they may hold names or stories.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Understood","items":["base-ten numbers","totals and subtotals","census and tribute"]},"r":{"t":"Still debated","items":["names of places?","histories?","colour meanings"]}}
About 1,000 quipus survive, many in museums. In 1583 a church council in Lima ordered some to be destroyed as idolatrous.
!? Some Andean villages still keep old quipus and bring them out at community ceremonies, though the knowledge to read them fully has been lost.
> Quipus show that a state can keep detailed accounts with string, and they still hold secrets.

# Machu Picchu
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~2,430 m","l":"above sea level"},{"v":"~1450","l":"built for Pachacuti"},{"v":"1911","l":"made famous abroad"}]}
Machu Picchu was built around 1450 as a royal estate for the Inca ruler Pachacuti, on a ridge high above the Urubamba river in Peru.
The Spanish never found it. It became world famous after the American explorer Hiram Bingham visited in 1911, guided by local farmers who already knew it well.
---
The site was a retreat for the emperor and his court, with temples, palaces, houses for servants and about 200 buildings in all. Perhaps several hundred people lived there, rising to more when the ruler visited. Radiocarbon dating published in 2021 suggests it was in use from about 1420, a little earlier than once thought.
### Stone without mortar
The finest walls are made of granite blocks cut to fit so tightly that a knife blade will not slip between them. In an earthquake the blocks shift slightly and settle back, which is one reason the walls still stand in a seismic zone.

### Engineering hidden below
• Terraces held up the steep slopes and gave space for crops
• Layers of stone and gravel under them drained the heavy rain
• Stone channels carried spring water to a chain of fountains

### Lost city?
Bingham presented it as a "lost city", but farmers were living and working on the terraces when he arrived, and a local farmer had written his name on a wall in 1902.

> Machu Picchu survived because the conquerors never found it, and today its biggest challenge is the number of visitors.

# Conquest and epidemics
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1519","l":"Cortés lands in Mexico"},{"y":"1520","l":"Smallpox in Tenochtitlan"},{"y":"1521","l":"Tenochtitlan falls"},{"y":"1532","l":"Pizarro seizes Atahualpa"}]}
Small Spanish forces conquered the Aztec and Inca empires within a few years: Hernán Cortés took Tenochtitlan in 1521, and Francisco Pizarro captured the Inca ruler Atahualpa in 1532.
Steel, horses and guns helped, but two things mattered more: thousands of indigenous allies who hated Aztec or Inca rule, and new diseases such as smallpox, which killed vast numbers.
---
Cortés landed in 1519 with about 500 soldiers. He was helped by Malintzin, a Nahua woman who became his interpreter and adviser, and by the Tlaxcalans, enemies of the Mexica, who supplied tens of thousands of warriors. The final siege of Tenochtitlan in 1521 was fought mostly by indigenous armies.
### Disease ahead of the soldiers
People in the Americas had never been exposed to smallpox, measles or influenza, so they had no immunity. Smallpox struck Tenochtitlan in 1520 and killed the new Aztec ruler, Cuitláhuac. It reached the Andes even before the Spanish did, killing the Inca ruler Huayna Capac around 1527 and probably his heir.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Inca ruler dies of smallpox","Two sons fight a civil war","Atahualpa wins","Pizarro arrives, 1532"]}
### Cajamarca
At Cajamarca in 1532 Pizarro, with fewer than 200 men, ambushed Atahualpa's escort and took him prisoner. Atahualpa filled a room with gold and silver as ransom, but was executed in 1533. Inca resistance continued from Vilcabamba until 1572; the last independent Maya kingdom fell only in 1697.
### The great dying
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"central Mexico population, millions (est.)","items":[{"l":"1519","v":20,"t":"~15–25"},{"l":"~1600","v":1.5,"t":"~1–2","hi":true}]}
Repeated epidemics over the 1500s, including the mysterious cocoliztli of 1545 and 1576, cut native populations by perhaps 80–90% in many regions. The figures are estimates and debated, but the catastrophe is not.
!! A few hundred Spaniards defeated empires alone => Indigenous allies did most of the fighting, and epidemics had already shattered both empires
> The conquest of the Americas was decided as much by germs and local alliances as by Spanish weapons.

# Legacy today
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Quechua","b":"~8–10 million speakers"},{"a":"Maya languages","b":"~6 million speakers"},{"a":"Nahuatl","b":"~1.5 million speakers"}]}
The empires fell, but their peoples did not vanish. Millions still speak Quechua, Maya languages and Nahuatl, and indigenous traditions shape life from Mexico to Bolivia.
The rest of the world inherited their crops, from maize, potatoes and tomatoes to chillies, cacao and vanilla, and words such as chocolate, tomato, avocado and llama.
---
Quechua, the language of the Inca administration, is the most widely spoken indigenous language family in the Americas and has official status in Peru and Bolivia. In Guatemala, people of Maya descent make up a large part of the population, and about 30 Maya languages are still spoken across the region.
### Words we borrowed

### Traditions that blended
• The Day of the Dead in Mexico mixes indigenous and Catholic customs, and is UNESCO-listed
• Inti Raymi, the Inca festival of the sun, has been re-enacted in Cusco every June since 1944
• Milpa farming of maize, beans and squash continues across Central America
### Rediscovering the past
Archaeology keeps changing the picture. In 1978 electricity workers digging in central Mexico City struck a huge carved stone of the goddess Coyolxauhqui, leading to the excavation of the Templo Mayor. Lidar surveys keep finding new Maya settlements under the forest.

!? Mexico's national flag shows the eagle and cactus from the founding legend of Tenochtitlan, and Peru's tourism draws heavily on Inca sites.
> The Maya, Aztec and Inca worlds live on in languages, foods, festivals and the faces of millions of their descendants.

# Chinese calligraphy
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Brush","Ink stick","Ink stone","Paper"]}
In China, beautiful handwriting was long considered the highest of the arts, above painting. A single brushstroke was thought to reveal the writer's character.
Calligraphers used the "four treasures of the study": brush, ink stick, ink stone and paper. Ink cannot be erased, so every stroke has to be right the first time.
---
A Chinese brush is made of animal hair, from goat, wolf or rabbit, set in bamboo. Pressed down, it makes a thick line; lifted, a fine one. Ink sticks are made of soot and animal glue, ground with water on the ink stone, so the calligrapher controls how dark the ink is.
### Main scripts
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Seal script","b":"ancient, used on seals"},{"a":"Clerical script","b":"flat, for Han officials"},{"a":"Regular script","b":"clear, like print"},{"a":"Cursive script","b":"flowing, almost abstract"}]}
### The Sage of Calligraphy
Wang Xizhi, who lived in the 300s, is the most admired calligrapher in Chinese history. His *Preface to the Orchid Pavilion*, written at a poetry party in 353, was copied for centuries. The original is lost: according to tradition, the Tang emperor Taizong loved it so much that it was buried with him.
### Learning by copying
Students spend years copying the work of old masters, stroke by stroke, before developing a style of their own. A classic exercise is the character 永, "eternity", which contains the eight basic strokes.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Grind the ink","Copy a master","Master the strokes","Find your own style"]}
Calligraphy spread with Chinese characters to Japan, where it is called shodō, and to Korea and Vietnam. Red seals stamped in cinnabar paste sign a work, and later owners often added their own.
!? Chinese calligraphy is on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage, along with the art of making Xuan paper in Anhui province.
> In Chinese calligraphy, writing is a performance: the brush records the speed, pressure and mood of a moment.

# Ink landscapes
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Chinese landscape","items":["many viewpoints","empty space, mist","ink on silk or paper"]},"r":{"t":"Renaissance picture","items":["one vanishing point","filled canvas","oil on panel"]}}
Chinese painters called landscape shanshui, "mountains and water". From the Song dynasty onward it became the most prestigious subject of painting.
Painters used black ink in many shades, and left empty space for mist, water and sky. Scrolls were meant to be read slowly, like a journey through the hills.
---
The great Song masters painted towering mountains in which people are tiny. Fan Kuan's *Travellers among Mountains and Streams*, from about 1000, shows a cliff filling most of the silk, while a mule train creeps along the bottom.
### Ways to look
The painter Guo Xi, in the 1000s, described "three distances": looking up at a peak, looking deep into valleys and looking level across to far hills. A single painting could combine them, inviting the eye to wander instead of staying at one spot.
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Guo Xi's three distances","items":["High: up at peaks","Deep: into valleys","Level: across to hills"]}
### Scholar painters
Later, many painters were scholar-officials, not professionals. They prized personal expression over skill at copying nature, and combined the "three perfections": poetry, calligraphy and painting on one sheet.
### Two formats
• Hanging scrolls are unrolled from top to bottom and hung on a wall
• Handscrolls are unrolled a section at a time, from right to left, on a table
Collectors added poems and seals over the centuries. The Qianlong emperor stamped and inscribed hundreds of paintings in the imperial collection.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Plum","b":"blooms in winter: resilience"},{"a":"Orchid","b":"modesty"},{"a":"Bamboo","b":"bends but does not break"},{"a":"Chrysanthemum","b":"late autumn: integrity"}]}
!? In Japan the Zen monk Sesshū, who visited China in the 1460s, developed his own bold ink style and is one of the country's most revered painters.
> Chinese landscape painting is less a view of a place than an invitation to travel through it in the mind.

# Ukiyo-e
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Actors","b":"kabuki stars"},{"a":"Beauties","b":"Utamaro's women"},{"a":"Landscapes","b":"Hokusai, Hiroshige"},{"a":"Warriors","b":"Kuniyoshi's heroes"}]}
Ukiyo-e means "pictures of the floating world": the pleasure districts, theatres and fashions of Edo, today's Tokyo, from the 1600s to the 1800s.
Most were colour woodblock prints, sold cheaply to ordinary townspeople. Their stars included Utamaro, Hokusai and Hiroshige, whose views of Mount Fuji and the Tōkaidō road became best-sellers.
---
"Ukiyo" was originally a Buddhist word for the sorrowful, passing world. In Edo it came to mean the opposite: living for the moment, enjoying theatre, food, fashion and pleasure while it lasted. Prints were the posters, fan pictures and celebrity photos of their day.
### From black and white to full colour
Early prints were black ink, sometimes coloured by hand. In 1765 "brocade pictures" printed in many colours appeared, using one carved block per colour and small marks to line up each sheet exactly. Printers also wiped ink across the block to make a soft gradient, often used for skies and water.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1670s","l":"Black ink prints"},{"y":"1765","l":"Full-colour prints"},{"y":"1830s","l":"Hokusai's Fuji views"},{"y":"1850s","l":"Hiroshige's Edo"}]}
### Masters
• Kitagawa Utamaro (about 1753–1806): close-up portraits of women
• Katsushika Hokusai (1760–1849): used about 30 different art names and kept drawing into his late 80s
• Utagawa Hiroshige (1797–1858): rain, snow and moonlight on famous places
### Rules and censors
The government tried to control the floating world. Reforms in 1842 banned prints of actors and courtesans for a time, and publishers had to get a censor's seal before printing.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~250","l":"years of the Edo period"},{"v":"53","l":"stations in Hiroshige's Tōkaidō"}]}
!? A popular print often cost about the same as a simple meal, so artists made money through huge print runs rather than high prices.
> Ukiyo-e was art for the city crowd: cheap, fashionable and made to be enjoyed now.

# Japonisme
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Japan opens ports, 1850s","Prints reach Paris","Artists collect them","New ways to compose"]}
When Japan opened to Western trade in the 1850s, its prints flooded into Europe. Hokusai's *Great Wave* and Hiroshige's views struck Paris artists as something completely new.
Monet, Degas, Van Gogh and Mary Cassatt borrowed flat colour, bold outlines, off-centre views and everyday subjects. The craze was called japonisme.
---
Japan had largely closed itself to foreign trade for over two centuries. After US warships arrived in 1853 and treaties followed in 1858, Japanese goods poured into Europe, and the Paris World's Fair of 1867 showed them to a huge public.
### What artists learned
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Cropping","b":"figures cut by the frame"},{"a":"High viewpoints","b":"looking down on scenes"},{"a":"Flat colour","b":"no modelling in shade"},{"a":"Strong outline","b":"clear, bold shapes"}]}
Degas cut dancers off at the edge of the picture, as if by chance. Toulouse-Lautrec's posters used flat areas of colour and sharp outlines, and Mary Cassatt made a set of colour prints in 1891 after seeing a large exhibition of Japanese prints in Paris.
### Van Gogh's copies
Van Gogh collected hundreds of cheap prints in Paris and pinned them to his walls. In 1887 he painted copies of two Hiroshige prints, a plum tree and a bridge in the rain, in thick oil paint and stronger colours, adding borders of Japanese characters. In Arles he said he was looking for his own Japan in the south of France.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Hiroshige print","items":["woodblock","flat, delicate colour","cheap, many copies"]},"r":{"t":"Van Gogh copy","items":["oil on canvas","thick, bright paint","one of a kind"]}}
### Beyond painting
The influence spread to posters, furniture, gardens and the curving lines of Art Nouveau. The critic Philippe Burty is usually credited with coining the word japonisme in 1872, when collecting Japanese fans, screens and ceramics had become fashionable across Europe.
!? Monet painted his wife in a red kimono in *La Japonaise* (1876), and built a Japanese-style footbridge over his water-lily pond at Giverny.
> Japanese prints helped push European painting away from realism and towards modern art.

# Indian temple sculpture
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Tower: the holy mountain","Hall for worshippers","Inner shrine with deity","Platform of carved stone"]}
Hindu temples in India were designed as homes for the gods and models of the cosmos, their towers rising like the sacred mountain at the centre of the world.
Their walls are covered with sculpture: gods, dancers, animals, lovers and scenes from epic stories. At Ellora, a whole temple was carved downwards out of a single cliff.
---
At the heart of a temple is a small, dark inner shrine, called the "womb chamber", holding the image of the deity. Worshippers move from the bright outside towards it, often walking around it clockwise.
### Two great styles
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"North (Nagara)","items":["curved beehive tower","over the shrine","e.g. Khajuraho"]},"r":{"t":"South (Dravida)","items":["stepped pyramid tower","huge gateway towers","e.g. Thanjavur"]}}
### Carved from the rock
The Kailasa temple at Ellora, in Maharashtra, was cut in the 700s from the top of a basalt hill down, with no blocks added. Workers removed an estimated 200,000 tonnes of stone to free a full temple, with halls, bridges and life-size elephants.
### Khajuraho
The Chandela kings built about 85 temples at Khajuraho between about 950 and 1050; around 25 survive. They are famous for their sensuous carvings, though erotic scenes make up only a small part; most show gods, dancers, musicians and daily life.
### Dancing bronze
Under the Chola kings of the south, around the 900s–1100s, sculptors cast bronze images by the lost-wax method. The best known shows Shiva as Nataraja, Lord of the Dance, in a ring of fire, dancing the cycle of creation and destruction.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Ring of fire","b":"the cosmos"},{"a":"Drum in hand","b":"the sound of creation"},{"a":"Flame in hand","b":"destruction"},{"a":"Raised foot","b":"release for the soul"}]}
!? A 2-metre statue of Shiva Nataraja, a gift from India in 2004, stands at CERN, the physics laboratory near Geneva.
> Indian temples were built as the universe in stone, and their sculpture is meant to be walked through, not just looked at.

# Mughal miniatures
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1526","l":"Babur founds the empire"},{"y":"1556","l":"Akbar's reign begins"},{"y":"1605","l":"Jahangir: nature studies"},{"y":"1628","l":"Shah Jahan's court art"}]}
The Mughal emperors, who ruled much of South Asia from 1526, ran royal workshops that produced exquisite small paintings for books and albums.
Persian and Indian artists worked side by side, and later borrowed ideas from European prints. The results include court histories, portraits and astonishingly precise studies of birds, animals and flowers.
---
Akbar (ruled 1556–1605) could not read well himself, but he loved books read aloud and pictures. His workshop employed around a hundred painters, led at first by two Persian masters his father had brought from Iran, and trained many Hindu artists such as Basawan and Daswanth.
### Made by teams
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Master draws outline","Colourist paints","Specialist adds faces","Page burnished smooth"]}
Pigments were ground from minerals and plants, including lapis lazuli, malachite and real gold. Fine brushes, sometimes of squirrel hair, allowed lines thinner than a human hair. Many pages carry notes naming the artists, so we know who did what.
### A giant project
The *Hamzanama*, the adventures of a legendary hero, took about 15 years and some 1,400 large paintings on cloth; around 200 survive. Later came the *Akbarnama*, the official history of Akbar's reign.
### Portuguese prints at court
In 1580 Jesuits from Portuguese Goa arrived at Akbar's court, bringing a richly illustrated Bible and European engravings. Mughal painters copied them and began to use halos, deeper perspective and softer modelling.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Akbar","items":["action and stories","busy crowds","history books"]},"r":{"t":"Jahangir","items":["portraits","birds and animals","single album pages"]}}
!? One of the most accurate pictures of a living dodo was painted at Jahangir's court, probably by the artist Ustad Mansur, from a bird brought to India.
> Mughal painting blended Persian, Indian and European ideas into a court art of jewel-like precision.

# Islamic geometric art
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Islamic ornament","items":["Geometry: stars, polygons","Arabesque: vines, leaves","Calligraphy: sacred text"]}
Mosques and many religious buildings avoid pictures of people and animals. Instead, artists developed dazzling ornament from geometry, flowing plant forms and calligraphy.
With only a compass and a ruler, they built complex star patterns that repeat endlessly across tiles, wood, plaster and metal, from Spain to Central Asia.
---
Every pattern begins with a circle. Dividing it into equal parts gives points that can be joined to make stars and polygons, and copying that unit across a grid makes a pattern that could, in principle, go on for ever, a reminder of the infinite.
ex: Draw a circle | Mark 8 equal points on it | Join every third point | An eight-pointed star appears
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Circle","Equal divisions","Join the points","Repeat across a grid"]}
### The Alhambra
The Nasrid palaces of the Alhambra in Granada, built mainly in the 1300s, are covered in tile mosaics, carved plaster and wooden ceilings. Mathematicians have identified many, perhaps all, of the 17 basic types of repeating pattern on a flat surface there. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher sketched them in 1922 and 1936, and they inspired his interlocking designs.
### Ahead of their time
A 2007 study argued that tile patterns on a shrine in Isfahan, from 1453, follow rules close to those of "quasicrystal" patterns that Western mathematicians only described in the 1970s.

> Islamic geometric art turns simple rules into infinite patterns, joining mathematics and beauty.

# Angkor Wat
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~1150","l":"completed (approx.)"},{"v":"~160 ha","l":"site area"},{"v":"5","l":"towers: peaks of Mount Meru"}]}
Angkor Wat, in Cambodia, was built in the early 1100s by the Khmer king Suryavarman II as a temple to the Hindu god Vishnu. It later became Buddhist and is still used today.
It is the largest religious monument in the world. Its five towers stand for the peaks of Mount Meru, home of the gods, and its wide moat for the ocean around it.
---
The Khmer Empire dominated much of mainland Southeast Asia from the 800s to the 1400s. Its capital, Angkor, was a huge spread-out city of wooden houses, rice fields, canals and reservoirs around stone temples. Only the temples, built for the gods, were made of stone, which is why they alone survive.
### A temple facing west
Most Khmer temples face east, towards the rising sun. Angkor Wat faces west, a direction linked to Vishnu and to death, so it may also have been the king's funerary temple. At the spring equinox the sun rises behind its central tower.
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Central tower","Three raised galleries","Causeway across the moat","Moat ~190 m wide"]}
### Stories in stone
The galleries hold hundreds of metres of carved reliefs. The most famous shows the Churning of the Ocean of Milk, in which gods and demons pull a giant serpent to stir up the elixir of immortality. More than 1,700 celestial dancers, the apsaras, are carved on the walls, each with her own hairstyle and jewellery.
### Water and decline
Lidar surveys since 2012 have mapped the vast network of canals and reservoirs that fed Angkor. Long droughts and floods in the 1300s and 1400s may have overwhelmed it, and after a Thai attack around 1431 the capital moved south.

!! Angkor Wat was a lost city rediscovered in 1860 => Monks never left it; Portuguese friars described it in the 1580s, long before a French explorer made it famous
> Angkor Wat is a model of the Hindu universe in stone, and the proud symbol on Cambodia's flag.

# Porcelain and trade
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["China: kaolin clay","Fired at ~1,300 °C","Ships and caravans","Europe copies, 1709"]}
Porcelain is a fine white ceramic made from kaolin clay fired at about 1,300 °C. It is hard, thin and translucent, and for centuries only China knew how to make it.
Chinese porcelain was traded across Asia and Africa and, from the 1500s, shipped to Europe in huge quantities. In English it simply became "china".
---
Porcelain needs two materials: kaolin, a white clay, and porcelain stone, which melts into glass in the kiln and binds the clay. At very high temperatures the mixture vitrifies, becoming waterproof and ringing like a bell when tapped.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Earthenware","items":["~1,000 °C","porous, opaque","breaks easily"]},"r":{"t":"Porcelain","items":["~1,300 °C","glassy, translucent","hard and thin"]}}
### Jingdezhen and blue-and-white
The town of Jingdezhen, in Jiangxi province, became China's porcelain capital, with imperial kilns from 1369. In the 1300s potters began painting under the glaze with cobalt blue imported from Persia, much of it for Middle Eastern buyers. Blue-and-white became the world's favourite porcelain.
### By sea
• A shipwreck found off Indonesia in 1998 held about 60,000 Chinese ceramics from the 800s, bound for the Persian Gulf
• From the early 1500s Portuguese ships carried porcelain directly to Lisbon
• The Dutch East India Company later shipped millions of pieces to Europe
!? In Lisbon's Palácio Santos, now the French embassy, a pyramid-shaped ceiling is covered with more than 250 pieces of Chinese porcelain collected in the 1500s and 1600s.
### Europe's secret recipe
European rulers spent fortunes trying to copy it. In 1709, in Saxony, the alchemist Johann Friedrich Böttger finally produced true hard porcelain, and the Meissen factory opened in 1710.

> Porcelain was one of the first truly global products, carried by sea from China to every continent.

# Korean celadon
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Carve the design","Fill with white and black slip","Glaze and fire","Jade-green finish"]}
During the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), Korean potters made celadon: stoneware with a soft blue-green glaze admired across East Asia for its jade-like colour.
They also invented an inlay technique, sanggam: designs were carved into the clay and filled with white and black slip before glazing, giving delicate cranes, clouds and willows.
---
Celadon's colour comes from a small amount of iron in the glaze. Fired in a kiln starved of oxygen, the iron turns the glaze green or blue-green; with too much oxygen, it goes yellowish brown. Potters controlled it by adjusting the fire and the air vents.
### Praised by a rival
Chinese potters made celadon too, but in 1123 a Chinese envoy to the Goryeo court, Xu Jing, praised Korean celadon as "kingfisher-coloured", a high compliment. Kilns at Gangjin and Buan on the south-west coast supplied the court.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Maebyeong","b":"tall vase, narrow mouth"},{"a":"Inlaid cranes","b":"symbol of long life"},{"a":"Incense burner","b":"often shaped as animals"}]}
### After Goryeo
Mongol invasions in the 1200s damaged the industry, and the quality of celadon declined. Under the Joseon dynasty (1392–1897), potters turned to buncheong, a looser stoneware with white slip, and to plain white porcelain, including the large, slightly lopsided "moon jars".
### Potters taken abroad
During Japan's invasions of Korea in the 1590s, many Korean potters were taken to Japan. One of them, Yi Sam-pyeong, is credited with finding porcelain clay at Arita around 1616, starting Japan's porcelain industry.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"918","l":"Goryeo dynasty begins"},{"y":"1123","l":"Xu Jing praises celadon"},{"y":"1392","l":"Joseon: new styles"},{"y":"~1616","l":"Porcelain begins at Arita"}]}
!? The word "celadon" comes from Céladon, a shepherd in a 17th-century French novel who wore pale green ribbons.
> Korean celadon turned a chemical reaction in the kiln into one of the most refined colours in ceramic art.
