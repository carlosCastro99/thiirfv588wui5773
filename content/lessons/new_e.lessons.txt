# Oracle bones
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Ask the ancestors","Heat the bone","Read the cracks","Carve the answer"]}
China's oldest writing survives on bones. Around 1200 BCE, kings of the Shang dynasty asked their ancestors about harvests, wars and illness by heating ox shoulder blades and turtle shells until they cracked.
Diviners read the cracks and carved the question, and sometimes the result, into the bone. Those signs are the direct ancestors of today's Chinese characters.
---
The Shang ruled the Yellow River valley from roughly 1600 to 1046 BCE. Their last capital, near modern Anyang in Henan province, has produced more than 150,000 inscribed fragments, the earliest large body of Chinese writing.
### How a divination worked
Diviners carved rows of small hollows into the back of a bone or shell. A heated rod pressed into a hollow made the surface crack with a sharp pop, and the shape of the crack was read as yes or no, lucky or unlucky.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Ox shoulder blade","b":"large, flat, easy to get"},{"a":"Turtle plastron","b":"prized belly shell"},{"a":"Drilled hollows","b":"control where cracks run"},{"a":"Inscription","b":"date, question, outcome"}]}
### What kings worried about
• Will it rain? Will the millet harvest be good?
• Should we attack this enemy?
• Will the queen's childbirth be lucky?
• Why does the king have toothache? Which ancestor is angry?
One name that appears often is Fu Hao, a royal consort who also led armies. Her tomb, found intact at Anyang in 1976, held hundreds of bronzes and jades.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"150,000+","l":"inscribed fragments"},{"v":"~4,500","l":"different characters"},{"v":"~1/3","l":"of them deciphered"}]}
!? The bones were rediscovered around 1899, when scholars noticed carvings on "dragon bones" sold in pharmacies to be ground into medicine.
!! Chinese characters have not changed in 3,000 years => The writing system is continuous, but the shapes of characters changed greatly and thousands of new ones were added
> Chinese writing began as a conversation between kings and their ancestors, scratched into bone.

# The First Emperor
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"475 BCE","l":"Warring States begin"},{"y":"221 BCE","l":"Qin unites China"},{"y":"210 BCE","l":"First Emperor dies"},{"y":"206 BCE","l":"Qin dynasty falls"}]}
After centuries of war between rival states, King Zheng of Qin conquered them all and in 221 BCE declared himself Qin Shi Huang, the "First Emperor".
He ruled through appointed officials rather than nobles, and imposed one script, one currency and one system of weights. His dynasty collapsed soon after his death, but the idea of a single China lasted.
---
Zheng became king of Qin at about 13 and spent some 25 years conquering the six other major states. The title he invented, huangdi, combined words used for legendary sages and gods, and every Chinese ruler used it until 1912. Many scholars think the Western name "China" comes from Qin.
### One standard for everything
@viz {"k":"list","items":["One script for official writing","Round coins with square holes","Standard weights and lengths","Same axle width for carts","Districts run by officials"]}
Standard axles mattered because carts wore deep ruts into dirt roads, and a cart of the wrong width could not use them. The old noble domains were replaced by commanderies run by officials the emperor could appoint and dismiss.
### Harsh rule
Qin followed **Legalism**, a philosophy that kept people in line with strict laws and severe punishments. According to the historian Sima Qian, writing about a century later, the emperor ordered books burned in 213 BCE and scholars killed. Some historians think the account is exaggerated, but the regime was certainly feared. Hundreds of thousands of conscripts built roads, canals, walls and his tomb.
### The Terracotta Army
In 1974 farmers digging a well near Xi'an found pieces of a clay soldier. Excavations revealed pits holding an estimated 8,000 life-size soldiers, plus horses and chariots, guarding the emperor in death.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Found so far","items":["~8,000 soldiers (est.)","horses and chariots","bronze weapons"]},"r":{"t":"Still unknown","items":["tomb never opened","how to save the paint","rivers of mercury?"]}}
!? Sima Qian wrote that the tomb held rivers of mercury. Soil tests over the mound have found unusually high mercury levels, one reason it has not been opened.
> The Qin dynasty lasted only 15 years, but its standards and its idea of one emperor over one China lasted more than 2,000.

# The Great Walls
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"600s BCE","l":"States build first walls"},{"y":"221 BCE","l":"Qin links northern walls"},{"y":"~100 BCE","l":"Han push into desert"},{"y":"1500s","l":"Ming brick walls"}]}
The Great Wall is not one wall but many, built over about 2,000 years by different states and dynasties to guard farmland against horsemen from the northern steppe.
The famous stone and brick sections near Beijing are fairly recent: most were built by the Ming dynasty, mainly in the 1500s. Older walls were mostly made of rammed earth.
---
A Chinese government survey published in 2012 counted every wall, trench and natural barrier from all periods and arrived at about 21,000 km, more than half the distance around the Equator. Many stretches are now low earth ridges that are easy to miss.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"length in km (approx.)","items":[{"l":"All walls (2012)","v":21000,"t":"~21,000","hi":true},{"l":"Ming walls","v":8850,"t":"~8,850"},{"l":"Equator","v":40000,"t":"~40,000"}]}
### Building materials
Builders used whatever was nearby.
• Rammed earth: soil pounded in layers inside wooden frames
• In the Gobi desert: layers of gravel with reeds or tamarisk branches
• Ming sections: fired bricks and cut stone, bonded with lime mortar
Studies of Ming mortar found sticky rice soup mixed into the lime, which made it stronger and more water-resistant.
### What walls were for
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Slow down raiders on horseback","Signal with smoke and fire","Control trade and taxes","Watch who comes and goes"]}
A wall could not stop a determined army, but it slowed raids, funnelled traffic through gates and let watchtowers pass warnings with fire at night and smoke by day. It also marked where settled farming China ended. In 1644 the Manchus came through the Shanhai Pass at its eastern end after a Ming general opened the gate to them.
!! The Great Wall is visible from space with the naked eye => It is very long but only a few metres wide and the colour of the land; China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei, said in 2003 that he could not see it
> The Great Wall is a 2,000-year collection of frontiers, each one a snapshot of where China's rulers drew their line.

# The Han and the Silk Road
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Went west","items":["silk","lacquer and iron","paper (later)"]},"r":{"t":"Came east","items":["horses","grapes and alfalfa","glass, Buddhism"]}}
The Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) ruled about as many people as the Roman Empire, at the same time. Most Chinese people still call themselves Han.
Han armies and envoys opened routes through Central Asia. Along them, silk travelled west, while horses, new crops and, later, Buddhism came east.
---
In 138 BCE Emperor Wu sent an officer called Zhang Qian west to find allies against the Xiongnu, a nomadic confederation that raided Han lands. He was captured by the Xiongnu and held for about ten years, escaped, and returned around 125 BCE without an alliance but with something more valuable: detailed reports on the kingdoms of Central Asia.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"138 BCE","l":"Zhang Qian sets out"},{"y":"~125 BCE","l":"He returns with reports"},{"y":"101 BCE","l":"Han win Ferghana horses"},{"y":"97 CE","l":"Envoy sent towards Rome"}]}
### Heavenly horses
Zhang Qian described tall, fast horses in the Ferghana valley, in today's Uzbekistan. Han cavalry needed them to fight the steppe riders, so the emperor sent armies thousands of kilometres to get them. Garrisons and watchtowers were then built along the Gansu corridor to protect the caravans.
### Two empires that never met
The Romans knew the Chinese only as the Seres, the "silk people", and the Han knew Rome vaguely as Daqin. In 97 CE an envoy, Gan Ying, was sent to reach it; he got as far as the Persian Gulf or the Black Sea region and turned back. Goods, not people, made the whole journey, passing through Parthian and other middlemen.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~57 million","l":"people in the Han census of 2 CE"},{"v":"~400","l":"years of Han rule"}]}
!? A Chinese chronicle records that in 166 CE visitors claiming to represent the Roman emperor Andun, probably Marcus Aurelius, arrived by sea. They were most likely enterprising merchants.
### Ideas on the road
Buddhism reached China from India along these routes in the 1st century CE. Monks, translators and pilgrims followed the merchants, and cave temples grew up at oasis towns such as Dunhuang.
> The Han turned a military search for horses and allies into a lasting link between East and West.

# The imperial exams
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Palace exam: the emperor","Metropolitan exam: capital","Provincial exam","Local exams"]}
For about 1,300 years, from 605 to 1905, China chose many of its officials through written exams on the Confucian classics, rather than by birth alone.
Candidates memorised hundreds of thousands of characters of ancient text and wrote essays on how to govern. Passing brought prestige, power and wealth to a whole family.
---
Confucius (551–479 BCE) taught that society works when rulers govern by moral example and everyone respects their duties: children to parents, subjects to rulers, friends to friends. Han emperors made his teachings state orthodoxy, and the Sui dynasty began formal exams in 605 CE. The system matured under the Song, when printed books made study more widely possible.
### Days in a cell
At the provincial level, candidates were locked into tiny brick cells for about three days, with one board as a desk and another as a bed. The examination hall in Nanjing had about 20,000 cells. Answers were recopied by clerks before marking, so examiners could not recognise handwriting.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Four Books","b":"Confucius, Mencius and more"},{"a":"Five Classics","b":"poetry, history, rites"},{"a":"Eight-legged essay","b":"rigid essay form, from Ming"},{"a":"Jinshi","b":"top degree"}]}
### Very few winners
Pass rates at the higher levels were often only a few per cent. Men sat the exams again and again, some into old age. Cheating was a constant battle: museums hold silk undershirts covered in thousands of tiny handwritten passages from the classics.
!! Anyone could rise through the exams => In theory most men could sit them, but women were excluded and years of study cost money, so wealthy families had a big advantage
### Copied abroad
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Korea and Vietnam: own exams","Europe: admired by Voltaire","Britain: civil-service exams","China today: the gaokao"]}
Korea and Vietnam adopted similar exams. European writers admired the idea of officials chosen by merit, and Britain's competitive civil-service exams, introduced from the 1850s, were partly inspired by the Chinese example.
> China's exams made learning the road to power for over a thousand years, an early form of government by merit.

# Four great inventions
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Paper","b":"Han dynasty, ~100 CE"},{"a":"Printing","b":"woodblocks by the 700s"},{"a":"Compass","b":"used at sea by ~1100"},{"a":"Gunpowder","b":"Tang alchemists, 800s"}]}
Paper, printing, the magnetic compass and gunpowder are often called China's "four great inventions". Each one later spread west and changed the world.
In 1620 the English philosopher Francis Bacon wrote that printing, gunpowder and the compass had transformed the world more than any empire. He did not know where they came from.
---
### Paper
Early paper fragments date from the 2nd century BCE. In 105 CE a court official, Cai Lun, presented an improved recipe: mulberry bark, hemp, rags and old fishing nets, pulped, spread on a screen and dried. Papermaking reached Samarkand in the 700s, Baghdad soon after, and Europe through al-Andalus, where Xàtiva near Valencia was making paper by the 1100s.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["China, ~100 CE","Samarkand, 700s","Baghdad, ~800","Al-Andalus, 1100s","Rest of Europe"]}
### Printing
Woodblock printing carved a whole page into one block. The oldest dated printed book, a Buddhist text called the Diamond Sutra, was printed in 868 and found in a cave at Dunhuang. Around 1040 an artisan called Bi Sheng made movable type from baked clay, but with thousands of characters to cast, whole-page blocks often stayed cheaper.
### Compass
Chinese thinkers knew that lodestone points south and first used it for divination. In 1088 the scholar Shen Kuo described a magnetised needle, and by about 1100 sailors used one to navigate in cloudy weather.
### Gunpowder
Alchemists searching for an elixir of long life noticed that mixing saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal could burn a house down. Fire arrows, bombs and rockets followed.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"~850","l":"Text warns of fire risk"},{"y":"1044","l":"First written formula"},{"y":"1200s","l":"Early metal guns"},{"y":"1300s","l":"Cannon in Europe"}]}
!? China produced many other inventions too, including cast iron, the wheelbarrow, porcelain and an earthquake detector built by Zhang Heng in 132 CE.
> The four inventions spread knowledge, guided ships and changed warfare, and every one crossed Asia before reaching Europe.

# Tang and Song
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Tang 618–907","items":["cosmopolitan Chang'an","golden age of poetry","Silk Road trade"]},"r":{"t":"Song 960–1279","items":["paper money","huge trading cities","landscape painting"]}}
The Tang dynasty (618–907) ruled from Chang'an, perhaps the world's largest city, full of merchants, monks and envoys from across Asia. It was the golden age of Chinese poetry.
The Song (960–1279) was weaker militarily but richer: rice farming boomed, cities grew, and the government issued the world's first paper money.
---
Tang Chang'an had perhaps a million people in and around its walls, laid out on a grid of wide avenues with two great markets. Persians, Sogdians, Arabs, Koreans and Japanese lived there, and Buddhist temples stood near Zoroastrian, Manichaean and Christian ones. Japan copied its layout for its capitals at Nara and Kyoto.
### Poets and an empress
Li Bai and Du Fu, still memorised by Chinese schoolchildren, wrote in the 700s. Wu Zetian became the only woman in Chinese history to rule as emperor in her own name, from 690 to 705. Then the An Lushan rebellion (755–763) devastated the empire, and the Tang never fully recovered.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"618","l":"Tang founded"},{"y":"690","l":"Wu Zetian rules"},{"y":"755","l":"An Lushan rebellion"},{"y":"960","l":"Song founded"},{"y":"1279","l":"Song falls to Mongols"}]}
### The Song economy
• Fast-ripening rice from Champa (now in Vietnam) allowed two harvests a year in the south
• China's population passed 100 million
• Sichuan merchants used paper notes; the state took over issuing them in 1024
• Kaifeng and later Hangzhou may each have held around a million people
@viz {"k":"cycle","steps":["More rice","More people","More trade","Bigger cities"]}
### Painters and scholars
Song scholar-officials, chosen by exam, also painted, wrote poetry and practised calligraphy. The long scroll *Along the River During the Qingming Festival*, painted in the 1100s, shows more than 800 people going about city life in minute detail.
!? Despite this wealth, the Song paid large yearly sums of silver and silk to northern neighbours to keep the peace, and still lost the north to the Jurchen in 1127.
> Tang brought power and openness; Song brought wealth, cities and paper money that later amazed Marco Polo.

# Zheng He's fleets
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"7","l":"voyages, 1405–1433"},{"v":"~300","l":"ships in the first fleet"},{"v":"~28,000","l":"sailors and soldiers"}]}
Between 1405 and 1433, the Ming admiral Zheng He led seven huge fleets across the Indian Ocean, reaching India, Arabia and East Africa.
The voyages showed off Ming power and collected tribute. Then they stopped: the court turned inward, and China left the oceans to others, decades before the Portuguese arrived.
---
Zheng He was born around 1371 into a Muslim family in Yunnan. Captured as a boy when Ming armies conquered the province, he was made a eunuch and served Prince Zhu Di. When the prince seized the throne as the Yongle Emperor, Zheng He became one of his most trusted commanders.
### Treasure ships
Ming records describe the largest "treasure ships" as about 120–135 m long. Many historians think that is exaggerated and that 60–75 m is more likely, but even that dwarfed European ships of the time.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"ship length in m (approx.)","items":[{"l":"Recorded claim","v":127,"t":"~120–135"},{"l":"Likely size","v":67,"t":"~60–75","hi":true},{"l":"Santa María","v":20,"t":"~20"}]}
### Where they went
• Champa, Java, Sumatra and Malacca
• Sri Lanka and Calicut in India
• Hormuz, Aden and the Red Sea
• Mogadishu and Malindi in East Africa
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Zheng He, 1405","items":["~300 ships","~28,000 men","show of power"]},"r":{"t":"Da Gama, 1497","items":["4 ships","~170 men","trade and forts"]}}
!? In 1414 the fleet brought a giraffe from East Africa, via Bengal, to the Ming court. Courtiers presented it as a qilin, a mythical creature said to appear only under a wise ruler.
### Why it stopped
After Yongle died in 1424, officials argued that the voyages were hugely expensive and brought back mainly luxuries. The real threat was the Mongols in the north, so money went to walls and to the new capital in Beijing. Later Ming governments even restricted private sea trade.
!! Zheng He reached America in 1421 => This popular claim from a 2002 book has no evidence behind it and is rejected by historians
> In the early 1400s China had the greatest navy in the world, and chose to stop using it.

# The Qing dynasty
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1644","l":"Manchus take Beijing"},{"y":"1757","l":"Trade limited to Canton"},{"y":"1842","l":"First Opium War ends"},{"y":"1864","l":"Taiping revolt crushed"}]}
In 1644 the Manchus, from the north-east, took Beijing and founded the Qing, China's last dynasty. Under emperors such as Kangxi and Qianlong it nearly doubled in size and grew rich.
In the 1800s it faced rising Western powers, two Opium Wars, unequal treaties and a devastating civil war, the Taiping Rebellion.
---
The Manchus were a small minority ruling a vast Han majority. They kept many Ming institutions, including the exams, but required Chinese men to shave the front of the head and wear a long braid, the queue, as a sign of loyalty.
### The high Qing
Kangxi (ruled 1661–1722) and his grandson Qianlong (1735–1796) had two of the longest reigns in Chinese history. Their campaigns brought Taiwan, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet under Qing control, roughly setting the borders of modern China.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"population of China, millions (approx.)","items":[{"l":"1700","v":150},{"l":"1800","v":300},{"l":"1850","v":430,"hi":true}]}
### Trade on China's terms
From 1757 Western merchants could trade only at Canton (Guangzhou), through licensed Chinese firms. Europeans paid for tea, silk and porcelain mostly in silver. In 1793 a British embassy under Lord Macartney asked for wider access; Qianlong replied that China had no need of British goods.
### Opium and unequal treaties
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Britain pays silver for tea","Sells Indian opium instead","China bans opium","Opium War, 1839–42","Hong Kong ceded"]}
The treaties that followed opened more ports, gave foreigners legal privileges and fixed low tariffs. Chinese historians call the era that began then the "century of humiliation".
The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), led by a man who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus, became one of the deadliest wars in history, with an estimated 20–30 million deaths.
!? Portugal had a presence in Macau from 1557, long before Britain took Hong Kong. It was handed back to China on 20 December 1999, the last European colony in Asia.
> The Qing built China's largest empire, then struggled to adapt when industrial powers arrived by sea.

# The end of empire, 1912
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1900","l":"Boxer uprising"},{"y":"1905","l":"Exams abolished"},{"y":"1911","l":"Wuchang uprising"},{"y":"1912","l":"Last emperor abdicates"}]}
After defeats by Western powers and by Japan, reforms came too late. In October 1911 an army uprising in Wuchang spread across the provinces.
On 1 January 1912 the Republic of China was proclaimed, and in February the six-year-old emperor Puyi abdicated. More than 2,000 years of imperial rule, begun by the First Emperor in 221 BCE, were over.
---
For almost half a century real power lay with the Empress Dowager Cixi, who dominated the court from 1861 until her death in 1908. She backed some modernisation, such as arsenals and shipyards, but in 1898 she crushed a burst of reforms by her nephew, the Guangxu Emperor, after only about a hundred days.
### Shocks from abroad
@viz {"k":"list","items":["1895: defeat by Japan","1898: reforms crushed","1900: Boxers besiege embassies","1901: huge indemnity imposed"]}
The Boxers, a movement that blamed foreigners and Christians for China's troubles, besieged the foreign legations in Beijing in 1900. An army of eight foreign powers took the city, and China was forced to pay a vast indemnity.
### Revolution
Revolutionary groups, many organised by Sun Yat-sen from exile, staged a series of failed uprisings. The one that worked began almost by accident: on 9 October 1911 a bomb went off by mistake in neighbouring Hankou, police found membership lists, and plotters in the Wuchang army rose the next day before they could be arrested. Province after province then declared independence from the Qing.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Sun Yat-sen","b":"first provisional president"},{"a":"Yuan Shikai","b":"general, arranged abdication"},{"a":"Puyi","b":"last emperor, aged six"}]}
### The last emperor
Puyi was allowed to live on in the Forbidden City until 1924. Japan later made him puppet ruler of Manchukuo, in north-east China. After years in Soviet and Chinese prisons he lived as an ordinary citizen in Beijing until his death in 1967, a story told in the 1987 film *The Last Emperor*.
!? Portugal had become a republic on 5 October 1910, so both countries ended their monarchies within about 16 months of each other.
> The fall of the Qing did not end China's troubles: it opened decades of warlords, invasion and civil war.
