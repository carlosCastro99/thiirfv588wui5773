# A city on seven hills
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "7", "l": "traditional hills"}, {"v": "~550,000", "l": "people in the city"}, {"v": "~2.9 M", "l": "in the metropolitan area"}]}
Lisbon climbs a set of steep hills on the north bank of the Tagus, just before the river meets the Atlantic. Tradition says there are seven, like Rome.
It is one of Europe's oldest cities and has been Portugal's capital since the 13th century. The city itself is small, but greater Lisbon holds over a quarter of the country's people.
---
The "seven hills" idea was popularised in 1620 by the writer Frei Nicolau de Oliveira, who wanted Lisbon to stand comparison with Rome. The usual list is São Jorge, São Vicente, Sant'Ana, Santo André, Chagas, Santa Catarina and São Roque. In reality there are more, and the highest ground, in the Monsanto forest park, is not on the list at all.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "São Jorge", "b": "the castle"}, {"a": "São Vicente", "b": "monastery of São Vicente"}, {"a": "Santa Catarina", "b": "Adamastor viewpoint"}, {"a": "São Roque", "b": "Bairro Alto"}]}
### An ancient harbour
The site was ideal: hills that were easy to defend, beside a huge sheltered estuary with fresh water and fish. Phoenician traders were probably here by about the 8th century BC. The Romans called the town Olisipo and gave it the title Felicitas Julia. Under Muslim rule it was al-Ushbuna, and in 1147 it was taken by Afonso Henriques.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~800 BC", "l": "Phoenician traders"}, {"y": "~138 BC", "l": "Roman Olisipo"}, {"y": "~714", "l": "Moorish rule"}, {"y": "1147", "l": "Taken by Afonso I"}, {"y": "~1255", "l": "Becomes the capital"}]}
### Small city, big region
• The municipality covers only about 100 km², smaller than many rural councils
• Its population peaked at about 800,000 in 1981, then fell as families moved to the suburbs
• The metropolitan area, from Sintra and Cascais across the river to Setúbal, has about 2.9 million people
!! Lisbon was founded by Ulysses => A charming legend; the name probably comes from a pre-Roman, possibly Phoenician, word, and its exact origin is unknown
> Lisbon grew where defensible hills meet a vast sheltered estuary, one of the finest natural harbours in Europe.

# Alfama and the Moorish city
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Alfama", "b": "from Arabic al-hamma, baths"}, {"a": "Mouraria", "b": "Moorish quarter after 1147"}, {"a": "Castelo", "b": "Moorish fortress on top"}, {"a": "Sé", "b": "cathedral begun c. 1147"}]}
Alfama, below the castle, is Lisbon's oldest quarter. Its name comes from Arabic, and its maze of steep alleys, stairways and tiny squares still follows the shape of the Moorish town.
Much of it survived the 1755 earthquake. Today it is a heartland of Lisbon fado and of the June festivals.
---
For about 400 years, from the early 700s to 1147, Lisbon was a Muslim city, part of al-Andalus. Its walled town sat on the castle hill and ran down to the river, roughly where Alfama is now. The name probably comes from al-hamma, "hot springs" or "baths", after warm springs that once rose there.
### Layers of history
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Castelo de São Jorge, hilltop", "Alfama's lanes and stairways", "Sé cathedral, mid-slope", "Riverside, old walls and gates"]}
After the Christian conquest, the Sé, a fortress-like Romanesque cathedral, was built where the main mosque is thought to have stood. Muslims who stayed were moved outside the walls to a district still called Mouraria. The castle on the summit was rebuilt many times; much of what visitors see today is a restoration from the 1940s.
### Why it survived 1755
Alfama is often said to have come through the earthquake better because it stands on firmer ground than the soft river silts of the Baixa. Whatever the reason, it is one of the few places where Lisbon's pre-earthquake street plan survives: dead-end alleys (becos), stairways (escadinhas) and little squares (largos).
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Alfama", "items": ["medieval maze", "firmer ground", "partly survived 1755"]}, "r": {"t": "Baixa", "items": ["planned grid", "soft river silt", "rebuilt after 1755"]}}
• Lisbon fado, sung in small taverns in Alfama and Mouraria, has been UNESCO intangible heritage since 2011
• On the night of 12–13 June, the Santo António festival fills the alleys with grilled sardines and pots of basil
• Tram 28 squeezes through streets barely wider than the car
!? Mouraria is where the 19th-century singer Maria Severa lived; she is often called the first great fadista.
> Alfama is a Moorish street plan that outlived the earthquake and is still lived in after more than a thousand years.

# Belém
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Jerónimos Monastery", "Belém Tower", "Padrão dos Descobrimentos", "Pastéis de Belém", "CCB and MAAT"]}
Belém, on the river west of the centre, is where fleets left for Africa, India and Brazil. Its monuments celebrate that age of voyages.
The Jerónimos Monastery and the Belém Tower, both in the ornate Manueline style, have been UNESCO World Heritage since 1983.
---
In the 1400s this was a beach called Restelo, outside the city, with a small chapel where sailors prayed before leaving. Vasco da Gama is said to have spent the night there before sailing for India in 1497. King Manuel I then built something far grander on the spot.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1501", "l": "Jerónimos begun"}, {"y": "~1520", "l": "Belém Tower finished"}, {"y": "1837", "l": "Pastéis shop opens"}, {"y": "1960", "l": "Padrão rebuilt in stone"}, {"y": "1983", "l": "UNESCO listing"}]}
### The Manueline style
Manueline architecture mixes late Gothic structure with carved ropes, anchors, armillary spheres, exotic plants and sea creatures: the imagery of an empire built on ships. Jerónimos was paid for largely by a tax on the spice trade. Vasco da Gama lies in its church, near a tomb honouring the poet Camões.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Armillary sphere", "b": "Manuel I's royal emblem"}, {"a": "Twisted rope", "b": "ships and rigging"}, {"a": "Cross of Christ", "b": "faith and the voyages"}]}
### The tower and the monument
The Belém Tower was a small fortress guarding the river mouth, built on a rock in the water. The shoreline has since crept out towards it, partly through land reclamation. The Padrão dos Descobrimentos, a stone prow over 50 m tall crowded with navigators, is much newer: a temporary version was made for the 1940 Portuguese World Exhibition, and the permanent one in 1960, for the 500th anniversary of Prince Henry's death.
Modern Belém adds the Centro Cultural de Belém (1992), the riverside MAAT museum (2016) and the Belém Palace, the official home of the President.
!? Pastéis de Belém have been sold beside the monastery since 1837, using a recipe said to come from its monks and still kept secret.
> Belém is the age of voyages written in stone, from 16th-century Manueline carving to 20th-century commemoration.

# Port of the world
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ships arrive from Asia", "Casa da Índia logs cargo", "Spices sold on to Antwerp", "Profits flow to the crown"]}
In the 1500s Lisbon was one of the busiest ports in Europe. Pepper, silk and porcelain from Asia, sugar from Brazil and gold from Africa passed along its riverside.
It was a crowded, cosmopolitan city, with merchants from all over Europe and a large African population, many of them enslaved.
---
King Manuel I moved the royal palace from the castle down to the river around 1500, a clear sign of where power and money now lay. The new Ribeira Palace stood beside the Casa da Índia, the royal house that controlled the spice trade, and the shipyards where carracks were built.
### What passed through
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "From India", "b": "pepper, cinnamon, cotton"}, {"a": "From China", "b": "porcelain, silk"}, {"a": "From Brazil", "b": "sugar, brazilwood"}, {"a": "From Africa", "b": "gold, ivory, enslaved people"}]}
Most spices were not used in Portugal. They were shipped on to Antwerp, then the trading heart of northern Europe, where the Portuguese crown kept a trading house.
### A cosmopolitan city
Lisbon grew to perhaps 100,000 people by the mid-1500s, among the largest cities in Europe. Its main shopping street, Rua Nova dos Mercadores, sold goods from every continent.
• Africans, most of them enslaved, may have made up around a tenth of the population
• Italian, Flemish and German merchant families helped finance the voyages
• New Christians, Jews forced to convert in 1497, played a big part in trade
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1498", "l": "Sea route to India"}, {"y": "~1500", "l": "Palace moves to river"}, {"y": "1580", "l": "Union with Spain"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Riverside destroyed"}]}
Little of this city survives: the palace, the Casa da Índia and Rua Nova all vanished in 1755.
!? In 1515 a live rhinoceros from India arrived in Lisbon as a gift for King Manuel; Albrecht Dürer's famous woodcut was based on a description and sketch of it.
> In the 1500s Lisbon was a hinge between Europe and the wider world, and its riverside showed it.

# After 1755: the Baixa
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Praça do Comércio", "Arch of Rua Augusta", "Grid of trade streets", "Rossio square"]}
The 1755 earthquake, tsunami and fire destroyed the lower town. It was rebuilt as the Baixa, a grid of straight streets running from the river to Rossio.
Today you can walk the whole plan in about fifteen minutes, from the riverside square, under a triumphal arch, to the old city squares.
---
The Baixa is best understood on foot. Start at the river in Praça do Comércio, laid out where the royal palace had stood. Its arcaded buildings now hold ministries, cafés and museums, and in the centre stands the bronze horseman of King José I, by the sculptor Machado de Castro, unveiled in 1775.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1755", "l": "Lower town destroyed"}, {"y": "1775", "l": "Statue of José I"}, {"y": "1873", "l": "Rua Augusta arch done"}, {"y": "1988", "l": "Chiado fire"}]}
### Up the grid
The triumphal arch of Rua Augusta was planned soon after the earthquake but only finished in 1873. Beyond it, the pedestrian Rua Augusta runs straight to Rossio, with parallel streets named after old trades, such as Rua do Ouro and Rua da Prata. Rossio itself has the wavy black-and-white pavement known as "mar largo" and a column topped by King Pedro IV.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Praça do Comércio", "b": "old palace site, riverfront"}, {"a": "Rua Augusta", "b": "pedestrian main street"}, {"a": "Rossio", "b": "wavy pavement, Pedro IV"}, {"a": "Chiado", "b": "rebuilt by Siza after 1988"}]}
### A second fire
On 25 August 1988 a fire destroyed much of the Chiado, the elegant shopping district on the slope above. The architect Álvaro Siza led the rebuilding, keeping the historic façades and modernising what lay behind them. The Santa Justa lift and the roofless Carmo Convent nearby link the Baixa to this upper town.
!! Every Baixa building is untouched 18th-century work => Many interiors were later gutted and rebuilt, and some lost their earthquake-resistant timber frames
!? Under Rua da Prata lie Roman galleries, probably foundations of a riverside building; they are usually flooded and open to visitors only a few days a year.
> The Baixa is a planned 18th-century city you can read from end to end, from river square to Rossio.

# Trams and lifts
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1873", "l": "Horse-drawn trams"}, {"y": "1884", "l": "First funicular"}, {"y": "1901", "l": "Electric trams"}, {"y": "1902", "l": "Santa Justa lift"}, {"y": "1959", "l": "Metro opens"}]}
Lisbon's hills made getting around hard, so the city built funiculars, a street lift and a tram network. The small yellow trams, like the famous number 28, still rattle through the old quarters.
Underground runs the Metro, opened in 1959, with four lines and many stations decorated with azulejos.
---
Horse-drawn trams on rails, called "americanos" because the idea came from the United States, arrived in 1873. Electric trams followed in 1901. Over the 20th century buses and the Metro took over most routes, and only a handful of tram lines survive.
### Why the small trams survive
The classic "remodelado" trams were built in the 1930s and 1940s and rebuilt in the 1990s with new motors and brakes. They are short and narrow, so they can take the tight bends and steep slopes of Alfama and Graça, where modern trams would not fit.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Remodelado", "items": ["1930s design", "one short car", "sharp bends, steep hills"]}, "r": {"t": "Modern tram", "items": ["long, articulated", "low floor", "flat riverside line 15"]}}
### Funiculars and a lift
• Lavra (1884), Glória (1885) and Bica (1892) are funiculars: two cars joined by a cable, one going up as the other comes down
• The Santa Justa lift (1902), a neo-Gothic iron tower, links the Baixa to the Carmo square
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Cable joins two cars", "One car goes up", "The other comes down", "Their weights balance"]}
In September 2025 a crash on the Glória funicular killed 16 people, and the city's funiculars were stopped for safety inspections.
!! The Santa Justa lift was designed by Eiffel => It was designed by Raoul Mesnier du Ponsard, an engineer born in Porto to French parents
> Lisbon's quirky transport is an answer to geography: small trams, cables and lifts for a city of steep hills.

# Miradouros and Lisbon light
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Senhora do Monte", "Graça", "Portas do Sol", "São Pedro de Alcântara", "Santa Catarina"]}
Lisbon's hills give it dozens of miradouros: public viewpoints with benches, kiosks and views over red roofs to the river.
The city is also famous for its light. Strong sun, reflected off the wide Tagus and pale limestone pavements, gives it a bright, golden glow.
---
A miradouro is a public terrace on a hilltop or slope, usually with trees, a kiosk and a bench. Most were laid out in the 19th and 20th centuries, as the views came to be treated as something everyone shared. Many sit at the end of a steep climb, so locals often time a visit with a tram or lift ride up.
### Where to look
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Senhora do Monte", "b": "among the highest views"}, {"a": "Santa Luzia", "b": "azulejos, Alfama roofs"}, {"a": "São Pedro de Alcântara", "b": "castle across the valley"}, {"a": "Santa Catarina", "b": "river and 25 de Abril bridge"}]}
### Why the light is special
Lisbon is one of the sunniest capitals in Europe, with about 2,800 hours of sunshine a year. The Tagus estuary, more than 10 km wide in places, acts like a huge mirror, and white limestone pavements and pale façades bounce the light back up. Clean Atlantic air helps too, and painters and photographers have long been drawn to it.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "sun hours a year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Lisbon", "v": 2800, "hi": true}, {"l": "Rome", "v": 2500}, {"l": "Paris", "v": 1700}, {"l": "London", "v": 1600}]}
• Sunset over the river is best from Santa Catarina or the riverside at Cais do Sodré
• Many miradouros have kiosks selling drinks and snacks, revived since the 2000s
• Morning light from the east is best at Graça and Senhora do Monte
!? The statue at the Santa Catarina viewpoint is Adamastor, the storm giant who, in Camões's epic The Lusiads, guards the Cape of Good Hope.
> Lisbon's viewpoints turn a tiring, hilly city into a free open-air gallery of light and roofs.

# Bridges over the Tagus
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "25 de Abril", "items": ["opened 1966", "suspension bridge", "road and rail"]}, "r": {"t": "Vasco da Gama", "items": ["opened 1998", "about 12 km long", "road only"]}}
For centuries, crossing the Tagus at Lisbon meant taking a boat. In 1966 a great red suspension bridge opened, today called the 25 de Abril. In 1998 the Vasco da Gama Bridge followed, one of the longest in Europe.
Ferries still cross too, linking Lisbon with Cacilhas, Seixal and Barreiro.
---
Before 1966 the nearest fixed crossing was far upstream at Vila Franca de Xira, opened in 1951. Commuters from the south bank relied on ferries, the cacilheiros. The new bridge transformed the south bank: Almada, Seixal and Barreiro grew into big suburbs.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~2.3 km", "l": "25 de Abril, total length"}, {"v": "1,013 m", "l": "its main span"}, {"v": "~190 m", "l": "height of its towers"}]}
### The 25 de Abril Bridge
It opened on 6 August 1966 as the Salazar Bridge and was renamed after the 1974 revolution. Its main span was then the longest of any suspension bridge in Europe. It was designed to take trains as well, but the lower rail deck was added only in 1999. On the south bank, the Cristo Rei statue (1959) looks back over the city.
!! It was built by the makers of the Golden Gate => It is a separate 1960s design, built by the American Bridge Company, which had worked on San Francisco's other great bridge, the Bay Bridge
### The Vasco da Gama Bridge
Built for Expo 98, it crosses the widest part of the estuary, from Sacavém to Montijo, for about 12 km. Its piles go deep into soft river mud, and it was designed to resist a very strong earthquake.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1951", "l": "Vila Franca bridge"}, {"y": "1966", "l": "Salazar Bridge opens"}, {"y": "1974", "l": "Renamed 25 de Abril"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "Vasco da Gama Bridge"}, {"y": "1999", "l": "Trains cross the river"}]}
A third crossing, between Chelas and Barreiro, is planned together with the new Lisbon airport at Alcochete.
> Lisbon's bridges stitched the two banks of the Tagus into one metropolis.

# Parque das Nações
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before the 1990s", "items": ["oil depots", "slaughterhouse", "polluted riverbank"]}, "r": {"t": "After Expo 98", "items": ["homes and offices", "riverside park", "Oriente transport hub"]}}
Expo 98 transformed about 5 km of Lisbon's eastern waterfront, an area of oil depots, warehouses and a slaughterhouse.
After the fair closed, the site became Parque das Nações, a new district of homes, offices and gardens with some of Portugal's best-known modern architecture.
---
The Expo was planned as the first stage of a new piece of city. The area covered over 300 hectares along the river, and the fair's income and land sales were meant to pay for cleaning it up, building infrastructure and later housing.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Gare do Oriente", "b": "Calatrava, steel 'trees'"}, {"a": "Portugal Pavilion", "b": "Siza, hanging concrete roof"}, {"a": "Oceanário", "b": "Chermayeff, on the water"}, {"a": "Vasco da Gama Tower", "b": "now part of a hotel"}]}
### Architecture as the attraction
Gare do Oriente, by the Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava, has a platform roof of steel and glass shaped like a forest canopy. Álvaro Siza's Portugal Pavilion is famous for a thin concrete canopy that sags like a sheet of paper between two porticoes. The Oceanário, by the American Peter Chermayeff, is built around one huge central tank holding millions of litres of seawater.
### A new neighbourhood
• Over 20,000 people now live in the area
• The large arena built as the Pavilhão Atlântico hosts concerts and the Web Summit
• A cable car and riverside gardens run along the water

# Lisbon today
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Tourism boom", "Tech and start-ups", "Rising rents", "New airport planned"]}
Since the 2010s Lisbon has become one of Europe's most visited cities and a hub for start-ups. Old buildings have been restored and the centre buzzes with visitors.
The boom has a cost: rents and house prices have risen far faster than wages, pushing many residents out of the historic quarters.
---
In 2011 Portugal needed an international bailout, and parts of central Lisbon were half-empty, with crumbling buildings. Within a decade the picture changed. Cheap flights, a 2012 law that freed up rents, tax breaks for new foreign residents, golden visas and online short-term rentals (alojamento local) brought money and people.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "city population, thousands", "items": [{"l": "1981", "v": 808}, {"l": "2001", "v": 565}, {"l": "2011", "v": 548}, {"l": "2021", "v": 545, "hi": true}]}
### Winners and losers
The restoration of old buildings and new jobs in tourism and tech were real gains. The Web Summit, one of the world's largest technology conferences, has been held in Lisbon since 2016. But in some Alfama and Mouraria streets a large share of homes became tourist flats, and long-time residents moved out.
• New short-term rental licences in the historic centre have been restricted since the late 2010s
• Golden visas through property purchase were ended in 2023
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Gains", "items": ["restored buildings", "jobs in tourism, tech", "global profile"]}, "r": {"t": "Strains", "items": ["high rents", "residents leave centre", "crowded old quarters"]}}
### Looking ahead
Lisbon was European Green Capital in 2020. A new airport is planned at Alcochete, south of the Tagus, to replace the cramped Humberto Delgado airport inside the city, and the Metro is being extended with a circular line.
!! Lisbon is shrinking because people leave the region => Most moved within the metropolitan area, whose population has kept growing
> Lisbon's challenge now is to stay a city for those who live in it, not only a destination for those who visit.

# From Portus Cale to Porto
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "Iron Age", "l": "Hilltop settlement"}, {"y": "Roman era", "l": "Port of Portus Cale"}, {"y": "1120", "l": "Given to the bishop"}, {"y": "1415", "l": "Fleet for Ceuta"}, {"y": "1832–33", "l": "Siege: 'Invicta'"}]}
Porto grew on a granite hill above the Douro, near the river's mouth. Its Roman port, Portus Cale, gave its name to Portugal.
A proud merchant city, it is nicknamed "Invicta", the unconquered, and its people are "tripeiros", tripe-eaters, after a story from 1415.
---
The hill where Porto Cathedral stands, the Morro da Sé, has been lived on since the Iron Age. Under Roman rule, the road from Lisbon to Braga crossed the Douro here, and a port grew on the river. Later the Suevi and Visigoths held the area, and by the 6th century the town had a bishop.
### A city of merchants and bishops
In 1120 Countess Teresa gave the town to its bishop, and for centuries bishops and citizens argued over who ran it. Porto's wealth came from trade, fishing and shipbuilding, not from a royal court. By tradition, nobles were not even allowed to stay in the city for more than three days.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Morro da Sé", "b": "oldest hill, cathedral"}, {"a": "Fernandine walls", "b": "14th-century city walls"}, {"a": "Ribeira", "b": "merchants by the river"}, {"a": "Bolsa", "b": "19th-century exchange"}]}
### Two nicknames
• **Tripeiros**: in 1415 the city is said to have given all its meat to the fleet sailing to conquer Ceuta, keeping only the tripe. Tripas à moda do Porto is still a local dish
• **Invicta**: in 1832–33 Liberal forces under Dom Pedro held Porto through a year-long siege by the absolutist army of his brother Miguel
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["1832: Liberals land", "Porto besieged ~1 year", "City holds out", "Called 'Invicta'"]}
!? Dom Pedro was so grateful that he left his heart to the city; it is still kept in the Lapa church.
> Porto is a city built by merchants rather than kings, and proud of it.

# The Ribeira
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Tall, narrow coloured houses", "Cais da Ribeira quayside", "Palácio da Bolsa", "São Francisco church", "Flood marks on the walls"]}
The Ribeira is Porto's old riverside quarter: tall, narrow houses in bright colours stacked up the slope above the Douro quay.
Together with the old centre and the Dom Luís I Bridge, it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1996.
---
For centuries the Ribeira was Porto's working heart. Boats unloaded at the quay, merchants traded under the arcades of Praça da Ribeira, and families lived above their shops in houses only a few metres wide but four or five storeys tall. Tiles and paint protected the façades from the damp. By the 1970s much of the quarter was run-down and overcrowded, and its restoration since the 1980s helped win UNESCO status.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Sé and Clérigos on the hilltop", "Steep stairways and alleys", "Tall merchant houses", "Quay and the Douro"]}
### Treasures nearby
• The **Palácio da Bolsa**, the 19th-century stock exchange, has a lavish "Arab Room" inspired by the Alhambra
• **São Francisco church** looks plain outside but is covered inside with gilded carved wood (talha dourada)
• The **Clérigos Tower**, about 75 m tall, by the Italian-born architect Nicolau Nasoni, was finished in 1763
### Living with the river
The Douro can rise many metres in winter floods. Plaques on Ribeira walls mark the highest levels; the flood of 1909 was among the worst. Since big dams were built upstream, floods are less frequent, but not gone.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Porto, north bank", "items": ["Ribeira houses", "cathedral hill", "UNESCO core"]}, "r": {"t": "Gaia, south bank", "items": ["port wine lodges", "Serra do Pilar", "cable car"]}}
!? The UNESCO site also includes the Serra do Pilar monastery on the Gaia side, from where the classic view of the Ribeira is taken.
> The Ribeira is Porto's postcard: a vertical city of narrow houses, tied to the river that made it rich.

# Porto's bridges
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1877", "l": "Maria Pia (Eiffel)"}, {"y": "1886", "l": "Dom Luís I"}, {"y": "1963", "l": "Arrábida"}, {"y": "1991", "l": "São João (rail)"}, {"y": "2003", "l": "Infante D. Henrique"}]}
Six bridges cross the Douro between Porto and Gaia. The iron Maria Pia railway bridge (1877) was built by Gustave Eiffel's company.
The Dom Luís I Bridge (1886) has two decks: the upper one now carries the Metro, the lower one cars and walkers beside the Ribeira.
---
Before the 19th century the Douro at Porto was crossed by boat and, from 1806, by a bridge of boats. In 1809, during the French invasion, it gave way under crowds fleeing the troops, and many hundreds of people drowned.
### The age of iron
When the railway from Lisbon reached the Douro, engineers had to span a deep gorge. Eiffel's company won with the Maria Pia Bridge (1877), a single wrought-iron arch of 160 m, then the longest of its kind in the world. Its engineer, Théophile Seyrig, later left Eiffel and designed the Dom Luís I Bridge (1886), whose arch spans 172 m.

# Port wine
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Grapes crushed", "Fermentation starts", "Grape spirit added", "Sweet, strong wine", "Aged in Gaia lodges"]}
Port is a fortified wine from the Douro. Adding grape spirit part-way through fermentation stops the yeast, leaving natural sweetness and about 20% alcohol.
For centuries the wine was shipped down the river and aged in the lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia, opposite Porto, before export.
---
Yeast turns grape sugar into alcohol. If winemakers add a clear grape spirit (aguardente) of about 77% alcohol when only part of the sugar has fermented, the yeast stops working and the rest of the sugar stays in the wine. The result is sweet, strong and keeps well.
ex: About 4 parts fermenting grape juice | + 1 part grape spirit at 77% | Sweet wine of about 19–22% alcohol
### Why the British loved it
Wars with France in the late 1600s cut England off from French wine. The Methuen Treaty of 1703 lowered duties on Portuguese wine, and British merchants set up firms in Porto and Gaia. Names such as Taylor's, Croft, Graham's and Sandeman still show that history.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Port styles", "items": ["Ruby: young, fruity", "Tawny: aged in wood", "Vintage: one great year", "White and rosé"]}
### Styles
Ruby ports age briefly and keep a deep red colour and fresh fruit. Tawny ports age for years in small wooden casks, slowly turning amber and nutty. Vintage port comes from a single outstanding year, ages mostly in the bottle and can last for decades. Today the Douro and Port Wine Institute controls quality and labels.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "77%", "l": "strength of grape spirit"}, {"v": "~20%", "l": "alcohol in port"}, {"v": "1703", "l": "Methuen Treaty"}]}
!? Until 1986, port had to be aged and shipped from the lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia; only then could Douro farms export directly.
> Port is wine with its fermentation deliberately stopped, a trick that made it sweet, strong and able to travel.

# The Douro valley
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "1756", "l": "region demarcated"}, {"v": "2001", "l": "UNESCO World Heritage"}, {"v": "3", "l": "sub-regions"}]}
Upstream from Porto, the Douro cuts through steep schist hills that people have carved into terraces of vines over centuries.
The Alto Douro Wine Region, one of the oldest demarcated wine regions in the world, has been a UNESCO cultural landscape since 2001.
---
The wine valley stretches for about 100 km towards the Spanish border. Mountains to the west, especially the Serra do Marão, block much of the Atlantic rain, so the upper valley has hot, dry summers, often above 40 °C, and cold winters. Vines thrive in the poor, stony schist, which splits into layers that roots can follow deep down to find water.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Atlantic rain", "Blocked by Serra do Marão", "Hot, dry valley", "Vines root in schist"]}
### Built by hand
The slopes are so steep that almost everything was done by hand. Workers broke the schist and built dry-stone walls to make narrow terraces (socalcos). Later terraces were cut by machine into wider earth banks (patamares), and some newer vineyards run straight up and down the slope.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old socalcos", "items": ["dry-stone walls", "built by hand", "narrow rows"]}, "r": {"t": "Patamares", "items": ["earth banks", "cut by machine", "tractors can pass"]}}
### Crisis and recovery
From the 1860s phylloxera, a tiny insect from America that attacks vine roots, killed most of the vines. Many terraces were abandoned and can still be seen as overgrown "mortórios". Vines were replanted on American rootstocks, which resist the pest.
• Baixo Corgo is the wettest sub-region, Cima Corgo around Pinhão the heart of port, and Douro Superior the driest
• The September harvest is still largely done by hand, and some quintas still tread grapes by foot in stone tanks called lagares
!? The railway station at Pinhão is decorated with azulejo panels showing scenes of Douro life and the harvest.
> The Douro is a landscape built by people, stone by stone, to grow wine where little else would grow.

# Rabelo boats
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Flat bottom", "b": "for shallow, rocky water"}, {"a": "Square sail", "b": "for the trip back upriver"}, {"a": "Long steering oar", "b": "worked from a high deck"}, {"a": "Cargo", "b": "pipes of wine, ~550 litres"}]}
For centuries, port wine travelled from the Douro farms to Gaia in rabelos: flat-bottomed wooden boats with a square sail and a long steering oar.
Railways, roads and dams ended the trade, but rabelos still float by the Gaia lodges and race on São João's day.
---
Before the railway, the Douro was the only practical road out of the wine valley. After the wine had rested through the winter, barrels called pipes, of about 550 litres each, were loaded onto rabelos at riverside quays and carried downstream to the lodges in Gaia.
### A dangerous river
The Douro was then fast and full of rapids and rocks. A crew of a handful of men steered with the long oar from a raised platform at the stern, using the current to go down. Coming back, they sailed with the sea breeze and were towed from the bank. Many boats and lives were lost.
@viz {"k": "cycle", "steps": ["Load pipes at the quinta", "Run the rapids down", "Unload at Gaia", "Sail and tow back up"]}
The worst obstacle was the Cachão da Valeira gorge, which blocked boats completely until rocks were blasted away in the 1780s and 1790s, opening the upper Douro.
### The end of an era
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1792", "l": "Valeira gorge opened"}, {"y": "1887", "l": "Railway to Spain"}, {"y": "20th c.", "l": "Lorries replace boats"}, {"y": "1980s", "l": "Dams and locks built"}]}
Five dams on the Portuguese Douro now have locks; the one at Carrapatelo lifts boats about 35 m, one of the highest in Europe. Today cruise boats, not rabelos, carry most people up the valley.
!? Since the 1980s the port houses have raced their rabelos at the mouth of the Douro on 24 June, São João's day.
> The rabelo was the Douro's lorry, built for a wild river that dams have since tamed.

# Braga and Guimarães
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Braga", "items": ["Roman Bracara Augusta", "seat of archbishops", "Bom Jesus (UNESCO)"]}, "r": {"t": "Guimarães", "items": ["early royal seat", "castle, ducal palace", "old town (UNESCO)"]}}
Two old cities of the Minho sit about 25 km apart. Braga, founded by the Romans, has been a religious capital for over 1,500 years and is crowded with churches.
Guimarães, linked to Portugal's first king, has a beautifully kept medieval centre listed by UNESCO.
---
Braga began as Bracara Augusta, founded around 16 BC under Emperor Augustus, and later became the capital of the Roman province of Gallaecia. In the 5th and 6th centuries it was the capital of the Suevi kingdom. Its archbishops long claimed to be "primate of the Spains", the senior churchmen of all Iberia, a title disputed with Toledo.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~16 BC", "l": "Bracara Augusta founded"}, {"y": "400s", "l": "Suevi capital"}, {"y": "1882", "l": "Bom Jesus funicular"}, {"y": "2001", "l": "Guimarães on UNESCO"}, {"y": "2019", "l": "Bom Jesus on UNESCO"}]}
### Bom Jesus do Monte
Above Braga, a zigzag Baroque stairway climbs a wooded hill to a church. Its chapels and fountains represent the Stations of the Cross, the five senses and the three virtues, and pilgrims traditionally climb it, some on their knees. Beside it runs a funicular from 1882 that is powered by water.

### Guimarães
Guimarães grew around a 10th-century monastery founded by Countess Mumadona Dias and a castle built to protect it. Its granite streets, squares and balconied houses were carefully restored from the 1980s, earning UNESCO listing in 2001. It was a European Capital of Culture in 2012, and today its university and young population keep the old centre lively.

# Granite and green
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Granite houses and walls", "Heavy Atlantic rain", "Oak, chestnut and pine", "Small, divided farm plots", "Rivers Minho, Lima, Cávado"]}
The north-west of Portugal is built of granite and soaked by Atlantic rain. Walls, houses, churches and even vine posts are made of it.
Mild, wet weather keeps the Minho green all year, with small fields, woods and fast rivers flowing down from the mountains.
---
Much of northern Portugal sits on granite and schist formed around 300 million years ago, when ancient continents collided to build the supercontinent Pangaea. Molten rock cooled slowly deep underground into granite, and erosion has since brought it to the surface as rounded hills and huge boulders.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Continents collide", "Magma cools deep down", "Granite forms", "Erosion exposes it"]}
### A granite culture
Because granite was everywhere, northern builders used it for almost everything: house walls, steps, window frames, wayside crosses, wine presses, granaries and posts for vines. In Porto, granite door and window frames contrast with façades covered in coloured azulejos.
### Why so green
Wet winds from the Atlantic rise over the coastal mountains and drop their rain. The Peneda-Gerês mountains receive around 3,000 mm in a year, among the highest totals in Europe, while parts of the Alentejo get less than a fifth of that.

• Land is split into many small plots, a legacy of inheritance shared among children
• The Minho has long been one of the most densely settled rural areas of Portugal
• Rivers rise fast after storms and have powered mills for centuries
!? Granite contains traces of uranium, so radon gas can build up in old stone houses; ventilating ground floors and cellars keeps levels down.
> The north is a land of granite and rain: hard stone underfoot and green growth all around.

# The industrious North
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Vale do Ave", "b": "textiles and clothing"}, {"a": "Felgueiras", "b": "footwear"}, {"a": "Santa Maria da Feira", "b": "cork processing"}, {"a": "Leixões", "b": "port for exports"}]}
The North is Portugal's manufacturing heartland. Thousands of family firms make textiles, clothing, shoes, furniture and cork products, much of it for export.
After hard years of competition from Asia, many moved upmarket, selling design, speed and quality rather than low prices.
---
Industry in the North grew from water and people. Fast rivers like the Ave powered 19th-century textile mills, and the crowded countryside supplied workers who often kept a small farm on the side. Small and medium-sized family firms, rather than giant factories, became the norm.

### Shock and recovery
When China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and global textile quotas ended in 2005, many northern factories closed and unemployment rose in the Ave valley. The survivors changed strategy: technical fabrics, small and fast orders for European fashion brands, and their own labels. Being two or three days by lorry from Paris or Madrid became an advantage over factories on the other side of the world.
### Products the world knows

# Born from the ocean floor
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Azores", "items": ["near Mid-Atlantic Ridge", "where 3 plates meet", "young, still active"]}, "r": {"t": "Madeira", "items": ["inside African plate", "over a hotspot", "older, quieter"]}}
Both archipelagos are the tops of huge volcanoes that rise thousands of metres from the Atlantic floor.
The Azores sit where three tectonic plates meet, near the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Madeira lies in the middle of the African plate, above a hotspot of hot, rising mantle rock.
---
At the Mid-Atlantic Ridge the sea floor is pulling apart by roughly 2 cm a year, and molten rock wells up to fill the gap. Near the Azores this happens across an unusually broad zone, the Azores plateau, probably because a plume of hot mantle also rises beneath it. The extra melt built a cluster of volcanoes tall enough to break the surface.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Plates pull apart", "Hot mantle rises, melts", "Lava piles up on seabed", "Volcano breaks surface"]}
### Hotspot islands
Madeira formed differently. It sits over a hotspot, a long-lived column of hot mantle, while the African plate creeps slowly over it. Each volcano is carried away from the source and goes quiet, and a new one grows above the hotspot. That is why Porto Santo, to the north-east, is much older and more worn down than Madeira.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "age, million years (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Porto Santo", "v": 14, "t": "~14"}, {"l": "Santa Maria", "v": 7, "t": "~6–8"}, {"l": "Madeira", "v": 5, "t": "~5"}, {"l": "Pico", "v": 0.3, "t": "<0.3", "hi": true}]}
### Youngest and oldest
• **Santa Maria**, the oldest Azorean island, is the only one with marine fossils and pale sandy beaches
• **Pico**, the youngest, is a near-perfect cone reaching 2,351 m, the highest point in Portugal
• Porto Santo's long golden beach is made of crushed shells and old reef, not volcanic rock
Because the islands are so young, their soils are thin on the newest lava and rich on older ash, and their coasts are mostly steep cliffs.

# Volcanoes still awake
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1522", "l": "Vila Franca landslide"}, {"y": "1957–58", "l": "Capelinhos eruption"}, {"y": "1980", "l": "Terceira earthquake"}, {"y": "1998", "l": "Undersea eruption"}]}
The Azores are still geologically active. Earthquakes are frequent, and several volcanoes have erupted since settlement, both on land and under the sea.
The 1957–58 Capelinhos eruption on Faial added new land to the island and led thousands of islanders to emigrate to the United States.
---
Since the Portuguese arrived, the Azores have seen many eruptions, on land and offshore, and hundreds of felt earthquakes. Madeira, by contrast, has had no eruption in historical times.
### Capelinhos
In September 1957 a volcano began erupting from the sea just off the western tip of Faial. For about 13 months it threw out ash, steam and lava, building a new peninsula of about 2.4 km² and burying nearby villages and the lighthouse in ash. No one died, but farms and homes were ruined.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Undersea eruption, 1957", "Ash buries farms", "New land joins Faial", "US opens its doors"]}
In 1958 the US Congress passed the Azorean Refugee Act, backed by Senator John F. Kennedy, which offered special visas to families hit by the eruption and opened the way for a large wave of emigration.
### Earthquakes and landslides
In 1522 an earthquake set off a landslide that buried Vila Franca do Campo, then the main town of São Miguel, killing thousands. On 1 January 1980 an earthquake of about magnitude 7 struck Terceira, São Jorge and Graciosa, killing about 60 people and badly damaging Angra do Heroísmo, which was rebuilt and listed by UNESCO in 1983.
### Living with it

# Empty islands
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1418", "l": "Porto Santo reached"}, {"y": "1419", "l": "Madeira explored"}, {"y": "1427", "l": "Azores sighted"}, {"y": "1439", "l": "Settlement authorised"}]}
Unlike the Canary Islands, Madeira and the Azores had no native people when Portuguese sailors arrived in the early 1400s.
Settlers came from mainland Portugal and, on some Azorean islands, from Flanders. They cleared forest, brought animals and crops, and built new societies from scratch.
---
Madeira was explored by João Gonçalves Zarco and Tristão Vaz Teixeira around 1419–20, sent by Prince Henry. Its name means "wood", after the dense forest. Settlers arrived from about 1425, and the islands were split into captaincies, each run by a captain on behalf of the crown.

Bartolomeu Perestrelo's daughter Filipa later married Christopher Columbus, who lived for a time on Porto Santo and in Madeira.
### Clearing the land
Chroniclers tell that settlers set fire to Madeira's forest and it burned for seven years. That is surely exaggerated, but huge areas were cleared. Rabbits released on Porto Santo multiplied so fast that they ruined early crops. In the Azores, sheep, goats and pigs were often left on islands before settlers arrived, so there would be food waiting.

Most settlers came from the Algarve, Alentejo and Minho. On Faial and Pico, Flemish families arrived under Josse van Huerter, whose name is said to survive in the town of Horta. Enslaved Africans were also brought, especially to Madeira's sugar plantations.

# Sugar, oranges and tea
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Wheat and woad", "Madeira sugar boom", "Wine and oranges", "Dairy, tea, tourism"]}
The islands' economies have changed again and again. Madeira had a sugar boom in the late 1400s, then turned to wine. The Azores grew wheat and dye plants, then exported oranges to Britain.
Today dairy farming dominates the Azores, while tourism leads in Madeira.
---
### Madeira's white gold
Sugar cane thrived on Madeira's warm south side. By around 1500 Madeira was the leading sugar producer of the Atlantic world, worked largely by enslaved people from Africa and the Canaries, and its mills and water channels became a model later copied in São Tomé and Brazil. Brazil's larger, cheaper sugar soon undercut it, and Madeira turned to wine.

### The Azores' crops
Early settlers grew wheat for Portugal's forts in North Africa and woad (pastel), a plant whose blue dye sold well in Flanders. Angra, on Terceira, became a vital stop for fleets returning from India and Brazil. In the 1800s São Miguel shipped huge numbers of oranges, mostly to Britain, until disease and competition ended the trade from the 1860s.
Growers then tried new crops. Pineapples were raised in glasshouses, and tea was planted with the help of experts brought from Macau in 1878.

### Today
• Cows are said to outnumber people in the Azores, and cheeses like São Jorge are famous

• Tourism is now the biggest earner in Madeira and growing fast in the Azores

# An Atlantic climate
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "yearly mean, °C (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Funchal", "v": 19, "hi": true}, {"l": "Ponta Delgada", "v": 17.5}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 17.5}, {"l": "Bragança", "v": 12.5}]}
The ocean keeps both archipelagos mild all year. Madeira is subtropical, with warm winters on its sunny south coast. The Azores are cooler, wetter and windier, with changeable skies.
The mountains create big contrasts: wet, cloudy north slopes and drier south coasts.
---
The sea changes temperature slowly, so islands far from land rarely get very hot or very cold. In Funchal, winter afternoons are often around 19–20 °C and summer days rarely pass 30 °C. Frost happens only on the highest peaks, where snow falls now and then in winter.

### The Azores High
The Azores lie near a zone of high pressure over the subtropical Atlantic, the Azores High. In summer it usually grows and shelters the islands, giving long settled spells. In winter it often shifts south, and Atlantic depressions sweep through with rain and gales. The Azores therefore get much less sunshine than Madeira, and hydrangeas thrive in the damp.
### Storms and floods
• In October 2019 Hurricane Lorenzo passed close to Flores and Corvo and wrecked much of the port at Lajes das Flores
• In February 2010 flash floods and mudslides in Madeira killed more than 40 people, mostly around Funchal
• On steep islands, heavy rain turns into a torrent within minutes
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Heavy rain on peaks", "Steep ravines fill", "Torrents carry rocks", "Floods hit the coast"]}
Madeira now builds channels and barriers in its ravines to slow these floods.
!? The Azores are not tropical: the sea around them is usually about 16 °C in late winter and around 23 °C at the end of summer.
> The ocean is the islands' thermostat: mild all year, but open to storms from every side.

# Laurel forest survivors
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Madeira", "b": "largest surviving area"}, {"a": "Azores", "b": "small scattered patches"}, {"a": "Canaries", "b": "remnants, e.g. La Gomera"}, {"a": "Priolo", "b": "rare Azores bullfinch"}]}
Madeira's laurel forest is a famous survivor, but laurisilva once covered much of the Azores too. There, most was cut down for fields and pasture, and invasive plants now threaten the rest.
Protecting these forests also protects rare island birds such as the priolo of São Miguel.
---
Laurisilva is a humid evergreen forest found in Macaronesia, the island groups of the Azores, Madeira and the Canaries. Each archipelago has its own endemic species, because plants and animals that arrived from the mainland evolved in isolation for millions of years.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Settlers clear forest", "Pasture, plantations", "Invasive plants spread", "Native species squeezed"]}
### The Azores' lost forest
When settlers arrived, laurel and juniper forest covered much of the Azores. Today native forest survives mainly on steep slopes, high ridges and in protected areas. Much of the land is pasture, or plantations of Japanese cedar (cryptomeria), a fast-growing timber tree introduced in the 1800s.
### Invaders
• Kahili ginger (conteira), a showy Himalayan plant, smothers the forest floor and stream banks
• Australian cheesewood (incenso) has spread across lowland woods
• Rangers and volunteers clear invaders and replant native species
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Clear ginger and cheesewood", "Grow native seedlings", "Replant cleared slopes", "Monitor birds and plants"]}
### The priolo
The Azores bullfinch, or priolo, lives only in the native forest around Pico da Vara in eastern São Miguel. In the 1990s just a few hundred birds were thought to survive, making it one of Europe's rarest birds. Removing invaders and replanting the native plants whose seeds and buds it eats helped numbers rise to around a thousand, and its threat status has been lowered.

# From whaling to whale-watching
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Whaling", "items": ["open boats, harpoons", "sperm whales", "ended 1987"]}, "r": {"t": "Whale-watching", "items": ["since 1989", "same lookouts, vigias", "whales and dolphins"]}}
For about a century, Azorean men hunted sperm whales from small open boats with hand-thrown harpoons. The last one was killed in 1987.
Two years later the first whale-watching company started on Pico. Today whale-watching is a major part of the islands' tourism.
---
Whaling came to the Azores with American ships from New England, which stopped in the islands for water, food and crew. Many young Azoreans signed on, and some settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts. In the 1800s islanders started their own shore-based whaling.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Vigia spots a spout", "Rocket calls the crew", "Boats row and sail out", "Harpoon thrown by hand", "Whale towed ashore"]}
### How it worked
Lookouts in stone huts on the cliffs, the vigias, scanned the sea. When they saw a spout, they fired a rocket, and men ran from fields and homes to the boats. The canoas were light, about 11 m long, and rowed or sailed by a crew of seven. The harpooner struck by hand, and a wounded whale could drag the boat for hours. Oil was then extracted at shore factories.
### The end of the hunt
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1800s", "l": "Shore whaling begins"}, {"y": "1981", "l": "Madeira stops"}, {"y": "1986", "l": "Global moratorium"}, {"y": "1987", "l": "Last Azores hunt"}, {"y": "1989", "l": "Whale-watching starts"}]}
### Sea giants today
• Sperm whales live around the islands all year
• Blue, fin and sei whales pass through in spring on migration
• Common, bottlenose and Risso's dolphins are seen on most trips
The whalers' museum at Lajes do Pico keeps the boats, tools and stories of the hunt.
!? In Moby-Dick (1851), Herman Melville noted that many whaling crews were filled out with hardy Azorean islanders.
> The Azores turned a hunting tradition into a watching one, using the same lookouts and knowledge of the sea.

# How autonomy works
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Portuguese state: defence, courts", "Representative of the Republic", "Regional Legislative Assembly", "Regional Government"]}
Since 1976 the Azores and Madeira have been autonomous regions, with their own elected parliaments and governments that run most local affairs.
They are also "outermost regions" of the European Union, which allows special support and rules because of their distance and small island economies.
---
Autonomy was a response to geography and history. The islands are far from Lisbon, and after the 1974 revolution independence movements briefly appeared in both, notably the FLA in the Azores and FLAMA in Madeira. The 1976 Constitution answered with self-government inside the Portuguese state.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Regional assembly", "b": "makes regional laws"}, {"a": "Regional government", "b": "health, schools, transport"}, {"a": "Representative", "b": "of the Republic, signs laws"}, {"a": "Lisbon", "b": "defence, foreign affairs"}]}
### Money and taxes
The regions keep most of the taxes raised on the islands and also receive transfers from the state budget. They may lower some taxes, which is why VAT rates are lower than on the mainland. Madeira also has a low-tax International Business Centre, whose rules have been examined by the European Commission.
### Outermost regions
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Far from mainland Europe", "Small markets, high costs", "Special EU funds and rules", "Full EU membership"]}
The EU recognises nine outermost regions: the Azores, Madeira, the Canary Islands and six French territories. EU treaties allow special measures for them, such as support for transport and farming.
### Politics
Madeira was led for 37 years, from 1978 to 2015, by Alberto João Jardim of the PSD. The Azores were governed by the PS from 1996 to 2020, and since then by a PSD-led coalition.
!? Lajes air base on Terceira, used by Allied forces since 1943, is still shared by the Portuguese and US air forces.
> Autonomy lets the islands govern most daily matters themselves while remaining fully part of Portugal and the EU.

# Madeira wine
@viz {"k": "scale", "lo": 0, "hi": 4, "unit": "sweetness", "items": [{"l": "Sercial", "v": 0.5}, {"l": "Verdelho", "v": 1.5}, {"l": "Boal", "v": 2.5}, {"l": "Malvasia", "v": 3.5, "hi": true}]}
Madeira wine is fortified like port, but then deliberately warmed for months or years. This "cooking" gives it nutty, caramel flavours and makes it almost indestructible.
Four classic grapes give four styles, from dry Sercial to rich, sweet Malvasia, also called Malmsey.
---
In the 1600s and 1700s, ships bound for India and the Americas called at Funchal and took casks of wine aboard. Merchants noticed that wine which had crossed the tropics and come back, called vinho da roda ("round-trip wine"), tasted better. Producers then found ways to copy the effect at home.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Estufagem", "items": ["tanks heated ~45–50 °C", "at least 3 months", "younger, cheaper wines"]}, "r": {"t": "Canteiro", "items": ["casks in warm lofts", "years to decades", "the finest wines"]}}
### How it is made
Like port, Madeira is fortified with grape spirit, added early for sweet styles and late for dry ones. Then comes the warming. Because the wine has already been heated and exposed to air, an opened bottle keeps for months, and some bottles from the 1700s and 1800s are still drinkable.
• **Sercial**: the driest, sharp and fresh
• **Verdelho**: medium-dry
• **Boal** (Bual): medium-rich
• **Malvasia** (Malmsey): the richest and sweetest
• **Tinta Negra**, a red grape, is the workhorse of most everyday blends
### Famous fans
Madeira was a favourite wine in colonial America, and it is often said that it was used to toast the US Declaration of Independence in 1776. Shakespeare mentions Malmsey, and an old story says the Duke of Clarence was drowned in a barrel of it in 1478.

# A small country, a huge sea
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "~1.7 M km²", "l": "exclusive economic zone"}, {"v": "~18×", "l": "Portugal's land area"}, {"v": "370 km", "l": "zone width (200 nm)"}]}
Portugal's exclusive economic zone (EEZ), the sea where it controls fishing, energy and seabed resources, covers about 1.7 million km². That is roughly 18 times its land area and one of the largest in Europe.
The waters around the Azores make up more than half of it.
---
Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982), a coastal state has full sovereignty over a territorial sea of 12 nautical miles, about 22 km. Beyond it, out to 200 nautical miles, about 370 km, lies the EEZ. There the state controls fish, oil, gas, minerals and energy, but other countries' ships and planes may pass freely.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Coast and internal waters", "Territorial sea: 12 nm", "Contiguous zone: 24 nm", "EEZ: 200 nm"]}
### Islands make the difference
Every island generates its own 200-mile zone. The Azores and Madeira lie scattered far out in the Atlantic, so each adds an enormous area of sea.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "thousand km² of EEZ (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Azores", "v": 950, "hi": true}, {"l": "Madeira", "v": 450}, {"l": "Mainland", "v": 330}]}
ex: Land: about 92,000 km² | Sea (EEZ): about 1,700,000 km² | Ratio: about 18 to 1
### What it means
• The navy and air force patrol it against illegal fishing and pollution
• Portugal also coordinates search and rescue over an even larger part of the Atlantic, from Lisbon and Ponta Delgada
• It holds potential resources, from fish to offshore wind and seabed minerals
Most of this sea is very deep: beyond the narrow shelf, the floor drops to 3,000–5,000 m.
!! Portugal is a small country => On land it is mid-sized for Europe; counting its sea, it is one of the largest
> Counting its sea, Portugal is not a small country at all.

# Claiming the seabed
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Map the seabed", "Prove the shelf continues", "Submit to the UN, 2009", "Commission reviews"]}
Beyond 200 nautical miles, a country can claim rights to the seabed if it proves the seabed is a natural extension of its land. Portugal submitted such a claim to the United Nations in 2009.
If accepted, it would add about 2 million km² of seabed, bringing the total to around 4 million km².
---
Under UNCLOS, the continental shelf means the seabed and what lies beneath it, not the water above. A state automatically has rights over it out to 200 nm. Further out, usually up to 350 nm, it must show with geological and depth data that the sea floor is a natural prolongation of its territory.
### Years of mapping
A special team, the EMEPC (Task Group for the Extension of the Continental Shelf), was set up in 2005 to gather the evidence. Using research ships, sonar and a remotely operated robot called Luso, able to dive to 6,000 m, it surveyed vast areas of the sea floor.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "EEZ", "items": ["water and seabed", "out to 200 nm", "fish, energy, minerals"]}, "r": {"t": "Extended shelf", "items": ["seabed only", "beyond 200 nm", "minerals, seabed life"]}}
### Under review
The UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf, a panel of scientists, studies claims one at a time, and each review can take many years. Portugal has added updated data since 2009. Some areas overlap with seabed claimed by Spain to the south, near the Selvagens and the Canary Islands, which would have to be settled between the two countries.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "million km² (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Land", "v": 0.09, "t": "0.09"}, {"l": "EEZ", "v": 1.7, "t": "1.7"}, {"l": "EEZ + shelf claim", "v": 4, "t": "~4", "hi": true}]}
ex: Seabed if claim accepted ≈ 4,000,000 km² | Land ≈ 92,000 km² | About 40 times the land area
!? The extended area would include seamounts and hydrothermal vents, among the least explored places on Earth.
> Portugal is seeking rights to the seabed far beyond its coast through science and law rather than force.

# Cold, rich waters
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["North winds in summer", "Surface water pushed out", "Cold deep water rises", "Nutrients feed plankton", "Fish thrive"]}
Swimmers on Portugal's west coast know the Atlantic is cold even in August. The reason is upwelling: summer north winds push surface water away from the coast, and cold, deep water rises to replace it.
That deep water is full of nutrients, which is why these seas are so rich in fish.
---
In summer the Azores High to the west and low pressure over hot Iberia make north winds, the nortada, blow along the coast. Because of the Earth's rotation, moving water in the northern hemisphere is deflected to the right, so the surface layer drifts west, away from the shore. Deep water, cold and full of nutrients, wells up to take its place.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "August sea temp., °C (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Viana do Castelo", "v": 17}, {"l": "Cascais", "v": 18}, {"l": "Algarve (south)", "v": 22, "hi": true}, {"l": "Valencia", "v": 26}]}
### A food factory
Nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates fertilise microscopic algae, the phytoplankton. They feed tiny animals, the zooplankton, which feed sardines, mackerel and anchovies, and in turn bigger fish, dolphins, seabirds and people. Upwelling coasts cover a tiny share of the world's ocean but supply a large share of the world's fish catch.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Dolphins, seabirds, people", "Sardines and mackerel", "Zooplankton", "Phytoplankton", "Nutrients from the deep"]}
### Why the south is warmer
The Algarve's south coast faces away from the north winds and is sheltered, so upwelling there is weaker. Its beaches are often several degrees warmer than those near Lisbon or in the Minho. On the west coast, a sudden cold spell in the water usually means the nortada has been blowing hard for days.
!? Iberia's upwelling is the northern end of the Canary Current system, one of the four great coastal upwelling regions of the world, along with those off California, Peru and south-west Africa.
> Portugal's cold summer sea is the price of its rich fishing grounds.

# A nation of fish eaters
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "kg per person a year (approx.)", "items": [{"l": "Portugal", "v": 57, "hi": true}, {"l": "Spain", "v": 43}, {"l": "EU average", "v": 24}]}
The Portuguese eat more fish and seafood per person than anyone else in the EU, about 57 kg a year, more than twice the EU average.
Grilled sardines, octopus, horse mackerel and clams are everyday food, landed by a fleet made up mostly of small boats.
---
Fishing has shaped coastal towns from Caminha to Vila Real de Santo António. Most of the fleet's several thousand vessels are under 12 m long and fish close to shore with nets, lines and traps. Larger trawlers and purse-seiners work further out. Fishing is now a small share of jobs, but it keeps harbours, markets and restaurants alive along the whole coast.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sardine", "b": "purse-seine, summer favourite"}, {"a": "Octopus", "b": "pots and traps, Algarve"}, {"a": "Horse mackerel", "b": "carapau, cheap and common"}, {"a": "Clams, cockles", "b": "Ria Formosa, Aveiro"}]}
### The sardine crisis
Sardines are the symbol of the summer festivals, but the stock off Iberia fell sharply in the 2010s. Portugal and Spain cut catches hard, at times closing the fishery for months. The stock then recovered and catches were raised again, though scientists keep a close watch, because sardine numbers swing with ocean conditions.
### From boat to plate
• Fresh fish must first be sold at public auctions (lotas) run by the state company Docapesca
• Canneries in Matosinhos, Peniche and Olhão turn sardines, mackerel and tuna into tins, a tradition from the 1800s
• Portugal still imports much of the fish it eats, including almost all of its cod

!? Portuguese tinned fish has become a gourmet export, sold in brightly decorated tins in specialist shops around the world.
> Fish is part of Portuguese identity, and keeping it on the table means fishing only what the sea can replace.

# The cod story
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1500s", "l": "Off Newfoundland"}, {"y": "1934", "l": "Cod campaign begins"}, {"y": "1950s", "l": "White Fleet at peak"}, {"y": "1970s", "l": "Distant fishing ends"}]}
Cod doesn't live in Portuguese waters, yet bacalhau is the national dish. For centuries, Portuguese ships sailed to Newfoundland and Greenland to catch it and salt it.
Today most cod comes from Norway and Iceland, and it is still eaten salted, soaked and cooked in hundreds of ways.
---
Salted, dried cod keeps for months without a fridge, so it suited a seafaring Catholic country with many meatless fast days. Portuguese fishermen were working the Grand Banks off Newfoundland soon after 1500, using salt from Aveiro and the Tagus.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Caught in the far north", "Split and salted on board", "Dried in the sun", "Soaked for 1–3 days", "Cooked"]}
### The White Fleet
Under the Estado Novo, a state-organised "cod campaign" from 1934 aimed to make Portugal self-sufficient. Each spring the White Fleet, white-painted sailing ships and trawlers, left for months on the Grand Banks and off Greenland. Men fished alone from small flat-bottomed boats called dories, with hand lines, often in fog and icy water, and many never came home.
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Lugre", "b": "sailing ship of the fleet"}, {"a": "Dóri", "b": "one-man rowing boat"}, {"a": "Gil Eannes", "b": "hospital and supply ship"}, {"a": "Ílhavo", "b": "museum of the cod fleet"}]}
The hospital ship Gil Eannes sailed with them; it is now a museum in Viana do Castelo.
### Why it ended
In 1977 Canada extended its fishing limits to 200 nm, closing most of the Grand Banks to foreign fleets. Overfishing then caused the collapse of Newfoundland cod, and Canada closed that fishery in 1992. Portugal turned to imports, mainly from Norway, where cod is now often salted especially for the Portuguese market.
!! Bacalhau is a Portuguese fish => It is Atlantic cod from cold northern seas; Portugal has almost no cod of its own and imports nearly all of it
> Bacalhau is a taste of history: a fish from distant northern seas that became the heart of Portuguese cooking.

# Nazaré's giant waves
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Atlantic winter swell", "Canyon keeps waves fast", "Waves meet at Praia do Norte", "Giant breaking peak"]}
Nazaré's Praia do Norte produces some of the biggest surfable waves on Earth, over 20 m on the best winter days.
The secret lies underwater: the Nazaré Canyon, a gorge in the seabed up to about 5,000 m deep, runs almost to the beach and focuses the energy of Atlantic swells.
---
Normally, as waves approach a coast, the shallow continental shelf slows them and drains their energy. At Nazaré, the canyon, well over 150 km long, cuts straight through the shelf to within about a kilometre of the shore, so waves arrive with much of their power intact.
### How the canyon builds waves
Waves travelling over the deep canyon keep their speed, while those crossing the shallow shelf on either side slow down and bend. The two sets meet near the beach and add together into a towering peak, and local currents can steepen them further.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Over the canyon", "items": ["deep water", "waves stay fast", "energy kept"]}, "r": {"t": "Over the shelf", "items": ["shallow water", "waves slow and bend", "turn towards canyon"]}}
### Records
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "2011", "l": "McNamara, ~24 m"}, {"y": "Feb 2020", "l": "Gabeira, ~22 m"}, {"y": "Oct 2020", "l": "Steudtner, ~26 m"}]}
Big-wave surfers at Nazaré use tow-in surfing: a jet-ski pulls them onto the wave, because waves this fast are almost impossible to paddle into. A second jet-ski waits to rescue them after a fall. The best season is roughly October to March, when deep Atlantic storms send long swells.
### Watching
The red lighthouse at the Fort of São Miguel Arcanjo, on the headland, is the classic viewpoint, and crowds gather there on big days. Before surfers came, Nazaré was known as a fishing town, where boats were hauled up the beach by oxen.
!! Nazaré's waves are caused by earthquakes or tsunamis => They are ordinary storm swells, focused and amplified by the shape of the seabed
> At Nazaré, the shape of the seabed turns ordinary Atlantic swells into giants.

# Ocean science and protection
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Deep-sea vents near the Azores", "Marine protected areas", "Fisheries and climate research", "UN Ocean Conference 2022"]}
Portugal's huge sea makes it an important place for ocean research. Hot springs on the seabed near the Azores, deep canyons and seamounts host life found nowhere else.
In 2024 the Azores created the largest network of marine protected areas in the North Atlantic, covering 30% of its sea.
---
Ocean science in Portugal has royal roots. King Carlos I, who reigned from 1889 to 1908, was a keen oceanographer who led research cruises off the coast on his yachts, collecting deep-sea fish and studying the sea. Today institutes such as IPMA, CIIMAR in Matosinhos and Okeanos at the University of the Azores carry on the work.
### Life without sunlight
In the 1990s scientists found hydrothermal vents on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge south-west of the Azores, with names like Lucky Strike, Menez Gwen and Rainbow. Seawater seeps into hot rock, heats to over 300 °C and gushes out loaded with minerals. Bacteria use these chemicals for energy, and shrimps, mussels and crabs live on the bacteria, in a food web that runs without sunlight.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Seawater seeps into rock", "Heated to over 300 °C", "Gushes out with minerals", "Bacteria use chemicals", "Mussels, shrimp thrive"]}
### Protecting the sea
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Azores network", "b": "30% of its sea, 2024"}, {"a": "Selvagens", "b": "large no-take reserve"}, {"a": "Gorringe Bank", "b": "seamount off the Algarve"}, {"a": "Berlengas", "b": "UNESCO biosphere reserve"}]}
In the Azores network, about half of the protected area is closed to all fishing. Lisbon co-hosted the UN Ocean Conference with Kenya in 2022.
Some vents and seamounts hold metal-rich deposits, and many scientists warn that mining them could destroy ecosystems before they are understood.
!? Some vent microbes and animals survive temperatures and chemicals that would kill almost any other life, and are studied for clues to how life began.
> Portugal's waters hold some of the Atlantic's strangest life, and the country now protects a growing share of them.

# Ports and cables
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Sines", "b": "deep water, containers, gas"}, {"a": "Leixões", "b": "the North's main port"}, {"a": "Lisbon", "b": "cruises, food, containers"}, {"a": "Setúbal, Aveiro", "b": "cars, bulk, industry"}]}
Portugal sits beside some of the world's busiest shipping lanes, between Europe, Africa and the Americas. Its largest port, Sines, is a deep-water hub for container ships and energy.
Under the sea run fibre-optic cables linking Europe with Africa and the Americas, several of them landing on the Portuguese coast.
---
Sines was a small fishing town until the 1970s, when the state built a large port and industrial complex there. Its great advantage is deep water close to shore, so even the largest ships can dock. A container terminal opened in 2004, and its gas terminal, receiving liquefied gas by ship, became more important to Europe after 2022.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Ship from Asia or America", "Unloads at Sines", "Boxes go on smaller ships", "Or by rail into Iberia"]}
### Transshipment
Many containers at Sines are only changing ships. Giant vessels drop boxes that smaller ships carry on to other ports in Europe, Africa and the Mediterranean. This makes Sines Portugal's busiest container port and a link in global trade routes.
### The cable coast
Almost all intercontinental internet traffic travels through undersea fibre-optic cables. Portugal's position makes it a natural landing point. The EllaLink cable, opened in 2021, links Sines directly with Fortaleza in Brazil, and other cables reach Africa and the United States. Large data centres are being built at Sines to use these links.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["EllaLink: Sines to Brazil", "Cables to Africa and the US", "Data centres at Sines", "Landing points near Lisbon"]}
!? Sines is the birthplace of Vasco da Gama, whose statue stands by the castle overlooking the port.
!! Ships are old-fashioned in the internet age => About 80% of world trade by volume still travels by sea, and the internet itself runs mostly on undersea cables
> Portugal's coast is a crossroads for both ships and data, linking Europe to the Atlantic world.

# Wind and waves offshore
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Fixed turbines", "items": ["legs in the seabed", "water up to ~60 m", "North Sea style"]}, "r": {"t": "Floating turbines", "items": ["anchored float", "deep water", "suits Portugal"]}}
Portugal's continental shelf is narrow, so the seabed drops steeply away from the coast. That rules out ordinary fixed offshore wind turbines in most places.
Instead, Portugal has pioneered floating wind: the WindFloat Atlantic farm off Viana do Castelo, opened in 2020, was the first of its kind in continental Europe.
---
WindFloat Atlantic has three turbines of about 8.4 MW each, 25 MW in total, about 20 km off the coast where the sea is around 100 m deep. Each stands on a triangular floating platform held in place by chains and anchors. The platforms were assembled in port and towed out to sea, avoiding costly work offshore.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "3", "l": "floating turbines"}, {"v": "25 MW", "l": "total capacity"}, {"v": "~20 km", "l": "offshore from Viana"}]}
### Why floating
Fixed turbines on foundations work in water up to about 50–60 m deep, as across much of the North Sea. Off Portugal, such depths are often only a few kilometres from shore. Floating platforms can go into deep water, where winds are strong and steady.
### Plans and doubts
The government has mapped areas for offshore wind off Viana do Castelo, Leixões, Figueira da Foz, Ericeira and Sines, and plans auctions for several gigawatts. Fishermen worry about losing fishing grounds, and floating wind still costs more than wind on land, so the timetable remains uncertain.

### Wave power
The Aguçadoura wave farm near Póvoa de Varzim was the world's first multi-machine wave farm, in 2008, but it ran only briefly because of technical and financial problems. Waves remain a promising but hard-to-harvest resource.

# Sea level and the coast
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Rising sea level", "Too little sand from rivers", "Storms erode beaches", "Homes and roads at risk"]}
Long stretches of Portugal's coast are retreating as beaches and dunes lose sand. Dams hold back sediment that rivers once delivered, and harbour walls block the sand's natural drift.
Sea level is rising too, so storms reach further inland. Towns like Espinho and Costa da Caparica spend heavily defending their beaches.
---
Sand on the west coast moves mostly from north to south, pushed by waves that usually arrive from the north-west. This longshore drift is fed by rivers, especially the Douro. Dams built in the 20th century trapped much of that sediment, and long jetties at ports such as Leixões and Aveiro block the flow further. Beaches to the south of them are starved of sand.

### Sea level rising
Cascais has one of the oldest tide gauges in the world, recording since 1882. It shows sea level rising by about 20 cm since then, and the rate has increased in recent decades. Global projections for 2100 range from roughly 30 cm to about 1 m above recent levels, depending mainly on future greenhouse gas emissions.

### Defend, adapt or retreat
• **Groynes and seawalls**: rock barriers protect places like Espinho, but often push erosion further south
• **Sand nourishment**: pumping sand from offshore or harbours onto beaches, as at Costa da Caparica
• **Managed retreat**: removing buildings from the most exposed spots, as on some barrier islands of the Ria Formosa
