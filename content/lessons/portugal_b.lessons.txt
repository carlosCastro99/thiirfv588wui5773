# From Latin to Portuguese
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Latin of Roman settlers","Spoken Latin of Gallaecia","Galician-Portuguese","Portuguese"]}
Portuguese is a Romance language: it grew out of the everyday Latin spoken in the Roman west of Iberia.
Over many centuries that Latin changed sound by sound until it became a new language. Most Portuguese words still go back to Latin, even when they look very different today.
---
The Romans landed in Iberia in 218 BC, during their war with Carthage, and needed about two centuries to conquer the west. The Lusitanians, led by Viriathus, resisted fiercely until he was murdered in 139 BC. Soldiers, traders and settlers brought not the polished Latin of Cicero but the everyday spoken Latin, often called Vulgar Latin.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"218 BC","l":"Romans land in Iberia"},{"y":"139 BC","l":"Viriathus killed"},{"y":"409","l":"Suebi enter Iberia"},{"y":"711","l":"Moors arrive"}]}
### Regular sound changes
Portuguese is easy to recognise by a few changes that happened again and again:
• An l between vowels disappeared: dolorem became dor, salire became sair
• An n between vowels vanished, often leaving a nasal vowel: luna became lua, manum became mão
• Pl-, cl- and fl- at the start of a word turned into ch-: plenum became cheio, flamma became chama
• The group -ct- became -it-: noctem became noite, lactem became leite
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"luna","b":"lua (moon)"},{"a":"clavem","b":"chave (key)"},{"a":"noctem","b":"noite (night)"},{"a":"dolorem","b":"dor (pain)"}]}
ex: Latin pluvia | pl- becomes ch- | Portuguese chuva (rain)
### Germanic traces
After Rome fell, the Germanic Suebi and then the Visigoths ruled the north-west. They soon adopted Latin but left a few dozen words, many of them military, such as guerra (war), guardar (to guard) and roubar (to rob), plus the colour branco (white).

!! Portuguese is a kind of "broken Spanish" => Portuguese and Spanish are sister languages that grew separately from spoken Latin
> Portuguese is Latin that kept changing, in regular steps, for two thousand years.

# Galician-Portuguese
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Galician","items":["Spain's north-west","co-official there"]},"r":{"t":"Portuguese","items":["Portugal's language","spread overseas"]}}
In the Middle Ages, people on both banks of the Minho spoke one language, now called Galician-Portuguese.
When Portugal became an independent kingdom and expanded south, its speech drifted away from that of Galicia, which came under Castile. Galician and Portuguese are still very close relatives.
---
The language took shape between the 9th and 12th centuries in the old Kingdom of Galicia, which reached south to the Douro. Formal documents were still written in Latin, but scribes let local words slip in, and by around 1200 whole texts appeared in the spoken language. Besides Afonso II's will, an early example is the Notícia de Torto, a list of grievances from the same years.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"~900s","l":"Spoken in Galicia"},{"y":"1143","l":"Portugal a kingdom"},{"y":"~1214","l":"First texts in it"},{"y":"1400s","l":"Two standards emerge"}]}
### Why they split
• Portugal's centre moved south to Coimbra and then Lisbon, where the language mixed with the Romance speech of the reconquered south
• Galicia was ruled from Castile, so Castilian became the language of power and writing there
• Portuguese gained its own court, chancery, university and, later, printing presses
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["One language, both banks","A border at the Minho","Lisbon becomes the centre","Two written standards"]}
### Close cousins today
Galician is co-official with Spanish in Galicia, a region of about 2.7 million people. A farmer from the Minho and one from Ourense can usually chat with little effort, and many Galicians watch Portuguese television. Spelling hides some of the closeness: Galician writes ñ and ll where Portuguese writes nh and lh.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"manhã / mañá","b":"morning"},{"a":"filho / fillo","b":"son"},{"a":"não / non","b":"no"}]}
!? Some Galicians, known as reintegrationists, argue that Galician should use Portuguese spelling, and a few associations already write it that way.
> Portuguese and Galician began as one language; borders and politics, not distance, pulled them apart.

# Words from Arabic
type: fact
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"aldeia","b":"ad-day'a: village"},{"a":"açúcar","b":"as-sukkar: sugar"},{"a":"Algarve","b":"al-gharb: the west"}]}
From 711, much of Iberia was ruled by Muslim states; in what is now southern Portugal, until 1249.
Arabic was the language of learning, farming and trade, and Portuguese absorbed several hundred of its words, from the kitchen to the building site, plus place names all over the south.
---
The Moors held Lisbon until 1147 and the Algarve until 1249. Christians, Muslims and Jews lived side by side, and many Christians under Muslim rule spoke a Romance dialect full of Arabic terms. As the Portuguese kingdom moved south, it took over farms, irrigation channels, markets and workshops, together with the words for them.
### The telltale al-
Arabic al means "the". Portuguese usually borrowed nouns with the article attached, which is why so many Arabic loans start with al- or a-.
@viz {"k":"list","items":["almofada: cushion","armazém: warehouse","alfaiate: tailor","azulejo: glazed tile","javali: wild boar"]}
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Arabic loanwords","items":["Farming: açude, alface","Trade: armazém, tarifa","Home: almofada, alcatifa","Places: Algarve, Alcântara"]}
### Names on the map
• Alcântara: al-qantara, "the bridge"
• Albufeira: al-buhayra, "the lagoon"
• Alcácer do Sal: al-qasr, "the castle"
• Fátima: an Arabic woman's name, borne by the Prophet's daughter
Most of these names cluster south of the Tagus, where Muslim rule lasted longest. In the Minho and Trás-os-Montes they are much rarer.
!? Oxalá, "let's hope so", probably comes from the Arabic phrase "law sha' Allah", "if God wills"; Spanish has the same word, ojalá.
!! Arabic left only a handful of words => Estimates run to several hundred everyday words, and many more counting place names
> Arabic gave Portuguese a whole layer of everyday words, many still recognisable by the al- at the start.

# The troubadours
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Cantigas de amigo: a girl's voice","Cantigas de amor: a man's lament","Escárnio e maldizer: satire"]}
In the 13th and 14th centuries, Galician-Portuguese was the fashionable language of lyric poetry across Christian Iberia.
Kings, nobles and wandering jograis composed songs of love, longing and mockery. About 1,700 of these cantigas survive, copied into a handful of medieval songbooks.
---
The fashion began with the troubadours of southern France, whose songs travelled along the pilgrim road to Santiago de Compostela. Poets in north-west Iberia adapted it, and Galician-Portuguese became so prestigious that Castilians wrote poetry in it too. Alfonso X of Castile composed over 400 songs to the Virgin Mary, the Cantigas de Santa Maria, in Galician-Portuguese, even though he wrote his laws in Castilian.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Troubadours of Provence","Pilgrim road to Santiago","Courts of Iberia","Cantigas written down"]}
### Three kinds of song
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Cantiga de amigo","b":"a girl longs for her love"},{"a":"Cantiga de amor","b":"a man pines for a lady"},{"a":"Escárnio, maldizer","b":"mocking and insult songs"}]}
The cantigas de amigo are the most original. Though written by men, they speak in a young woman's voice, often by the sea, at a spring or under a hazel tree, waiting for her "amigo". Their refrains and repeated lines suggest roots in older folk songs.
### A poet king
King Dinis, who reigned from 1279 to 1325, left about 137 cantigas, more than almost any poet of his time; Alfonso X was his grandfather.
• Martin Codax, a jogral from Vigo, left seven songs about the sea, six of them with their music, a rare survival
• The songs reach us through three main songbooks: the Cancioneiro da Ajuda in Lisbon and two later Italian copies, now in the Vatican Library and Portugal's National Library
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~1,700","l":"secular cantigas"},{"v":"~420","l":"Cantigas de Santa Maria"},{"v":"~137","l":"songs by King Dinis"}]}
!? The insult songs can be startlingly crude: poets mocked cowardly knights, stingy patrons and each other in terms that still make readers blush.
> For about 150 years, Galician-Portuguese was the language Iberian poets used to sing about love.

# Camões and the Lusiads
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"1572","l":"Os Lusíadas printed"},{"v":"10","l":"cantos"},{"v":"1,102","l":"stanzas of eight lines"}]}
Luís Vaz de Camões gave Portuguese its great epic. Os Lusíadas, printed in 1572, retells Vasco da Gama's voyage to India as the story of a whole people.
Its success helped fix Portuguese as a literary language, which is still often called "the language of Camões".
---
Camões was born around 1524, probably in Lisbon, and lived a restless life. He lost an eye fighting in North Africa, was jailed after a street brawl in Lisbon, and sailed for India in 1553. He spent about 17 years in the East, in Goa and possibly Macau, before returning home poor.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"~1524","l":"Camões born"},{"y":"1553","l":"Sails for India"},{"y":"1572","l":"Os Lusíadas printed"},{"y":"1580","l":"Dies in Lisbon"}]}
### The poem
The epic follows Gama's fleet from Lisbon to Calicut while the gods of Olympus argue over the sailors: Venus protects them and Bacchus plots against them. On the way, characters tell Portuguese history, from Viriathus to Aljubarrota. It opens with "As armas e os barões assinalados", "arms and the famous heroes", an echo of Virgil's Aeneid.
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Gods: Venus against Bacchus","Voyage: Lisbon to Calicut","History told on board","Old Man of Restelo's warning"]}
The poem is not pure praise. As the fleet leaves Belém, the Old Man of Restelo condemns the greed and vanity that drive the empire.
### Fixing the language
Portuguese grammar was being written down at the same time: Fernão de Oliveira published the first grammar in 1536 and João de Barros another in 1540. Camões's vocabulary and style then served as a model for writers for centuries.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"10 June","b":"date of his death, 1580"},{"a":"Prémio Camões","b":"top prize, since 1989"},{"a":"Instituto Camões","b":"teaches Portuguese abroad"}]}
!? According to tradition, Camões survived a shipwreck at the mouth of the Mekong by swimming ashore with his manuscript held above the water.
> Camões turned a sea voyage into the founding poem of the Portuguese language.

# Across the oceans
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Trading language of Asian ports","Creoles in Africa and Asia","Loanwords in many languages","Brazil: the biggest legacy"]}
From the 1400s, Portuguese sailors, traders and missionaries carried their language along the coasts of Africa, Asia and South America.
For about two centuries it served as a common trading language in many ports. It left behind new creole languages and a trail of borrowed words.
---
Portuguese spread in two different ways. In Brazil, and later in Angola and Mozambique, it became the language of settlers, the state, the Church and schools. In much of Asia it spread as a contact language: merchants, interpreters and local traders used a simplified Portuguese with each other, even when no Portuguese were present. Dutch and English traders arriving in the 1600s often had to learn it to do business.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Settlement","items":["Brazil, Angola","state, school, church"]},"r":{"t":"Contact","items":["Asian, African ports","trade, missions"]}}
### Creoles
Where people of many origins mixed, new languages formed, with mostly Portuguese words but a grammar of their own.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Kriolu","b":"Cape Verde"},{"a":"Kriol","b":"Guinea-Bissau"},{"a":"Forro","b":"São Tomé"},{"a":"Kristang","b":"Malacca, Malaysia"}]}
Papiamento, spoken in Aruba, Curaçao and Bonaire, also has a strong Portuguese element, though linguists debate how it began.
### Words that travelled
• Swahili meza (table), from mesa
• Hindi and Bengali chabi (key), from chave, and Konkani and Marathi pav (bread roll), from pão
• Japanese kappa (raincoat), from capa
• English marmalade, from marmelada, quince paste, and cobra, from cobra de capelo

!! Creoles are just broken Portuguese => They are full languages with their own grammar; Kriolu alone is the mother tongue of about a million people
> Portuguese travelled first as a tool of trade, and it left words and whole new languages wherever ships called.

# About 260 million speakers
type: fact
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~260 M","l":"speakers worldwide"},{"v":"~80%","l":"of them in Brazil"},{"v":"~4%","l":"in Portugal"}]}
Portuguese has about 260 million speakers, which puts it among the ten most spoken languages on Earth.
About eight in ten live in Brazil. Portugal itself has only around 4% of the world's speakers, and Africa's share is the one growing fastest.
---
Counting speakers is never exact. Censuses ask different questions, and in Africa many people speak Portuguese as a second or third language alongside local ones. The figure of about 260 million, used by the Instituto Camões among others, includes both native speakers and fluent second-language speakers.
### Where they live

### Growing in Africa
Angola and Mozambique have young, fast-growing populations, and Portuguese is spreading as the language of cities, schools and media. In Luanda many children now grow up with Portuguese as their first language. Demographers expect Africa's share of speakers to keep rising through this century.
### Where it counts
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"European Union","b":"official language"},{"a":"African Union","b":"official language"},{"a":"Mercosur","b":"official language"},{"a":"UNESCO","b":"5 May: Portuguese Day"}]}
Portuguese is also one of the most used languages online, usually ranked in the top ten for websites and social media, largely thanks to Brazil. It is not one of the six official languages of the United Nations, a status Portuguese-speaking countries have long asked for.
!? São Paulo state alone has about 45 million people, more than four times the population of Portugal.
> Portugal is the birthplace of Portuguese, but its future is being written mostly in Brazil and Africa.

# European and Brazilian
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"comboio / trem","b":"train"},{"a":"autocarro / ônibus","b":"bus"},{"a":"telemóvel / celular","b":"mobile phone"},{"a":"casa de banho/banheiro","b":"bathroom"}]}
European and Brazilian Portuguese are one language with two standards, a little like British and American English.
They differ in pronunciation, some grammar, vocabulary and the everyday word for "you". Speakers understand each other well, with the occasional laugh over false friends.
---
### Sounds
Brazilians pronounce unstressed vowels clearly, while the Portuguese swallow many of them. In much of Brazil, t and d before an i sound become "tch" and "dj", so dia sounds like "djia". In Portugal, and in Rio de Janeiro, an s at the end of a syllable sounds like "sh".
### Grammar
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Portugal","items":["estou a fazer","diz-me","tu (informal)"]},"r":{"t":"Brazil","items":["estou fazendo","me diz","você (informal)"]}}
• Ongoing actions: Portugal says "estou a ler", Brazil "estou lendo", for "I'm reading"
• Pronoun position: Portugal says "chamo-me", Brazil usually "me chamo"
• "You": in Portugal tu is for family and friends and você can sound distant; in most of Brazil você is the normal word, though tu survives in the south and parts of the north-east
### False friends
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"propina","b":"PT tuition fee, BR bribe"},{"a":"fato","b":"PT suit, BR fact"},{"a":"apelido","b":"PT surname, BR nickname"},{"a":"constipado","b":"PT has a cold, BR constipated"}]}
In writing, Angola, Mozambique and the other African countries generally follow the European standard, though each has its own accent, slang and local words.
!? Brazilian telenovelas, starting with Gabriela in 1977, made Brazilian accents and expressions familiar to a whole generation in Portugal.
> Two standards, one language: the differences are real, but smaller than they first sound.

# The 1990 spelling agreement
type: fact
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1990","l":"Agreement signed"},{"y":"2009","l":"Portugal starts using it"},{"y":"2015","l":"Compulsory in Portugal"},{"y":"2016","l":"Compulsory in Brazil"}]}
For most of the 20th century, Portugal and Brazil spelled many words differently.
The Orthographic Agreement, signed in Lisbon in 1990, set common rules for all Portuguese-speaking countries. It took decades to apply and is still debated, but schools and official texts in Portugal and Brazil now follow it.
---
Separate reforms in 1911 in Portugal, after the Republic, and in 1943 in Brazil put the two countries on different paths. A common agreement in 1945 failed because Brazil never applied it, and Portugal kept its own rules until the new agreement took effect.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1911","l":"Portugal's reform"},{"y":"1943","l":"Brazil's own rules"},{"y":"1945","l":"Common deal fails"},{"y":"1990","l":"New agreement"}]}
### What changed in Portugal
• Silent consonants dropped: acção became ação, óptimo became ótimo, director became diretor
• Months and seasons lost their capitals: Janeiro became janeiro
• K, W and Y officially joined the alphabet, making 26 letters
• Some hyphens went: hei-de became hei de
@viz {"k":"fix","items":[{"w":"acção, óptimo","r":"ação, ótimo"},{"w":"Janeiro, Inverno","r":"janeiro, inverno"}]}
### What changed in Brazil
Brazil dropped the diaeresis, so lingüiça became linguiça, and some accents, so idéia became ideia and vôo became voo. By commonly cited estimates, the agreement changed about 1.5% of words in Portugal and about 0.5% in Brazil.
ex: Consonant pronounced in Portugal, silent in Brazil | Portugal keeps facto, Brazil writes fato | Both spellings are correct
### Still unfinished
Angola has not ratified the agreement, and its use across Africa and in Timor-Leste is uneven. In Portugal, some writers, newspapers and citizens' groups still reject it, while supporters argue that a common spelling helps schools, dictionaries and international use.
!! The agreement made Portuguese and Brazilian spelling identical => Many differences remain, because spelling still follows each country's pronunciation
> The 1990 agreement brought the two spellings much closer, but not into one.

# Sounds that puzzle learners
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Nasal vowels: pão, mãe, bom","Swallowed vowels: telefone","S as 'sh': dois, mais","lh and nh: filho, vinho","Four sounds for x"]}
European Portuguese sounds softer and more compact than its spelling suggests; some foreigners even mistake it for a Slavic language.
Learners struggle with nasal vowels, unstressed vowels that almost vanish and a final s that sounds like "sh". The letter x alone has four different sounds.
---
### Nasal vowels
Portuguese has five nasal vowels, made by letting air flow through the nose as well as the mouth, plus nasal diphthongs such as ão, õe and ãe. They are marked by a tilde or by an m or n closing the syllable. The difference changes meaning, so a learner who drops the nasal can say something quite different.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"pau / pão","b":"stick / bread"},{"a":"mau / mão","b":"bad / hand"},{"a":"vi / vim","b":"I saw / I came"}]}
### Vanishing vowels
In European Portuguese, unstressed vowels shrink or disappear. An unstressed e often vanishes completely, so telefone sounds close to "t'lefon'". An unstressed o turns into a u sound, so porto ends in "u". Strings of consonants result, which is why the language can sound clipped to outsiders.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Written: telefone","Unstressed e weakens","Spoken: t'lefon'"]}
### Tricky letters
• lh and nh sound like Italian gli and gn, as in filho and vinho
• Initial r and rr are a throaty sound for most speakers, but some older people in the north and interior still roll them
• In the Minho and much of the north, v and b often merge, so vinho can sound like "binho"
• X can sound like "sh" (xadrez), "z" (exame), "ks" (táxi) or "s" (próximo)
!? In parts of the Minho and Trás-os-Montes, some speakers still say ch as "tch", the way all Portuguese did in the Middle Ages, so chave sounds like "tchave".
> European Portuguese is spoken far more compactly than it is written, and learning to hear the missing vowels is half the battle.

# The CPLP
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1996","l":"CPLP founded, 7 states"},{"y":"2002","l":"Timor-Leste joins"},{"y":"2014","l":"Eq. Guinea joins"},{"y":"2021","l":"Mobility agreement"}]}
The Community of Portuguese Language Countries, the CPLP, was founded in Lisbon in 1996. It now has nine members on four continents, home to about 300 million people.
It promotes the language, cooperation and easier movement between members, but it has no common market or army.
---
The CPLP was founded on 17 July 1996 by seven countries: Portugal, Brazil, Angola, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, and São Tomé and Príncipe. Its headquarters are in Lisbon, and its leaders meet at a summit every two years, with a rotating presidency.
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"CPLP: 9 members","items":["Europe: Portugal","Americas: Brazil","Africa: 6 countries","Asia: Timor-Leste"]}
### What it does
• Runs the International Institute of the Portuguese Language, based in Praia, Cape Verde
• Coordinates cooperation on health, education, justice and election observation
• Since a 2021 agreement signed in Luanda, eases visas and residence between members
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Lisbon","b":"headquarters"},{"a":"Praia","b":"language institute"},{"a":"Every 2 years","b":"summit of leaders"},{"a":"5 May","b":"Portuguese Language Day"}]}
### The newest member
Equatorial Guinea was a Spanish colony, but its islands of Bioko and Annobón were Portuguese until a 1778 treaty handed them to Spain, and Annobón still speaks a Portuguese-based creole. The country added Portuguese as an official language in 2010 and joined in 2014. One condition, abolishing the death penalty, was met with a 2022 law.
### Strengths and limits
Members differ hugely in size, wealth and political system, and decisions need consensus. Critics see the CPLP as more a forum than a powerful organisation; supporters point to its role in Timor-Leste's independence and in crises in Guinea-Bissau. Dozens of countries, from Japan to Uruguay, have joined as associate observers.
!? Portugal used the 2021 mobility agreement to create a special CPLP residence permit, and tens of thousands of people from member countries applied within months.
> The CPLP turns a shared language into a club of nine countries, loose but lasting.

# Brazil
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~213 M","l":"people"},{"v":"8.5 M km²","l":"area, ~92× Portugal"},{"v":"1822","l":"independence"}]}
Brazil is by far the largest Portuguese-speaking country, with about 213 million people.
A Portuguese colony from 1500, it became independent in 1822 under a Portuguese prince. Today the ties run both ways: Brazilians are now the largest foreign community in Portugal.
---
For three centuries Brazil's wealth came from sugar, then from gold and diamonds in Minas Gerais in the 1700s, and later from coffee. Much of it was built on the labour of enslaved Africans: around 4 to 5 million were taken to Brazil, more than to any other country in the Americas. Brazil abolished slavery only in 1888, the last country in the Americas to do so.
### The court in Rio
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1500","l":"Cabral lands"},{"y":"1808","l":"Royal court in Rio"},{"y":"1815","l":"Kingdom equal to PT"},{"y":"1822","l":"Independence"},{"y":"1889","l":"Republic"}]}
When Napoleon's army invaded Portugal in 1807, the royal family and much of the court sailed to Rio de Janeiro, which became the capital of the empire. In 1815 Brazil was raised to a kingdom equal to Portugal. After King João VI returned to Lisbon in 1821, his son Pedro stayed and declared independence on 7 September 1822, becoming Emperor Pedro I. He later returned to Portugal and, as Pedro IV, fought a civil war to win the throne for his daughter.
### Ties today
• Brazilians in Portugal number several hundred thousand, by far the largest foreign community
• Millions of Brazilians have Portuguese ancestry, and many hold Portuguese passports
• Brazilian music, from bossa nova to sertanejo, is part of everyday life in Portugal
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Portugal","items":["~10.7 million people","~92,000 km²"]},"r":{"t":"Brazil","items":["~213 million people","~8.5 million km²"]}}
!? Rio de Janeiro is often described as the only city outside Europe ever to serve as the capital of a European empire, from 1808 to 1821.
> Brazil began as Portugal's colony and grew into its giant sibling, with twenty times its population.

# Angola
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Capital","b":"Luanda"},{"a":"People","b":"about 37 million"},{"a":"Independence","b":"11 November 1975"},{"a":"Main exports","b":"oil and diamonds"}]}
Angola, on Africa's south-west coast, is the second-largest Portuguese-speaking country by population, with about 37 million people.
Rich in oil and diamonds, it has rebuilt since a long civil war ended in 2002. Portuguese is spreading fast and is now the first language of many young Angolans.
---
The navigator Diogo Cão reached the mouth of the Congo river in 1482, and Luanda was founded in 1576. For centuries the colony was above all a source of enslaved people shipped to Brazil. Large-scale Portuguese settlement came only in the 20th century, and by 1974 a few hundred thousand settlers lived there.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1482","l":"Diogo Cão at the Congo"},{"y":"1576","l":"Luanda founded"},{"y":"1975","l":"Independence"},{"y":"2002","l":"Civil war ends"}]}
### Languages
Angola has many national languages, among them Umbundu, Kimbundu and Kikongo. Since independence, Portuguese has spread as the language of schools, cities and national unity. In the 2014 census about 70% of people said they spoke it, and in Luanda it is the home language of most families.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"National languages","items":["Umbundu, Kimbundu","Kikongo, Chokwe"]},"r":{"t":"Portuguese","items":["official, schools","spreading in cities"]}}
### Oil and the economy
Oil makes up around nine-tenths of Angola's exports, so the economy rises and falls with world oil prices; diamonds come second. Angola left OPEC, the oil exporters' club, in 2024. Portuguese banks, builders and retailers are active there, and Angolan investors own stakes in Portuguese companies.
### Culture
Angolan music crossed to Lisbon: kizomba and the fast electronic kuduro fill dance floors there, and slang such as "bué", meaning "loads", entered everyday Portuguese.
!? During Portugal's crisis in the early 2010s, Angola became a leading destination for Portuguese emigrants, reversing the flow of 1975.
> Angola is a young, oil-rich country where Portuguese is turning from an official language into a mother tongue.

# Mozambique
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Capital: Maputo","About 34 million people","Independent since 25 June 1975","Indian Ocean coast, ~2,500 km"]}
Mozambique stretches along Africa's south-east coast on the Indian Ocean, with about 34 million people.
Portuguese is the official language and a bridge between dozens of local languages, though only a minority speak it at home. It was also the first member of the Commonwealth that had never been a British colony.
---

# The 1976 Constitution
type: fact
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1976","l":"Constitution approved"},{"y":"1982","l":"First revision"},{"y":"1989","l":"Privatisation allowed"},{"y":"2005","l":"Seventh revision"}]}
Portugal's Constitution was approved on 2 April 1976 by an assembly elected a year after the revolution.
It defines Portugal as a democratic republic based on human dignity, the rule of law and the separation of powers. It has been revised seven times and remains the supreme law.
---
The Constituent Assembly elected on 25 April 1975 spent almost a year drafting the text amid the turmoil of the revolution. The result is long and detailed, with about 300 articles, and gives a large place to social rights such as health, education, housing and work.
### What it contains
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Fundamental principles","Rights and duties","Economic organisation","Organisation of political power","Guarantees and revision"]}
• Freedoms of expression, assembly, religion and the right to strike
• A ban on the death penalty and on life imprisonment
• A national health service that is universal and tends to be free, and free compulsory schooling
• Principles that no revision may remove, such as the republican form of government, universal suffrage and the separation of Church and State
### How it changes
The Assembly of the Republic may revise the Constitution five years after the last revision, or at any time if four-fifths of MPs agree to open the process. Each change needs a two-thirds majority, and the President cannot veto a revision.
ex: 230 MPs | two-thirds needed | at least 154 votes to change the text
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"1982","b":"civilian control of army"},{"a":"1989","b":"privatisations allowed"},{"a":"1992","b":"Maastricht treaty rules"},{"a":"1997","b":"emigrants vote for President"}]}
!! The death penalty was abolished in 1976 => Portugal abolished it for civil crimes in 1867, among the first countries; the Constitution confirmed the ban
> The 1976 Constitution is the rulebook that every law, government and court in Portugal must obey.

# The President
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Elected directly, 5-year term","At most two terms in a row","Appoints the Prime Minister","Veto laws, dissolve parliament","Commander of the armed forces"]}
The President of the Republic is head of state, elected directly by the people for five years and limited to two consecutive terms.
The role is mostly that of a referee. The President does not run the government but appoints the Prime Minister, can veto laws and can dissolve parliament.
---
Portugal's system is often called **semi-presidential**: power is shared between a directly elected President and a Government that answers to parliament. In practice the Prime Minister leads day-to-day policy, while the President's power lies in a few strong but rarely used tools and in public influence.
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"The President's powers","items":["Appoints PM, hears parties","Vetoes laws, asks the court","Dissolves the Assembly","Commands the armed forces"]}
### The veto
When the Assembly sends a law, the President has 20 days to sign it or veto it, or 8 days to ask the Constitutional Court to check it. The Assembly can override a political veto by confirming the law with an absolute majority of MPs, or two-thirds for some kinds of law. A veto of a government decree is final.
ex: 230 MPs | absolute majority = more than half | 116 votes override a veto
### Dissolution
The President can dissolve the Assembly and call early elections, but not in the six months after it was elected, nor in the last six months of the presidential term, nor during a state of emergency. Because it is so drastic, it is nicknamed the "atomic bomb"; it was used three times between 2021 and 2025.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"President","items":["head of state","referee, arbiter","Belém Palace"]},"r":{"t":"Prime Minister","items":["head of government","runs policy","São Bento"]}}
### Elections
Candidates must be Portuguese citizens by origin, aged at least 35, and nominated by 7,500 to 15,000 voters, not by parties. To win in the first round a candidate needs more than half of the valid votes; otherwise the top two meet in a run-off three weeks later.
!? Every President elected since 1976 has gone on to win a second term.
> The President rarely governs but holds the keys: who becomes Prime Minister and when the people vote again.

# The Assembly of the Republic
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"230","l":"MPs (deputados)"},{"v":"22","l":"constituencies"},{"v":"4 years","l":"normal term"}]}
The Assembly of the Republic is Portugal's parliament, a single chamber of 230 members elected for four years.
It makes laws, approves the budget, can bring down the government and elects key officials. It meets in the São Bento Palace in Lisbon, a former Benedictine monastery.
---
### What MPs do
• Pass laws on almost every subject, including all laws on rights, elections and crimes
• Approve the State Budget each year, usually in the autumn
• Question ministers and the Prime Minister in regular debates and committees
• Elect, by two-thirds, 10 of the 13 Constitutional Court judges and the Ombudsman
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Laws","b":"voted, then sent to President"},{"a":"Budget","b":"voted every autumn"},{"a":"Censure","b":"116 votes topple government"},{"a":"Committees","b":"examine bills in detail"}]}
### Where MPs come from
The country is divided into 22 constituencies: the 18 mainland districts, the Azores, Madeira, and two for Portuguese living abroad, one for Europe and one for the rest of the world, each electing two MPs. The other seats are shared according to the number of registered voters, so Lisbon elects 48 MPs and Portalegre just 2.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"MPs elected (recent elections)","items":[{"l":"Lisbon","v":48,"hi":true},{"l":"Porto","v":40},{"l":"Braga","v":19},{"l":"Portalegre","v":2}]}
### Who can sit
Any voter can stand, but only on the list of a party or coalition; independents can appear on those lists. Since 2019, lists must include at least 40% of each sex. The Assembly is led by its own President, the second-highest office of the state after the President of the Republic.
!? The São Bento Palace was the Monastery of São Bento da Saúde until religious orders were abolished in 1834; parliament has met there ever since.
> The Assembly is where the people's vote turns into laws, budgets and the rise and fall of governments.

# The d'Hondt method
type: example
@viz {"k":"terms","f":"votes ÷ (seats + 1)","items":[{"s":"votes","m":"a list's total votes"},{"s":"seats","m":"seats it has won so far"}]}
Seats are shared out by proportional representation: parties win seats roughly in line with their votes.
Portugal uses the d'Hondt method, devised by the Belgian lawyer Victor d'Hondt in 1878. In each constituency, voters choose a party list, not individual candidates.
example: 5 seats; A 10,000 votes, B 8,000, C 3,000 | Largest quotients: A 10,000, B 8,000, A 5,000, B 4,000, A 3,333 | A wins 3 seats, B 2, C none
---
### How it works
Each list's votes are divided by 1, 2, 3 and so on. The seats go, one at a time, to the largest numbers in the whole table until all are filled. In effect, each new seat goes to the list that would have the most votes per seat after winning it.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Count votes per list","Divide by 1, 2, 3…","Rank all the quotients","Largest ones win seats"]}
### Big and small constituencies
There is no legal minimum share of the vote, but constituency size matters a lot. In Lisbon, with 48 seats, a party can win a seat with around 2% of the vote. In a district with only 2 or 3 seats it may need 20% or more. That is why smaller parties win most of their MPs in Lisbon, Porto and Setúbal.
= Rough threshold for one seat ≈ 100% ÷ (seats + 1)
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"% for one seat (illustrative)","items":[{"l":"Lisbon, 48","v":2,"hi":true},{"l":"Braga, 19","v":5},{"l":"Viseu, 8","v":11},{"l":"Portalegre, 2","v":33}]}
ex: Portalegre, 2 seats | A 40%, B 35%, C 15% | Seat 1 to A; then B's 35 beats A's 20 | A 1, B 1, C 0
### Strengths and criticisms
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Strengths","items":["seats follow votes","many voices heard","simple to count"]},"r":{"t":"Criticisms","items":["voters can't pick names","favours larger parties","small districts skewed"]}}
Proposals for reform, such as a national top-up constituency or letting voters choose candidates within lists, come up regularly but have not been adopted.
!? Portugal also uses d'Hondt for town councils, parish assemblies and its 21 seats in the European Parliament.
> d'Hondt shares seats in proportion to votes, but its rounding favours larger parties, most of all where seats are few.

# Government and Prime Minister
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["General election","President hears parties","PM appointed","Programme faces parliament"]}
The Government runs the country day to day: it proposes the budget, directs the civil service and can issue decree-laws.
It is led by the Prime Minister, appointed by the President in light of the election results. A government survives only as long as parliament does not reject or censure it.
---
After an election, the President meets every party that won seats and then appoints as Prime Minister the person most likely to have parliament's support, usually the leader of the largest party or coalition. The Prime Minister proposes the ministers, whom the President then appoints.
### Facing parliament
Within ten days, the new Government presents its programme to the Assembly. It does not need a vote of approval: it falls only if an absolute majority of MPs, 116, votes a motion of rejection. This rule lets minority governments take office, which has happened often since 1976.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Motion of rejection","b":"blocks a new programme"},{"a":"Motion of censure","b":"116 votes bring it down"},{"a":"Vote of confidence","b":"government asks; can lose"},{"a":"Budget defeat","b":"can lead to elections"}]}
### How a government ends
• A general election and a new Assembly
• The Prime Minister's resignation or death
• A censure motion passed by an absolute majority, or a lost confidence vote
• Dismissal by the President, allowed only to protect the regular functioning of democratic institutions
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Majority","items":["one side has 116+","rarely loses votes"]},"r":{"t":"Minority","items":["needs other parties","deals vote by vote"]}}
### How it works inside
The Council of Ministers, all the ministers chaired by the Prime Minister, meets weekly to approve decree-laws and bills to send to parliament. The Prime Minister's official residence is a mansion behind the parliament building in São Bento.
!? Single-party absolute majorities are rare: they happened only in the elections of 1987, 1991, 2005 and 2022.
> In Portugal a government does not need parliament's approval to start, only to avoid a majority voting against it.

# The Constitutional Court
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"13","l":"judges"},{"v":"9 years","l":"single term each"},{"v":"1983","l":"first sitting"}]}
The Constitutional Court decides whether laws respect the Constitution and can strike them down. Created by the 1982 revision, it has 13 judges serving a single nine-year term.
Alongside it, independent courts, prosecutors, an Ombudsman and a Court of Auditors keep watch on the other powers.
---
### How judges are chosen
Ten judges are elected by the Assembly with a two-thirds majority, which in practice requires agreement between the largest parties. These ten then co-opt the remaining three. At least six must be career judges from other courts; the rest are legal experts, often law professors.
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Constitutional Court","items":["10 elected by parliament","3 chosen by those 10","6+ career judges","9 years, no renewal"]}
### How a law reaches the Court
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Before signing: President asks","After: MPs, PM, Ombudsman ask","Any court case, on appeal","Gaps: a law that is missing"]}
If the Court finds a law unconstitutional before it is signed, the President must veto it. Once a rule is struck down with general binding force, it stops applying everywhere. Any ordinary court must also refuse to apply a rule it considers unconstitutional, and the case can then go to the Constitutional Court on appeal.
Between 2012 and 2014, for example, the Court struck down several budget measures, including cuts to public-sector holiday and Christmas pay, ruling that they treated groups unequally.
### Other guardians
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Supreme Court","b":"top civil, criminal court"},{"a":"Supreme Admin. Court","b":"cases against the state"},{"a":"Court of Auditors","b":"checks public spending"},{"a":"Ombudsman","b":"citizens' complaints"}]}
Prosecutions are led by the Public Prosecution Service, which is independent of the Government and headed by the Attorney General, appointed by the President on the Government's proposal for six years.
!! The Constitutional Court hears every kind of appeal => It decides only constitutional questions; ordinary cases end at the Supreme Court
> In Portugal no law is safe until it fits the Constitution, and 13 judges have the last word on that.

# Azores and Madeira
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Azores","items":["9 islands","57-seat assembly","Ponta Delgada, Horta"]},"r":{"t":"Madeira","items":["2 inhabited islands","47-seat assembly","Funchal"]}}
The Azores and Madeira are autonomous regions, a status the 1976 Constitution created because of their distance from Lisbon and their island identity.
Each has its own elected assembly and regional government, which make laws on many local matters. Defence, foreign policy and justice stay with the national state.
---
Autonomy answered old grievances: islanders had long felt neglected by Lisbon, and in 1975 small independence movements briefly appeared in both archipelagos. The Constitution gave them self-government within the Portuguese state, and their autonomy is among the principles no revision may remove.
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Constitution and national laws","Representative of the Republic","Regional legislative assembly","Regional government"]}
### What they control
• Their own laws on matters such as health, education, transport, tourism, fisheries and farming
• Most taxes raised in the region, with the power to lower some rates within limits
• Their own flags, anthems and regional holidays
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"standard VAT rate, % (2025)","items":[{"l":"Mainland","v":23},{"l":"Madeira","v":22},{"l":"Azores","v":16,"hi":true}]}
### The Representative of the Republic
The President appoints a Representative of the Republic for each region, who signs or vetoes regional laws and can send them to the Constitutional Court. The Representative also appoints the President of the Regional Government in light of the regional election results, much as the President of the Republic does with the Prime Minister.
### Why not the mainland?
The Constitution also foresees administrative regions on the mainland, but in a 1998 referendum about 64% voted against creating eight of them. Turnout was under 50%, so the result was not binding, but no regions have been created since. Mainland planning is coordinated by five regional development commissions, the CCDRs.
!? The Azores spread their institutions across islands: the regional parliament sits in Horta, on Faial, while the regional government is based in Ponta Delgada, on São Miguel.
> Autonomy lets the islands run most of their own affairs while remaining fully part of Portugal.

# Municipalities and parishes
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["The state","308 municipalities","Over 3,000 parishes"]}
Local government has two levels: 308 municipalities, the concelhos, and below them more than 3,000 parishes, the freguesias.
Each has elected bodies that run local services such as roads, schools, water, waste and culture. Mayors and parish presidents are often the politicians people know best.
---
### The municipality
Each municipality has two bodies, both elected every four years. The Câmara Municipal is the executive; it is led by its president, the mayor, who is the first name on the most-voted list. The Assembleia Municipal debates and approves the budget and holds the Câmara to account; it also includes the presidents of all the parish councils.

### The parish
Parishes are the level closest to citizens. They look after local paths and green spaces, issue some certificates, run markets and support schools and older people. In 2013 their number was cut from about 4,260 to about 3,090 by merging many rural parishes, and some of those mergers were reversed before the 2025 local elections.

### Rules and money
• Mayors may serve at most three consecutive terms in the same municipality
• Groups of citizens can run independent lists in local elections, unlike in national ones
• Councils are funded by local taxes such as the property tax (IMI), a share of state taxes and EU funds
• Since 2019 many tasks in education, health and social support have been transferred from the state to the municipalities
!? Corvo, in the Azores, is Portugal's smallest municipality and the only one with no parish at all.
> Local power in Portugal runs from big-city mayors to parish councils that know every street by name.

# Elections and voting
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Legislative: every 4 years","Presidential: every 5 years","Local: every 4 years","European: every 5 years","Regional: Azores, Madeira"]}
Citizens aged 18 and over vote in national, presidential, local, regional and European elections, and in occasional referendums.
Elections are held on Sundays, overseen by an independent commission, and votes are counted in public at each polling station.
---
### Election day
Voting takes place on a Sunday, usually in schools. The day before is a "day of reflection": campaigning stops, and opinion polls cannot be published until voting ends. Ballot papers list the parties with their names and symbols, and voters mark a cross.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["About 2 weeks of campaign","Day of reflection","Sunday vote, 8:00–19:00","Public count, results"]}
### Who runs elections
The National Elections Commission, the CNE, watches over fairness and equal treatment of candidates, while the Ministry of Internal Administration handles the logistics. Citizens are registered automatically when they get their Citizen Card. Anyone can also ask to vote early, a week before, at a polling place of their choice.
### Referendums
A national referendum must be proposed by the Assembly or the Government, checked by the Constitutional Court and called by the President. It is binding only if more than half of registered voters take part, and none of the three held so far reached that level.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"June 1998","b":"abortion: No, low turnout"},{"a":"November 1998","b":"regions: No, low turnout"},{"a":"February 2007","b":"abortion: Yes, low turnout"}]}
After the 2007 vote, parliament chose to legislate in line with the majority anyway.
!! Voting is compulsory in Portugal => The Constitution calls voting a civic duty, but there is no fine for not voting
> Portugal's elections are frequent, free and simple: a cross on a Sunday, counted in public that same night.

# Portugal in the EU
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"European Parliament","b":"21 Portuguese MEPs"},{"a":"Council of the EU","b":"ministers vote for Portugal"},{"a":"Commission","b":"one Portuguese commissioner"},{"a":"EU law","b":"applies directly in Portugal"}]}
Since 1986 Portugal has been a member of the European Union, sharing some decisions with 26 other countries.
It elects 21 members of the European Parliament, sends ministers to the Council and nominates one commissioner. Where the treaties give the EU power, EU law takes priority over national law.
---
Membership means that some decisions are taken jointly in Brussels, Strasbourg and Luxembourg rather than in Lisbon alone. Trade, competition rules, farming and fisheries policy, and for euro countries interest rates, are largely set at EU level. Health, schools, pensions and most taxes remain mainly national.

### How Portugal takes part
Ministers vote in the Council of the EU, where many decisions use a qualified majority. The Assembly's European Affairs Committee scrutinises EU proposals, and Portuguese judges and officials work in all the institutions.

### Portuguese at the top
José Manuel Durão Barroso was President of the European Commission from 2004 to 2014, and António Costa has chaired the European Council since December 2024.
### Language and courts
Portuguese is one of the EU's 24 official languages, so every EU law is published in it and citizens can write to the institutions in Portuguese. Portuguese courts can ask the EU Court of Justice in Luxembourg how to interpret EU law, and its answer binds them.

# A small open economy
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~€285 bn","l":"GDP in 2024"},{"v":"~10.7 M","l":"people"},{"v":"~3/4","l":"of output from services"}]}
Portugal's economy produces about €285 billion of goods and services a year, under 2% of the EU total. Services such as tourism, trade and finance dominate.
Exports now equal close to half of GDP, far more than in the past, so the country depends heavily on selling abroad.
---
### What the economy is made of
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"share of value added, % (approx.)","items":[{"l":"Services","v":76,"hi":true},{"l":"Industry, energy","v":17},{"l":"Construction","v":5},{"l":"Farming, fishing","v":2}]}
Services include shops, tourism, transport, property, finance and public services. Industry is smaller than in Germany or Czechia but strong in a few clusters: cars and parts, paper and pulp, cork, textiles and shoes, food and wine, and oil refining at Sines.
### Wealth per person
GDP per person is roughly €27,000 a year, about 80% of the EU average once differences in prices are taken into account. That places Portugal in the lower half of the EU, and in recent years several Central European members, such as Czechia, Slovenia and Lithuania, have moved ahead of it.
ex: GDP ~€285 billion | ÷ ~10.7 million people | ≈ €27,000 per person a year
@viz {"k":"scale","lo":50,"hi":130,"unit":"EU average = 100","items":[{"l":"Bulgaria","v":66},{"l":"Portugal","v":82,"hi":true},{"l":"Spain","v":91},{"l":"Germany","v":116}]}
### Open to the world
Exports of goods and services were about 30% of GDP around 2010 and close to half by the 2020s, one of the biggest changes in the economy's history. Spain is by far the largest customer, taking around a quarter of goods exports, followed by France, Germany and the United States.
!! Portugal mainly lives off farming and fishing => Farming and fishing make up only about 2% of output; services and industry produce almost everything
> Portugal is a small, service-heavy economy that lives more and more by selling to the rest of the world.

# From EU entry to the euro
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1986","l":"Joins the EEC"},{"y":"1986–2000","l":"Fast catch-up"},{"y":"2001–2010","l":"Near stagnation"},{"y":"2011","l":"Bailout"}]}
Joining the European Community in 1986 opened markets and brought large EU funds, and Portugal grew fast for about 15 years.
After the euro arrived in 1999, growth slowed sharply. The 2000s became a "lost decade" in which the economy barely grew while debts piled up.
---
### The catch-up years
Between 1986 and 2000, GDP per person rose from just over half to roughly 70% of the then EU average, measured in purchasing power. Foreign investment arrived, such as the Autoeuropa car plant at Palmela, and EU funds paid for infrastructure and training. As markets prepared for the euro, interest rates fell sharply, making borrowing cheap for families, firms and the state.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"average yearly GDP growth, %","items":[{"l":"1986–2000","v":3.8,"t":"~4","hi":true},{"l":"2001–2010","v":0.7,"t":"<1"},{"l":"2015–2019","v":2.6,"t":"~2.5"}]}
### Why growth stalled
• China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and Central European countries joined the EU in 2004, competing in the low-cost textiles, shoes and assembly work Portugal relied on
• Inside the euro, Portugal could no longer devalue its currency to make its goods cheaper abroad
• Cheap credit flowed into housing, roads and consumption more than into productive firms
• Wages rose faster than productivity, so firms lost competitiveness
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Cheap euro credit","Borrowing and spending","Competitiveness falls","Debts pile up"]}
### EU money
Over four decades Portugal has received well over €100 billion in EU funds, counting farm support. Since 2021 the Recovery and Resilience Plan, known as the PRR, adds about €22 billion in grants and loans for health, housing, digital and green projects.
!! The euro caused the crisis on its own => Economists point to a mix: cheap credit, weak productivity, new global competition and the 2008 financial crash
> EU entry lifted Portugal fast, but the euro's first decade showed that cheap money alone does not make an economy productive.

# Bailout and recovery
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"public debt, % of GDP (approx.)","items":[{"l":"2007","v":72},{"l":"2014","v":133},{"l":"2020","v":135,"hi":true},{"l":"2024","v":95}]}
In 2011, unable to borrow on the markets at affordable rates, Portugal asked the EU and the IMF for a rescue loan. Three years of spending cuts, tax rises and reforms followed.
The economy has since recovered: unemployment fell, the state ran budget surpluses in several years and public debt dropped below 100% of GDP.
---
### Why it happened
By early 2011 the interest rate on Portugal's ten-year bonds had climbed above 7%, after Greece and Ireland had already needed rescues. The minority government resigned in March 2011 when parliament rejected its new austerity package, and in April it requested help.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"European Commission","b":"EU rescue funds"},{"a":"European Central Bank","b":"monitoring with the others"},{"a":"IMF","b":"about a third of the loan"}]}
### What the programme involved
• Cuts to public wages and pensions, and higher taxes
• Privatisation of the electricity company EDP, the grid operator REN, the airports company ANA and the postal service CTT
• Labour market changes, such as lower severance pay
• Four public holidays suspended from 2013 to 2015, then restored in 2016
### The recovery
Exports, tourism and foreign investment led the rebound. In 2019 the state recorded its first budget surplus in the democratic era. After the dip of the Covid pandemic, surpluses returned in 2023 and 2024, and public debt fell from about 135% of GDP in 2020 to about 95% in 2024.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"unemployment rate, %","items":[{"l":"2013","v":16.2,"t":"~16","hi":true},{"l":"2019","v":6.5,"t":"~6.5"},{"l":"2024","v":6.4,"t":"~6.4"}]}
ex: Debt ~€270 billion | GDP ~€285 billion | 270 ÷ 285 ≈ 95% of GDP
!? Portugal repaid its IMF loans early, finishing in 2018, years ahead of schedule.
> The bailout years were painful, but within a decade Portugal went from rescue case to one of the eurozone's fastest debt reducers.

# Tourism
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~31 M","l":"guests in lodgings, 2024"},{"v":"~€27 bn","l":"spent by foreign visitors"},{"v":"~1/5","l":"of all exports"}]}
Tourism is one of Portugal's biggest industries. Visitors come for beaches, cities, food, golf and history, and in 2024 tourist lodgings recorded about 31 million guests.
Spending by foreign visitors, about €27 billion a year, makes tourism the country's largest single export.
---
### Who comes
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Portuguese residents: ~3 in 10 nights","United Kingdom","Spain, Germany, France","United States, Brazil"]}
The United Kingdom has long been the biggest foreign market, especially for the Algarve and Madeira. Spain, Germany and France follow, and American visitors have grown fastest in recent years, helped by new direct flights.
### Where they go
Lisbon, the Algarve and Madeira dominate, while Porto and the North have grown fast since low-cost flights expanded in the 2000s and 2010s. Lisbon's Humberto Delgado airport handled over 35 million passengers in 2024 and is close to capacity; in 2024 the government chose Alcochete, across the Tagus, for a new airport.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Algarve","b":"beaches, golf, summer peak"},{"a":"Lisbon","b":"city breaks, cruises"},{"a":"Madeira","b":"all year, nature walks"},{"a":"Porto and North","b":"wine, food, the Douro"}]}
### Benefits and strains
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Benefits","items":["jobs and exports","restored old centres","more flights"]},"r":{"t":"Strains","items":["housing pressure","crowded centres","seasonal, low pay"]}}
Short-term rentals, known as alojamento local, spread fast in Lisbon and Porto from the 2010s. They helped restore run-down buildings but added to pressure on rents, and rules on new licences have been changed several times. Lisbon charges a tourist tax of €4 per person per night.
!! Tourism is all about foreigners => Portuguese residents account for roughly three in ten nights spent in tourist lodgings
> Tourism has become a pillar of Portugal's exports and jobs, and managing its side effects is now a central policy question.

# What Portugal sells
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Cars and car parts","Machinery and electronics","Fuels from the Sines refinery","Textiles, clothing, shoes","Paper, cork, food, wine"]}
Portugal's goods exports are more varied than many people think. Cars and components lead, followed by machinery, refined fuels, plastics, paper, textiles and shoes.
Most go to other EU countries, above all Spain, as Portuguese firms plug into European supply chains.
---
### Cars
The Volkswagen Autoeuropa plant at Palmela, opened in 1995, is one of the country's largest exporters; it builds the T-Roc, almost all for export. Stellantis builds vans in Mangualde, and hundreds of suppliers make seats, tyres, wiring and glass for car plants across Europe.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"1995","l":"Autoeuropa opens"},{"v":"~200,000","l":"cars a year at Palmela"},{"v":"~99%","l":"of them exported"}]}
### Textiles and shoes
The north, from Guimarães and Barcelos to Felgueiras and São João da Madeira, has long been the heart of textiles, clothing and footwear. Hit hard by Asian competition in the 2000s, many firms survived by moving upmarket: technical fabrics, short production runs, fast delivery to European brands and their own shoe labels. Textile and clothing exports reach around €6 billion a year, and shoes about €2 billion.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"2000s","items":["cheap mass orders","lost to Asia"]},"r":{"t":"Today","items":["design, quality","fast, small batches"]}}
### Other strengths
• Paper and pulp from eucalyptus plantations
• Refined fuels from Galp's refinery at Sines, the only one left since Matosinhos closed in 2021
• Moulds and plastics from Marinha Grande and Leiria
• Food such as tomato paste, olive oil and tinned fish
!? Portugal has become the EU's largest bicycle maker, with many factories around Águeda.
> Behind the image of sun and cork, Portugal is an industrial exporter wired into European supply chains.

# Cork: a world leader
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Bark stripped by hand","Boiled and flattened","Stoppers punched out","Scraps ground to granules"]}
Portugal is the world's largest producer and exporter of cork, selling about €1.2 billion of it abroad each year.
Most becomes wine stoppers, but cork also goes into floors, insulation, shoes and even spacecraft. Nothing is wasted: offcuts are ground into granules and pressed into new products.
---
### An industry built on one tree
Portugal has roughly a third of the world's cork oak forests, mostly in the Alentejo and the Tagus valley, yet it processes even more cork than it grows, importing raw bark from Spain and North Africa. The industry is clustered around Santa Maria da Feira, south of Porto, where hundreds of firms turn bark into finished goods. Corticeira Amorim, founded in 1870, is the world's largest cork company.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"cork exports by value, % (approx.)","items":[{"l":"Stoppers","v":70,"hi":true},{"l":"Building materials","v":25},{"l":"Other uses","v":5}]}
### From bark to bottle
The planks rest outdoors for months, are boiled to clean and soften them, then dried. Natural stoppers are punched from the thickest planks. Thinner cork and offcuts are ground and glued into "technical" stoppers for sparkling and everyday wines; champagne corks are agglomerated cork topped with natural discs.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Natural stopper","items":["one piece of bark","fine wines, ageing"]},"r":{"t":"Technical stopper","items":["glued granules","everyday, sparkling"]}}
### Beyond the bottle
• Floor and wall tiles that are warm, quiet and springy underfoot
• Insulation boards for walls and roofs
• Shoe soles, sports balls, gaskets and fashion items
• Heat shields that protect rockets and spacecraft at launch
In the 2000s, screw caps and plastic stoppers took a big share of the market. The industry answered by tackling cork taint, the musty smell that spoils some wines, and by promoting cork as natural and recyclable.
!? Expanded cork insulation needs no added glue: heating the granules with steam releases cork's own resin, which binds them together.
> Portugal turned the bark of a tree into a high-tech export industry that uses every last scrap.

# Wine as an export
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"wine exports, € million (approx.)","items":[{"l":"2014","v":730},{"l":"2024","v":930,"hi":true}]}
Portugal makes around 7 million hectolitres of wine a year, among the world's top dozen producers, and exports close to €1 billion of it.
Port is still the best-known product, but table wines now earn more abroad in total. The industry's aim is to sell fewer cheap bulk wines and more bottles at higher prices.
---
### Who buys
@viz {"k":"list","items":["France: a big buyer of port","United States","United Kingdom","Brazil, Canada, Germany"]}
Portugal exports less wine by volume than Spain or Italy, but its average export price has been rising as more is sold bottled and under brand names. Shared language and emigrant communities help in markets such as Brazil, Angola and Canada.
### Port: a regulated brand
Port is still among the most valuable Portuguese wines sold abroad, though sales volumes have slowly fallen since the 2000s as tastes changed. Its supply is tightly managed: each year the Douro's regulator, the IVDP, sets how much grape must may be fortified into port, and each vineyard gets a share according to a grading of its site, from A downwards.
ex: Fermenting grape juice | add grape spirit (~77% alcohol) | sweet port of about 20%
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Bulk wine","items":["sold by the litre","low margins"]},"r":{"t":"Bottled, branded","items":["sold per bottle","higher value"]}}
### Growers and pressures
Most vineyards are small family plots, and many growers sell grapes to cooperative wineries or large firms. Costs are high on steep terraces where machines cannot work, and labour at harvest is scarce. In 2024 many Douro growers struggled to sell their grapes at a fair price as unsold wine piled up, and climate change is bringing hotter, drier summers and earlier harvests.
!? Wine tourism has become a business of its own: quintas in the Douro and Alentejo now earn a growing share of their income from visits, meals and rooms.
> Portuguese wine's economic challenge is to earn more from every bottle rather than to make more bottles.

# Renewable electricity
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"renewables, % electricity use","items":[{"l":"2010","v":50,"t":"~50"},{"l":"2020","v":58,"t":"~58"},{"l":"2024","v":71,"t":"~70","hi":true}]}
Portugal is one of Europe's leaders in clean electricity. In 2024 renewables, mainly hydro, wind and solar, supplied around 70% of the electricity the country used.
Its last coal power station closed in 2021, and governments have set targets to go higher still by 2030.
---
### Where it comes from
Dams on northern rivers, built from the 1950s on, long made hydropower the backbone of the system; in wet years it supplies over a quarter of electricity, in droughts much less. From the 2000s wind farms spread along the ridges of the interior, and since about 2019 solar power has boomed, especially in the Alentejo, after auctions set record-low prices.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Hydro","b":"northern dams; needs rain"},{"a":"Wind","b":"inland ridges, since 2000s"},{"a":"Solar","b":"Alentejo, fastest growing"},{"a":"Biomass","b":"forest and pulp waste"}]}
### Quitting coal
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"2016","l":"4 days fully renewable"},{"y":"2020","l":"Floating wind farm"},{"y":"Jan 2021","l":"Sines coal plant shuts"},{"y":"Nov 2021","l":"Pego, last coal, shuts"}]}
Portugal was the fourth EU country to stop burning coal for electricity, after Belgium, Austria and Sweden. Natural gas plants and imports from Spain now fill the gaps when rain, wind and sun fall short.
### Challenges
• Sun and wind vary, so the grid needs storage: pumped-storage dams pump water uphill when power is cheap and release it later, and large batteries are arriving
• Links with Spain, and through Spain to France, are limited; the Iberian blackout of 28 April 2025, which began in the Spanish grid, cut power across mainland Portugal for most of a day
• Large solar farms, wind turbines and power lines sometimes face local opposition over land and landscape
!? Off Viana do Castelo, WindFloat Atlantic was one of the world's first floating offshore wind farms: three large turbines on platforms anchored in water about 100 m deep.
> Rain, wind and sun now power most of Portugal's homes and factories, and the next challenge is keeping that supply steady.

# Start-ups and the Web Summit
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"2016","l":"Web Summit to Lisbon"},{"y":"2018","l":"Farfetch lists in NY"},{"y":"2018","l":"OutSystems a unicorn"},{"y":"2022","l":"Digital nomad visa"}]}
Since the mid-2010s, Lisbon and Porto have become popular bases for tech start-ups and remote workers.
The Web Summit, one of the world's largest tech conferences, has been held in Lisbon every November since 2016. A handful of companies with Portuguese founders have grown into "unicorns" valued at over $1 billion.
---
### The Web Summit
Founded in Dublin in 2009, the Web Summit moved to Lisbon in 2016, drawn by state support, the large venue at Parque das Nações and the city's appeal. A 2018 agreement with the government and the city keeps it in Lisbon until 2028 in exchange for about €11 million a year. It now draws around 70,000 people each November.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"~70,000","l":"attendees a year"},{"v":"2016","l":"first Lisbon edition"},{"v":"2028","l":"deal runs until"}]}
### Home-grown unicorns
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Farfetch","b":"online luxury fashion"},{"a":"OutSystems","b":"low-code software"},{"a":"Talkdesk","b":"call-centre software"},{"a":"Feedzai","b":"AI fraud detection"}]}
Most of these firms keep large engineering teams in Portugal even when their headquarters are in the United States or elsewhere. Their fortunes vary: Farfetch, founded by José Neves, ran into financial trouble and was bought by South Korea's Coupang in 2024.
### Why here?
• Engineers from strong universities such as Técnico in Lisbon, FEUP in Porto and the University of Minho, at lower salaries than in London or Berlin
• Good English, a time zone close to London's, quality of life and safety
• Visas and tax schemes for start-ups, tech workers and remote workers
• Large international firms also run tech centres in Lisbon, Porto and Braga
### Limits
Start-ups still find less local venture capital than in bigger hubs, and rising housing costs in Lisbon and Porto worry workers. Tech remains a small part of total employment compared with tourism or industry.
!? Many Portuguese start-ups are founded with the US market in mind from day one, registering their company there while building the product in Portugal.
> Lisbon's tech scene is real and growing, though still small next to tourism or industry.

# Wages and productivity
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"national minimum wage, € a month","items":[{"l":"2015","v":505},{"l":"2020","v":635},{"l":"2026","v":920,"hi":true}]}
Wages in Portugal are among the lower ones in Western Europe. The national minimum wage has risen quickly, to €920 a month in 2026, paid 14 times a year.
The deeper issue is productivity: each hour worked produces much less value than the EU average, which limits how far pay can rise.
---
### Fourteen payments
Most workers receive 14 payments a year: 12 normal months plus a holiday allowance, the subsídio de férias, usually paid before the summer, and a Christmas allowance, the subsídio de Natal. To compare with countries that pay 12 times a year, multiply by 14 and divide by 12.
ex: €920 × 14 payments | = €12,880 a year | ÷ 12 ≈ €1,073 a month
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Portugal","items":["14 payments a year","€920 minimum (2026)"]},"r":{"t":"12-month basis","items":["same yearly total","≈ €1,073 a month"]}}
### Productivity
Output per hour worked is roughly two-thirds of the EU average. According to the OECD, Portuguese workers put in more hours a year than Germans, about 1,650 against 1,350, yet produce less per hour.
@viz {"k":"bars","unit":"hours worked per worker a year","items":[{"l":"Portugal","v":1650,"t":"~1,650","hi":true},{"l":"Germany","v":1350,"t":"~1,350"}]}
### Why the gap?
• Very many small firms, which invest less in machines, technology and training
• A historic education gap: in 2000 over 40% of young people left school early, while today it is under 10%, but many older workers had little schooling
• Low investment per worker, especially after the 2011 crisis
• A large share of jobs in labour-intensive sectors such as tourism, retail and construction
### Pay and people
Because the minimum wage has risen faster than average pay, the gap between them has narrowed. Many young graduates move abroad for higher salaries, while Portugal itself attracts immigrants, who now make up more than a tenth of residents.
!! The Portuguese earn less because they work less => They work more hours than most Europeans; the gap is in output per hour
> In the long run pay follows productivity, so Portugal's wage question is above all about skills, investment and better-paid sectors.
