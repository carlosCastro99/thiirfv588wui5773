# The Atlantic diet
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Fish and seafood, often grilled","Vegetables, beans and potatoes","Bread, olive oil and wine","Slow stews shared at the table"]}
Portugal and Galicia share what nutritionists call the Atlantic diet: plenty of fish, vegetables, potatoes, bread and olive oil, with a little wine, eaten slowly with family.
It looks like the Mediterranean diet, but it leans more on the sea, on cabbage and potatoes, and on soups and stews cooked for hours.
---
The name was popularised in the 2000s by doctors and nutritionists in Galicia and northern Portugal, to describe how people along the Atlantic edge of Iberia traditionally ate. It is less a set of rules than a portrait of a farming and fishing kitchen.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Mediterranean","items":["olive oil at the centre","pasta and tomatoes","dry, sunny farming"]},"r":{"t":"Atlantic","items":["fish at the centre","potatoes, cabbage, beans","wet, green farming"]}}
### Built around the sea
Portugal eats more fish per person than any other EU country, about 55–60 kg a year, more than twice the EU average. Sardines, horse mackerel (carapau), hake, octopus and cod are everyday food, not luxuries.
### Soup first
A typical meal still starts with soup: caldo verde, made from finely shredded couve galega (a dark leafy cabbage), potatoes and olive oil, or a simple vegetable purée. Bread is on every table, from the dense maize broa of the north to the wheat loaves of the Alentejo.
• Pulses: chickpeas, broad beans and many kinds of bean
• Seasonal vegetables, often from a family plot
• Fruit for dessert, with sweets kept for feast days
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Soup","Fish or meat, potatoes","Fruit","A short black coffee"]}
### What science says
In 2024 a trial in Galicia found that families who followed the Atlantic diet for six months were less likely to develop metabolic syndrome, a cluster of risks for heart disease and diabetes. One study proves little on its own, but it fits a wider pattern: diets rich in fish, vegetables and olive oil tend to be healthy.
!! Portuguese food is simply Mediterranean => Portugal is included in UNESCO's Mediterranean diet listing, but it has no Mediterranean coast, and its cooking leans on the Atlantic
> The Atlantic diet is simple food from the sea and the garden, eaten slowly and together.

# Bacalhau
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1500s","l":"Fishing off Newfoundland"},{"y":"1930s","l":"White Fleet expands"},{"y":"1970s","l":"Fleet era ends"},{"y":"Today","l":"Cod from Norway, Iceland"}]}
Portugal's favourite fish doesn't live in Portuguese waters. Fishermen sailed to Newfoundland and Greenland to catch cod, salting it on board so it would keep for months.
Today most bacalhau comes from Norway and Iceland, but the Portuguese way of salting, drying and soaking it remains a national ritual.
---
Cod lives in cold northern seas. Portuguese sailors were fishing the Grand Banks off Newfoundland soon after 1500. Salt cod suited a Catholic country with many meatless fast days and long sea voyages: it was cheap, light and very hard to spoil.
### The White Fleet
Under the Estado Novo, cod fishing became a national campaign to make Portugal self-sufficient. From the 1930s the White Fleet, ships painted white, sailed north each spring for about six months.
• Each fisherman fished alone from a tiny boat called a dóri, with hand lines
• Fog, ice and storms made it one of Europe's most dangerous jobs
• A hospital ship, the Gil Eannes, sailed with the fleet; it is now a museum in Viana do Castelo
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Sail north in spring","Fish alone from a dóri","Gut and salt on board","Dry in the sun at home"]}
The fleet faded in the 1970s, as countries extended their fishing zones to 200 nautical miles and cod stocks collapsed. Portugal now imports almost all its cod, much of it from Norway.
### From the sea to the plate
Salt cod must be soaked (demolhado) for one to three days, changing the water often, before it is cooked.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Bacalhau à Brás","b":"shredded, with egg and fries"},{"a":"À Gomes de Sá","b":"Porto style, baked, potatoes"},{"a":"Com natas","b":"baked in a creamy sauce"},{"a":"Pataniscas","b":"fritters in batter"}]}
!? Bacalhau à Brás is said to be named after Brás, a tavern keeper in Lisbon's Bairro Alto, though almost nothing is known about him.
> Bacalhau is a fish from the far north turned into a Portuguese staple by salt, sun and centuries of habit.

# Sardines
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"June–Oct","l":"best season"},{"v":"~20 cm","l":"a large sardine"},{"v":"1853","l":"oldest cannery founded"}]}
Grilled sardines are the smell of a Portuguese summer. They are best from about June to October, when they are fat and oily, and are eaten on a slice of bread that soaks up the juices.
Portugal is also famous for tinned fish: canneries have packed sardines, tuna and mackerel in olive oil since the 19th century.
---
The sardine is a small, oily fish that swims in huge shoals along the Iberian coast. In spring it is thin; through the summer it feeds on plankton and builds up fat. Grilled whole over charcoal with coarse salt, it needs nothing else.
### How to eat them
• Lay a hot sardine on bread, often maize broa in the north
• Eat with your fingers, lifting the flesh off the bones
• Add a salad of tomato and roasted peppers, and boiled potatoes
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Coarse salt on whole fish","Charcoal grill, few minutes","Onto a slice of bread","Eat with your fingers"]}
### The canning towns
Canning arrived in the 19th century and boomed during the two world wars, when armies needed food that kept. Setúbal, Matosinhos, Portimão, Olhão and Peniche were lined with factories, where thousands of women cleaned and packed fish by hand on long shifts. Ramirez, founded in 1853, says it is the oldest canning company in the world still working.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1853","l":"Ramirez founded"},{"y":"1914–18","l":"War boosts tinned fish"},{"y":"1980s","l":"Many canneries close"},{"y":"2010s","l":"Gourmet tins revival"}]}
### Fewer fish, then more
Sardine numbers rise and fall naturally. In the 2010s stocks off Portugal and Spain fell so low that catches were tightly limited for several years. They have since recovered, but scientists and fishermen still watch the quotas closely.
!? Some fans age tins of sardines for years, like wine, turning them now and then, and say the fish grow softer and richer in the oil.
> A sardine is at its best simply grilled in summer, and at its most lasting packed in olive oil.

# Regional dishes
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Regional dishes","items":["North: caldo verde, tripas","Centre: leitão da Bairrada","Alentejo: pork and bread","Algarve: cataplana"]}
Portugal is small, but each region cooks differently. The green, rainy north eats hearty soups, tripe and cured meats; the centre roasts suckling pig; the Alentejo makes bread soups and pork dishes; the Algarve cooks fish and shellfish.
The islands add their own: stews cooked in volcanic ground in the Azores, and beef skewers on bay-wood sticks in Madeira.
---
Regional cooking follows the land. Where there is rain and grass, there are cows and cabbages; where there are cork-oak plains, there are black pigs; along the coast, there is fish.
### North
Porto people are nicknamed tripeiros, "tripe eaters". Legend says that in 1415 the city gave all its meat to the fleet sailing to conquer Ceuta and kept only the offal, creating tripas à moda do Porto, a stew of tripe and white beans. The francesinha, a meat sandwich under melted cheese and a spicy beer-and-tomato sauce, appeared in Porto in the mid-20th century.
### Centre
Bairrada, between Coimbra and Aveiro, is known for leitão: suckling pig roasted whole in wood ovens, with crisp skin and a peppery sauce, eaten with the region's sparkling wine.
### South
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Açorda","b":"bread soup, garlic, coriander"},{"a":"Migas","b":"fried bread with pork"},{"a":"Porco à alentejana","b":"pork with clams"},{"a":"Cataplana","b":"seafood in a copper pan"}]}
Black Iberian pigs (porco preto) roam the Alentejo's montado, open woodland of cork and holm oaks, and fatten on acorns in autumn.
### Islands
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Azores","items":["cozido das Furnas","stewed ~6 h in hot soil","pineapples and tea"]},"r":{"t":"Madeira","items":["espetada on bay sticks","bolo do caco bread","Madeira wine"]}}
!! Pork with clams comes from the Alentejo => Despite its name, carne de porco à alentejana is often said to have been created on the coast, pairing Alentejo pork with Algarve clams
> Portuguese regional food is a map of the landscape: pasture, oak plains, coast and volcano.

# Olive oil
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Olives picked Oct–Jan","Crushed within hours","Oil separated from paste","Bottled as extra virgin"]}
Olive oil, azeite, is the fat of Portuguese cooking. It dresses fish and boiled potatoes, finishes soups and is poured generously over bacalhau.
Portugal has become one of the world's leading producers, mainly thanks to new irrigated groves in the Alentejo. Traditional varieties such as Galega and Cobrançosa give oils from mild to peppery.
---
The word azeite comes from the Arabic az-zayt, "the oil". Olive trees have grown here since Roman times or earlier: an olive tree at Mouriscas, near Abrantes, has been estimated by researchers at over 3,000 years old, among the oldest in Europe.
### Old groves and new
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Traditional","items":["~100 trees per hectare","old trees, hand care","lower yields"]},"r":{"t":"Super-intensive","items":["1,500+ trees per ha","machine harvest, drip","high yields"]}}
### The Alqueva effect
The Alqueva dam on the Guadiana, completed in 2002, created one of Western Europe's largest artificial lakes and brought irrigation to the dry Alentejo. Hedgerow olive groves spread fast, and Portugal went from importing olive oil to exporting it. Critics worry about water use, pesticides and the loss of traditional landscapes.
### Grades on the label
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Extra virgin","b":"best, acidity ≤ 0.8%"},{"a":"Virgin","b":"good, acidity ≤ 2%"},{"a":"Olive oil","b":"refined oil plus virgin"}]}
• Acidity here measures free fatty acids, not sourness you can taste
• Several regions, such as Trás-os-Montes and Moura, have protected-origin (DOP) oils
• Light and heat spoil oil, so dark bottles and cool cupboards help
!! You shouldn't fry with extra virgin olive oil => Its smoke point, around 190–210 °C, is above normal frying temperatures, and it is quite stable when heated
> Portugal's olive oil boom joined ancient trees with modern irrigation, bringing real gains and real trade-offs.

# Cheese
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Serra da Estrela","b":"ewe's milk, soft, spoonable"},{"a":"Azeitão","b":"ewe's milk, near Setúbal"},{"a":"Nisa","b":"ewe's milk, semi-hard"},{"a":"São Jorge","b":"cow's milk, hard, Azores"}]}
Portugal's best-known cheeses are made from ewe's milk and set not with animal rennet but with the flowers of a wild thistle. The result is often soft, creamy and slightly bitter.
Serra da Estrela is the most famous, but Azeitão, Nisa, Évora and São Jorge from the Azores all have protected status.
---
The thistle is the cardoon, a wild relative of the artichoke. Its dried purple flowers are soaked and crushed, and the liquid, rich in plant enzymes, curdles the milk. This method is rare outside Portugal and Spain.
### How Serra da Estrela is made
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Raw ewe's milk, warmed","Thistle flower infusion","Curds pressed by hand","Cured at least 30 days"]}
It is made from late autumn to spring around the Serra da Estrela, mainland Portugal's highest range, from the milk of local sheep breeds. When ripe, the paste is so soft that the top crust is cut off like a lid and the cheese is scooped out with a spoon. Aged longer, as Serra da Estrela Velho, it becomes firm and sharp.
### Across the country
• Azeitão: small, creamy ewe's cheese from the Arrábida hills
• Nisa and Évora: firmer ewe's cheeses from the Alentejo
• São Jorge: a hard cow's milk cheese, sharp and peppery
• Requeijão: a fresh whey cheese, often eaten with pumpkin jam
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Thistle rennet","items":["plant enzymes","creamy, slightly bitter","suits vegetarians"]},"r":{"t":"Animal rennet","items":["enzyme from calves","milder, cleaner taste","most cheeses worldwide"]}}
!? The Azores have roughly as many cows as people and produce about a third of Portugal's milk, much of it from cows grazing outdoors all year.
> In Portugal a wild thistle, not a calf's stomach, turns sheep's milk into some of Europe's most distinctive cheese.

# Convent sweets
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Egg whites starch linen","Yolks left over","Sugar from the colonies","Convent sweets"]}
Many Portuguese sweets were born in convents and monasteries. Nuns and monks used egg whites to starch linen and clear wine, leaving mountains of yolks, which they mixed with sugar from Madeira and Brazil.
The results have heavenly names: toucinho do céu, papos de anjo, barriga de freira. The pastel de nata is the most famous heir of this tradition.
---
From the 15th century, sugar from Madeira and later Brazil made Portugal one of the first European countries where sugar was fairly plentiful. Convents had eggs from their farms, sugar from the trade, time and skill. They sold sweets to raise money or gave them to benefactors.
### Names with a wink
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Toucinho do céu","b":"'heaven's bacon', almond cake"},{"a":"Papos de anjo","b":"'angel's chins', yolk puffs"},{"a":"Barriga de freira","b":"'nun's belly', bread pudding"},{"a":"Ovos moles","b":"sweet yolk in wafer shells"}]}
### When the convents closed
In 1834, after the Liberal Wars, religious orders for men were abolished and their property sold; convents for women could stay open until their last nun died. Recipes passed to families and bakeries. In 1837 a shop beside the Jerónimos Monastery began selling the custard tarts now known as pastéis de Belém, from a recipe still kept secret.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1450s","l":"Sugar cane on Madeira"},{"y":"1500s","l":"Brazil's sugar trade"},{"y":"1834","l":"Male orders abolished"},{"y":"1837","l":"Belém tarts on sale"}]}
### Nata or Belém?
Only the Belém shop can call its tarts pastéis de Belém; everywhere else they are pastéis de nata. Both have flaky, rolled pastry and a custard of egg yolks, milk and sugar, scorched in a very hot oven and dusted with cinnamon if you like.
• Aveiro: ovos moles in shell-shaped wafers
• Sintra: queijadas and travesseiros
• Tentúgal, near Coimbra: paper-thin pastries filled with egg cream
!? Ovos moles de Aveiro were the first Portuguese pastry to receive EU protected status, in 2008.
> Portugal's sweets are the sweet side of history: sugar from the empire, eggs from the farm and patience from the cloister.

# Wine regions
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Vinho Verde: fresh, light, Minho","Douro: port and bold reds","Alentejo: warm, ripe, smooth","Plus Dão, Bairrada, Madeira"]}
Portugal packs very different wines into a small country. In the rainy Minho, Vinho Verde is light and fresh. Up the Douro valley, terraced schist slopes give port and powerful reds. On the hot Alentejo plains, wines are ripe and smooth.
Most are made from native grapes found almost nowhere else, such as Alvarinho, Touriga Nacional and Antão Vaz.
---
### Vinho Verde
The region in the green north-west, demarcated in 1908, is one of the largest in Europe. Its best-known grape is Alvarinho, grown around Monção and Melgaço on the Minho river. Light, fairly low in alcohol and sometimes slightly fizzy, the wines suit fish and seafood.
!! Vinho Verde is made from green grapes => "Verde" means young: the wine is meant to be drunk fresh, and it can be white, red or rosé
### Douro
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"1756","l":"region demarcated"},{"v":"2001","l":"UNESCO World Heritage"},{"v":"~43,000 ha","l":"of vineyards"}]}
The Douro's vineyards climb steep schist slopes, cut into terraces by hand over centuries. Summers are very hot, and vine roots push deep into cracks in the rock to find water. Port is made here and traditionally aged in Vila Nova de Gaia, opposite Porto, while unfortified Douro reds and whites have become famous in their own right.
### Alentejo
In the south, wide plains, hot summers and large estates give rich, fruity reds. The Alentejo also keeps a Roman method alive: vinho de talha, fermented in huge clay jars.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Vinho Verde","items":["cool, rainy, granite","fresh, crisp, light"]},"r":{"t":"Alentejo","items":["hot, dry plains","ripe, soft, strong"]}}
### More to explore
• Dão: elegant reds from granite hills
• Bairrada: Baga reds and sparkling wines
• Madeira and Setúbal: fortified and sweet wines
!? The Portuguese drink more wine per person than almost any other nation, about 60 litres a year per adult.
> Climate, soil and native grapes make Portugal's wine map one of the most varied in Europe.

# Coffee culture
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Bica / cimbalino","b":"espresso: Lisbon / Porto"},{"a":"Pingado","b":"espresso, drop of milk"},{"a":"Galão","b":"milky coffee in a glass"},{"a":"Meia de leite","b":"half coffee, half milk"}]}
In Portugal, um café means a short, strong espresso, drunk standing at the counter in a minute or two, often several times a day. In Lisbon it's a bica, in Porto a cimbalino.
Cafés and pastelarias are neighbourhood living rooms. Coffee is cheap, usually about €1 or less, and roasted in a dark, smooth style shaped by Portugal's links with Brazil and Africa.
---
Coffee became popular in Portugal in the 18th century, and the empire supplied it: Brazil, then São Tomé, Angola and Timor. In the early 1970s Angola was one of the world's largest producers, and its robusta beans shaped the strong Portuguese blend, which usually mixes arabica and robusta.
### Beyond the bica
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Carioca: a weaker espresso","Garoto: small milky coffee","Abatanado: long black coffee","Descafeinado: decaf"]}
The Porto word cimbalino comes from La Cimbali, an Italian brand of espresso machine that spread through the city's cafés.
### Historic cafés
• A Brasileira (1905), in Chiado, with a bronze Fernando Pessoa at a table outside
• Majestic (1921), on Porto's Rua de Santa Catarina, all mirrors and carved wood
• Nicola, on Rossio, a writers' café since the 18th century, rebuilt in art deco style in 1929
### A coffee town
Campo Maior, in the Alentejo near the Spanish border, is home to Delta, Portugal's biggest coffee brand, founded by Rui Nabeiro in 1961. The town grew around roasting, and it now has a coffee science museum.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1700s","l":"Coffee reaches Lisbon"},{"y":"1905","l":"A Brasileira opens"},{"y":"1961","l":"Delta founded"},{"y":"1970s","l":"Angola a top producer"}]}
!! "Bica" stands for "Beba Isto Com Açúcar" => It's a popular story with no evidence; "bica" also means a spout, and the word's real origin is uncertain
> In Portugal coffee is less a drink than a short pause, taken standing up, many times a day.

# Tempura and beyond
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Peixinhos da horta","Portuguese reach Japan, 1543","Batter frying in Nagasaki","Tempura"]}
Japanese tempura probably owes its start to Portuguese traders and missionaries, who reached Japan in the 1540s. They fried fish and vegetables in batter, much like Portugal's peixinhos da horta, battered green beans.
The name may come from têmporas, the Ember Days, when Catholics avoided meat. Portuguese cooking also left its mark in Goa, Macau, Malacca and Brazil.
---
Portuguese traders first landed in Japan in 1543, on the island of Tanegashima. Nagasaki became their main port from 1571. For almost a century, until they were expelled in 1639, Portuguese merchants and Jesuit missionaries brought goods, guns and recipes.
### The batter story
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Peixinhos","items":["green beans","flour and egg batter","fast days in Lisbon"]},"r":{"t":"Tempura","items":["prawns, fish, veg","light, ice-cold batter","eaten all year"]}}
The most quoted origin of the name is the Latin quattuor tempora, Portuguese têmporas: four short seasons of fasting in the Church year, when fish and vegetables replaced meat. Other words have been suggested, such as tempero (seasoning), so the origin isn't certain. Japanese cooks later made the batter thinner and crisper.
### Other traces in Japan
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Kasutera","b":"sponge cake, 'pão de Castela'"},{"a":"Konpeitō","b":"sugar sweets, from confeito"},{"a":"Pan","b":"bread, from pão"},{"a":"Botan","b":"button, from botão"}]}
### Around the world
• Goa: vindaloo, from vinha d'alhos, a wine and garlic marinade
• Macau: Macanese cooking mixes Portuguese, Chinese and Malay flavours
• Malacca: Kristang families of Portuguese descent still cook "devil's curry"
!! "Arigatō" comes from "obrigado" => The Japanese word existed long before the Portuguese arrived; the likeness is a coincidence
> Portuguese sailors carried recipes as well as spices, and some, like tempura, became famous under new names.

# What fado is
@viz {"k":"list","items":["One voice, often in a black shawl","Portuguese guitar and viola","Songs of fate, love and loss","Born in Lisbon, early 1800s"]}
Fado is Lisbon's urban song: a single voice, with the Portuguese guitar and a classical guitar, singing of love, jealousy, poverty, the sea and fate. The word comes from the Latin fatum, destiny.
It grew in the city's poor riverside districts in the 19th century, and UNESCO added it to its list of intangible heritage in 2011.
---
Nobody knows exactly how fado began. Most historians point to Lisbon in the 1820s–40s, in districts such as Alfama, Mouraria and Bairro Alto, among sailors, dockworkers and the taverns where they drank.
### Many roots
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Possible roots of fado","items":["Afro-Brazilian lundum dance","Brazilian modinha songs","Sailors' and street songs","Lisbon tavern culture"]}
The lundum and the modinha came from Brazil, especially after the royal court returned from Rio de Janeiro in 1821. The romantic idea that fado descends from Moorish songs is popular but has little evidence behind it.
### Maria Severa
The first legendary fadista was Maria Severa (1820–1846), a singer from Mouraria whose love affair with an aristocrat, the Count of Vimioso, was retold in plays, a novel and Portugal's first sound film, A Severa (1931). By tradition, the black shawl many fadistas wear mourns her.
### From taverns to the world
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1840s","l":"Maria Severa sings"},{"y":"1930s","l":"Radio and fado houses"},{"y":"1950s","l":"Amália tours abroad"},{"y":"2011","l":"UNESCO listing"}]}
Records and radio carried fado across Portugal in the 20th century, and emigrants took it to Brazil, France and North America. The Fado Museum in Alfama opened in 1998, and UNESCO's listing in November 2011 recognised fado as a living tradition rather than a museum piece.
!! Fado is always sad => Many fados are cheeky, funny or joyful, like the fast fado corrido and songs in praise of Lisbon's neighbourhoods
> Fado is Lisbon's own song, born in its poor quarters and now heard around the world.

# Fado houses
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Dinner is served","Lights go down","Silence is called","Fado is sung"]}
The classic place to hear fado is a casa de fado, a small restaurant where singers take turns between courses. When the lights dim, waiters stop serving and the room falls silent.
In fado vadio, "vagrant fado", amateurs stand up to sing in neighbourhood taverns, keeping the tradition alive away from the tourist trail.
---
Fado houses took their modern form in the 1930s, when performances moved from streets and taverns into licensed venues. Today Alfama, Mouraria and Bairro Alto have dozens of them, from tiny family taverns to elegant dining rooms. Some are run by fadistas themselves: the singer Argentina Santos ran Parreirinha de Alfama for decades.
### House rules
The phrase you may hear before a song is "silêncio, que se vai cantar o fado": "silence, fado is about to be sung".
• Talking during a song is a serious faux pas
• Doors may be closed and waiters stand still
• Applause comes at the end, never in the middle
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Casa de fado","items":["professional singers","dinner and music","set programme"]},"r":{"t":"Fado vadio","items":["amateurs take turns","a glass and a snack","local taverns"]}}
### How a night works
A house usually has a resident guitarist and viola player and several singers, who each sing a short set of three or four fados before the next takes over. Late in the evening, staff, cooks and guests who sing may join in. Many singers of today began this way.
@viz {"k":"list","items":["A set of 3–4 fados per singer","A pause to eat and talk","The next fadista takes over","Late on, others may join in"]}
!? Many fado houses charge a minimum spend instead of a ticket, so the dinner pays the musicians.
> In a fado house the music comes first: dinner pauses, the room goes dark and everyone listens.

# Lisbon and Coimbra
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Lisbon","items":["traditional tunes","new verses, old music","women and men"]},"r":{"t":"Coimbra","items":["composed songs","student serenades","men, by tradition"]}}
Fado has two homes. In Lisbon, singers often set new poems to a stock of traditional melodies, such as the fado menor or fado corrido, and phrase them freely.
Coimbra's fado grew in its university: composed songs sung by men at night, tied to student life, farewells and the city itself.
---
### Lisbon's fixed fados
Traditional Lisbon fado uses a repertoire of a few hundred named melodies. A singer chooses a melody and a poem with the right rhythm and metre, so the same tune can carry hundreds of different lyrics. The three oldest, the fado menor, fado corrido and fado mouraria, are often called the "castiço" (true-born) fados.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Fado menor","b":"slow, minor key, intense"},{"a":"Fado corrido","b":"quick, major key, lively"},{"a":"Fado mouraria","b":"named after the old quarter"},{"a":"Fado canção","b":"a song with a refrain"}]}
### The art of bending a tune
Lisbon singers talk of estilar: stretching, delaying and ornamenting the melody differently every time, so no two performances are alike. The musicians must follow every pause.
### Coimbra's songs
In Coimbra, songs were usually written by named composers and sung closer to the page, like a Romantic art song. They follow the academic year, above all the Queima das Fitas in May, when finishing students burn the ribbons of their faculty colours.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Freshers arrive","Years of serenades","Queima das Fitas in May","Farewell to Coimbra"]}
Many Coimbra musicians prefer the name "canção de Coimbra", Coimbra song, to stress that it is its own tradition.
!! Coimbra fado is Lisbon fado sung by students => It has its own repertoire, composers and guitar tuning, and grew out of Romantic song rather than tavern fado
> Lisbon fado reinvents old tunes with new words; Coimbra fado sings composed songs of student life.

# Guitarra and viola
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Guitarra","items":["12 steel strings","melody, replies","pear-shaped body"]},"r":{"t":"Viola","items":["6 strings","chords and rhythm","the 'classical guitar'"]}}
In fado, "guitarra" means the pear-shaped Portuguese guitar, while "viola" is the ordinary six-string guitar. The guitarra answers the singer's lines; the viola keeps the harmony and pulse.
The players follow the singer closely, since melodies stretch and pause. Instrumental pieces called guitarradas let the guitarra shine on its own.
---
Fado musicians rarely read sheet music. A guitarist must know hundreds of fados by ear and follow a singer who may hold a note, speed up or drop out without warning. Players describe it as a conversation more than an accompaniment.
### Who does what
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Viola sets key and rhythm","Guitarra plays the intro","Singer enters","Guitarra fills the gaps"]}

# Amália Rodrigues
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1920","l":"Born in Lisbon"},{"y":"1939","l":"Fado house debut"},{"y":"1956","l":"Olympia, Paris"},{"y":"1999","l":"Dies; national mourning"}]}
Amália Rodrigues (1920–1999) took fado from Lisbon's taverns to concert halls across the world. Born into a poor family, she sold fruit on the Lisbon docks as a girl before her voice made her a star.
She sang great poets, worked with composers who widened fado's sound, and remains the voice most people link with Portugal.
---
Amália made her professional debut in 1939 at the Retiro da Severa, a Lisbon fado house. Within a few years she was the city's best-paid fadista, touring Brazil and starring in films.
### A singer of poets
Amália chose lyrics by major poets, from Camões to David Mourão-Ferreira, Pedro Homem de Mello and Alexandre O'Neill. From 1962 the French-born composer Alain Oulman wrote melodies for her that broke with the traditional fado forms. Some purists objected; most now see it as a turning point.
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Camões","b":"16th-century epic poet"},{"a":"Mourão-Ferreira","b":"20th-century lyric poet"},{"a":"O'Neill","b":"playful, surrealist poet"},{"a":"Alain Oulman","b":"composer of new fados"}]}
### Fame abroad
The French film Les Amants du Tage (1955) featured her singing, and in 1956 she played the Olympia in Paris. She went on to tour Europe, the Americas, the Soviet Union and Japan, singing in several languages.
### Politics and memory
Because she was famous under the dictatorship, some accused her after 1974 of being the regime's singer. Later accounts say she quietly helped opponents of the regime, and historians still debate her politics.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"3 days","l":"of national mourning"},{"v":"2001","l":"moved to the Pantheon"},{"v":"1st","l":"woman buried there"}]}
When she died in October 1999, during an election campaign, the government declared three days of national mourning. In 2001 she became the first woman laid to rest in the National Pantheon.
!? Her house on Rua de São Bento, in Lisbon, is now a museum, kept much as she left it.
> Amália made fado international by pairing a unique voice with great poetry and new music.

# Fado and censorship
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Lyrics sent to censors","Approved, cut or banned","Singer needs a licence","Fado in licensed houses"]}
Under the dictatorship, fado lyrics had to be approved by censors before being sung in public, and professional singers needed an official licence. Fado moved from the streets into licensed fado houses.
The regime favoured a tidy, patriotic fado, and after 1974 some saw it as the old regime's music. It took years for fado to lose that label.
---
After the military coup of 1926, the new authorities brought in prior censorship of shows, and from 1927 fado lyrics had to be submitted for approval. Over time, rough tavern songs about crime, prostitution and injustice faded, replaced by songs about love, Lisbon and the nation.
### Licensed and controlled
• Professional fadistas needed a carteira profissional, an official card
• Performances moved into registered houses with set programmes
• Radio, and later television, spread a polished, approved style

### "Fado, Fátima, football"
Critics summed up the regime's culture as the "three Fs": fado, Fátima and football, supposedly used to keep people content. Historians see this as a simplification. Some officials disliked fado as gloomy and low-class, and the shrine and the sport had lives of their own.
### After the revolution
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1927","l":"Lyrics need approval"},{"y":"1974","l":"Censorship ends"},{"y":"Late 70s","l":"Fado seen as old-regime"},{"y":"1990s","l":"New generation revives it"}]}
In the years after 1974, protest songs and new pop dominated, and fado seemed old-fashioned. Singers such as Carlos do Carmo showed it could carry modern, socially aware poetry, and by the 1990s a new generation brought it back.
> Censorship tamed fado for half a century, but the genre outlived both the regime and the stigma.

# Cante alentejano
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"2014","l":"UNESCO listing"},{"v":"10–30","l":"singers in a group"},{"v":"0","l":"instruments"}]}
Cante alentejano, the unaccompanied group singing of the Alentejo, joined UNESCO's list in 2014. Its songs, called modas, are slow and spacious, like the plains they come from.
Once sung in fields, mines and taverns, cante now lives in choirs, festivals and schools, and young groups mix it with new music.
---
Cante belongs to everyday life as much as to the stage. Men gathered in taverns after work and began to sing, one voice at a time, until the whole room joined in. In mining towns such as Aljustrel, singing together was a way of holding a hard-working community together.
### Passed on by ear
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["A moda is remembered","Taught by ear","Sung by the group","Handed to the young"]}
Most groups are village choirs or local associations. Men often perform in traditional farm workers' clothes, with hats, waistcoats and boots. A single moda can last several minutes with only a few lines of text, stretched out and repeated.
### Saving a tradition
By the late 20th century, as villages emptied and farm work was mechanised, cante seemed to be ageing with its singers. Towns in the region prepared the UNESCO bid, and the listing brought new attention and pride.
• More women's and mixed choirs have formed
• Schools in the region have run cante classes
• Pop, jazz and fado musicians have recorded with cante groups

!? Serpa, Beja and other Alentejo towns hold regular cante festivals, and Serpa has a house dedicated to the tradition, the Casa do Cante.
> Cante survived because communities chose to keep singing it, now with new voices joining in.

# Grândola, Vila Morena
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"22:55","l":"First signal song"},{"y":"00:20","l":"Grândola broadcast"},{"y":"Dawn","l":"Troops take Lisbon"},{"y":"25 April","l":"Regime falls"}]}
Just after midnight on 25 April 1974, Rádio Renascença played José Afonso's "Grândola, Vila Morena". It was the second signal for army officers to begin the coup that ended 48 years of dictatorship.
The song, about brotherhood and equality in an Alentejo town, became the anthem of the Carnation Revolution and is still sung at every 25 April celebration.
---
José Afonso (1929–1987), known as Zeca, began as a Coimbra singer and became the leading voice of Portuguese protest song. Many of his songs were banned from radio, and he was arrested by the political police in 1973.
### The song
Afonso wrote it as a tribute to the town of Grândola, where he had sung in 1964 for a workers' music society. It describes a sun-browned town of fraternity and equality, where the people have the final say, set to a slow melody in the spirit of cante. It came out on the album Cantigas do Maio (1971), recorded in France. Instead of instruments, it opens with the crunch of marching feet on gravel.
### Two songs, two signals
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["22:55: E Depois do Adeus","Units get ready","00:20: Grândola","Troops move on Lisbon"]}
The first signal, at 22:55 on 24 April, was Paulo de Carvalho's "E Depois do Adeus", Portugal's Eurovision entry that year. A harmless love song was a safe choice. "Grândola", by a singer the regime distrusted, confirmed that the operation was going ahead.
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"1971","l":"song released"},{"v":"00:20","l":"broadcast, 25 April"},{"v":"48 years","l":"of dictatorship ended"}]}
By the next day, crowds filled the streets and handed carnations to soldiers, who put them in their rifles.
!? In 2013, protesters in the public gallery interrupted the prime minister in parliament by singing "Grândola", showing it is still a living protest song.
> A slow song about a small town became the sound of Portugal's return to democracy.

# Folk instruments
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Cavaquinho","b":"small 4-string, Minho"},{"a":"Gaita-de-foles","b":"bagpipe, Trás-os-Montes"},{"a":"Adufe","b":"square frame drum, Beira"},{"a":"Viola campaniça","b":"folk guitar, Alentejo"}]}
Beyond fado, Portuguese folk music uses a family of local instruments: the small cavaquinho, regional guitars called violas, bagpipes in the north-east and square drums in the Beira.

### From cavaquinho to ukulele
The cavaquinho, a tiny four-string guitar from the Minho, travelled with emigrants. Madeiran woodworkers began building them in Honolulu, and Hawaiians adopted it as the ukulele.

Portuguese music today mixes old and new. Since the late 1990s, a generation of fadistas has brought fado back to young audiences, often with new instruments and poets.

# Romanesque churches
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Thick granite walls","Round arches","Small, high windows","Battlements on cathedrals"]}
Portugal's oldest great churches are Romanesque, built mostly in the 12th and 13th centuries as the kingdom took shape. They have thick granite walls, round arches and small windows.
Cathedrals such as those of Lisbon, Porto and Coimbra look like castles, with battlements, because they were built in a time of war.
---
Romanesque arrived from France with Burgundian nobles and monks, including Count Henry of Burgundy, father of the first king, and along the pilgrim routes to Santiago de Compostela. The granite north has the most examples.
### Fortress cathedrals
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Braga","b":"begun around 1070"},{"a":"Lisbon","b":"begun after 1147 conquest"},{"a":"Coimbra (Sé Velha)","b":"1160s, with battlements"},{"a":"Porto","b":"towering over the Ribeira"}]}
Lisbon's cathedral was begun soon after Afonso Henriques took the city in 1147. Coimbra's Old Cathedral, started around 1162, is one of the best-preserved Romanesque cathedrals in Portugal, its crenellated walls ready for a siege.
### Village churches
In the valleys of the Sousa, Tâmega and Douro, east of Porto, dozens of small Romanesque churches, monasteries and bridges are linked in a tourist route, the Rota do Românico, with 58 monuments. Carvings around their doors show animals, monsters and knots, symbolic rather than realistic.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Romanesque","items":["round arches","thick walls, dim","heavy and low"]},"r":{"t":"Gothic","items":["pointed arches","thinner walls, windows","tall and light"]}}
### A rare town hall
The Domus Municipalis in Bragança, a five-sided building over a water cistern, is one of very few civic buildings in Romanesque style surviving in the Iberian Peninsula.
!? On many granite churches you can still spot masons' marks, small signs each stonemason carved on his blocks so his work could be counted and paid.
> Portugal's Romanesque churches are castles of faith: heavy granite, round arches and little light.

# Alcobaça
@viz {"k":"nums","items":[{"v":"1153","l":"monastery founded"},{"v":"~106 m","l":"length of the church"},{"v":"1989","l":"UNESCO listing"}]}
King Afonso Henriques gave Alcobaça to Cistercian monks in 1153. Its church, begun in 1178, was one of the first Gothic buildings in Portugal.
Its long, bare, white nave reflects Cistercian ideals of simplicity. Inside lie the carved tombs of King Pedro I and Inês de Castro, Portugal's most famous lovers.
---
The Cistercians, a reforming order shaped by Bernard of Clairvaux, preached poverty, work and plain buildings without figures or colour. At Alcobaça they ran huge estates, draining marshes and planting orchards, and the monastery became one of the richest in the kingdom.
### A church of light and stone
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Ribbed vault, ~20 m high","Tall, plain columns","Aisles almost as high","Long nave, ~106 m"]}
Alcobaça is a hall church: its side aisles rise almost as high as the central nave, so light spreads evenly instead of falling in shafts. The effect is calm and very tall.
### Pedro and Inês
Inês de Castro was a Galician noblewoman loved by the heir to the throne, Pedro. His father, Afonso IV, feared her family's influence, and in 1355 she was killed by royal courtiers. As king, Pedro took revenge on two of the killers. Their carved Gothic tombs face each other, so that, tradition says, they will see each other first when they rise on Judgement Day.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1153","l":"Given to Cistercians"},{"y":"1178","l":"Church begun"},{"y":"1355","l":"Inês de Castro killed"},{"y":"1989","l":"World Heritage Site"}]}
### The kitchen
In the 18th century the monks built a vast tiled kitchen, with a channel of the river Alcoa running through it to bring fresh water and fish.
!! Inês was crowned as a corpse in a real ceremony => The coronation of her body is a legend with no contemporary evidence; Pedro did declare they had married in secret
> Alcobaça shows Cistercian Gothic at its purest: tall, bare and full of even light.

# Batalha
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1385","l":"Aljubarrota victory"},{"y":"1386","l":"Building begins"},{"y":"1517","l":"Work largely stops"},{"y":"1983","l":"World Heritage Site"}]}
King João I promised a monastery to the Virgin Mary if he won the Battle of Aljubarrota in 1385. The result, Batalha ("battle"), took over a century to build and is a masterpiece of Portuguese Gothic.
Its chapter house has a vault about 19 metres wide with no central column, and its Unfinished Chapels are open to the sky.
---
Work started in 1386 and went on in phases into the early 16th century, under several master builders, first Afonso Domingues and then a master called Huguet. The style blends local Gothic with touches of English Perpendicular, perhaps a nod to the queen, Philippa of Lancaster.
### What to see
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Founder's Chapel","b":"tombs of João I, Philippa"},{"a":"Chapter house","b":"vast vault, no column"},{"a":"Royal cloister","b":"Gothic and Manueline"},{"a":"Unfinished Chapels","b":"roofless octagon"}]}
In the Founder's Chapel, João I and Philippa lie side by side, holding hands, surrounded by the tombs of their sons, including Henry the Navigator.
### A daring vault
The chapter house is a square of about 19 metres whose star-shaped vault spans the whole room without support. Legend says it fell twice, and that the old, blind architect Afonso Domingues sat under it for three days after the scaffolding came down to prove it would stand. The writer Alexandre Herculano made the story famous in the 19th century.
### The Unfinished Chapels
King Duarte planned a second pantheon: an octagon of chapels behind the church. Work stopped when Manuel I moved money and builders to the Jerónimos in Lisbon, leaving huge carved pillars rising towards a roof that was never built.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Vow before battle","Victory, 1385","A century of building","Pantheon left roofless"]}
!? Since 1921 the chapter house has held the tomb of two unknown soldiers of the First World War, one from Flanders and one from Africa, with a permanent guard of honour.
> Batalha is a battle won in stone: a royal vow turned into Portugal's finest Gothic building.

# Manueline style
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Twisted ropes","Armillary spheres","Cross of the Order of Christ","Coral, seaweed and shells"]}
Manueline is Portugal's own late Gothic style, from about 1490 to 1530, the age of King Manuel I. It covers doors, windows and columns with carved ropes, anchors, shells and coral.
It was paid for by the wealth of the sea route to India, and its decoration celebrates the voyages and the king's power.
---
The name is modern: the historian Francisco Adolfo de Varnhagen coined "Manueline" in 1842. Builders at the time simply mixed Gothic structures with ornament inspired by the sea, by royal emblems, and by Spanish, Flemish and Italian ideas.
### Reading the symbols
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Armillary sphere","b":"Manuel I's personal emblem"},{"a":"Cross of Christ","b":"order that funded voyages"},{"a":"Ropes and knots","b":"ships and the sea"},{"a":"Coral, seaweed","b":"the ocean's riches"}]}
### The window of Tomar
The most famous Manueline window is in the Convent of Christ at Tomar, seat of the Order of Christ. Designed by Diogo de Arruda around 1510, it is framed by carved roots, ropes, coral, chains and buoys, crowned by the cross of the order and the royal arms. Some read it as a tree growing out of the sea, carrying the king's power.

### Beyond the big monuments
• The Church of Jesus in Setúbal (1490s), with columns twisted like ropes, is an early example
• Manueline doors and windows appear on churches and houses in towns across the country
• The 19th century revived the style as Neo-Manueline, as at Lisbon's Rossio station

> Manueline is the Age of Discovery carved in stone: ropes, spheres and coral wrapped around Gothic bones.

# Jerónimos and Belém Tower
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Jerónimos","items":["monastery, from 1501","tombs: Gama, Camões","vast cloister"]},"r":{"t":"Belém Tower","items":["fortress, 1514–1519","guarded the Tagus","stone rhinoceros"]}}
On the riverbank at Belém stand Portugal's two most famous Manueline buildings. The Jerónimos Monastery, begun in 1501, holds the tombs of Vasco da Gama and Luís de Camões.
The Belém Tower, built about 1514–1519, guarded the entrance to Lisbon's harbour. Both have been World Heritage Sites since 1983.
---
Manuel I founded the Jerónimos near the chapel where Vasco da Gama's crew is said to have prayed before sailing for India in 1497. He gave it to the Hieronymite monks, who were to pray for the king and for sailors. A tax on the spice trade helped pay for it.
### Two architects
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Diogo de Boitaca","b":"first plan, from 1501"},{"a":"João de Castilho","b":"south portal, vault, cloister"},{"a":"Later builders","b":"Renaissance details"}]}
Inside the church, slender columns open like palm trees into a wide vault that survived the 1755 earthquake. The two-storey cloister, carved in soft golden limestone, is one of the richest in Europe.
### The tower in the river
The Belém Tower, by Francisco de Arruda, was built on a rock in the Tagus. Over the centuries the riverbank moved, and the tower now stands close to the shore. Its turrets show Moorish influence, which Arruda knew from his work on Portuguese forts in Morocco.

A small carved rhinoceros head under one turret is thought to show the animal sent from India to Manuel I in 1515, the first live rhino seen in Europe since Roman times.

> At Belém, the money and pride of the sea route to India became a monastery and a fortress.

# Tiled façades
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Tiled houses in Brazil","Emigrants return rich","Tiles cover façades","Streets of colour"]}
Azulejos moved from church interiors onto the outside of houses in the 19th century. Emigrants returning from Brazil, where tiled façades were common, are thought to have brought the fashion home, and factories made patterned tiles cheaply.
Tiles protect walls from rain and reflect light. Today whole streets in Lisbon, Porto, Aveiro and Ovar are wrapped in them.
---
In Brazil's humid climate, tiles had proved a practical way to protect walls. Back in Portugal, rich returning emigrants built showy houses, and factories in Lisbon, Porto and Gaia turned out stencilled and printed patterns that ordinary owners could afford.
### Great tiled walls

The entrance hall of Porto's São Bento station, decorated by Jorge Colaço between about 1905 and 1916, shows scenes of Portuguese history and country life in some 20,000 tiles.
### Modern tiles
From the 1950s the painter Maria Keil designed abstract tile panels for the first Lisbon Metro stations, making tiles modern again. Later stations commissioned many leading artists, turning the metro into an underground gallery.

# The Pombaline cage
@viz {"k":"stack","items":["Timber frame inside the walls","Diagonal X-shaped braces","Brick or rubble infill","Stone arches on ground floor"]}
After the 1755 earthquake, Lisbon's Baixa was rebuilt with the gaiola pombalina, a timber cage hidden inside masonry walls. Its diagonal braces help a building sway rather than burst apart.
The same planning ideas created a whole new town, Vila Real de Santo António in the Algarve, laid out from 1774.
---
### How the cage works
Plain masonry walls are strong when squeezed but crack and fall outwards when shaken sideways. The gaiola ties walls and floors together. Its key part is the frontal, an inner wall of upright and horizontal timbers braced with diagonal crosses and filled with brick or rubble.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Ground shakes sideways","Timber X-braces flex","Floors stay tied to walls","Walls crack but stand"]}
Laboratory tests in Lisbon on rebuilt frontal walls confirm that they can bend a long way and absorb energy before they fail.
### Weakened over time
• Many ground floors were opened up for shops, cutting through the cage
• Extra storeys were added on top, adding weight
• Damp rotted timbers, and some were replaced with concrete

### A planned town
Vila Real de Santo António, on the Guadiana opposite Spain, was built from scratch by Pombal's government to control fishing and the border. Its grid of streets and its central square repeat the ideas of the Baixa, and some of the stone was cut in Lisbon and shipped south.
!? The diagonal braces are called cruzes de Santo André, after the X-shaped cross on which Saint Andrew is said to have died.
> The gaiola pombalina made buildings flexible and tied together, an idea modern seismic design still follows.

# Estado Novo buildings
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Português Suave: 'soft Portuguese'","Grand public buildings","Standard primary schools","Monuments to the Discoveries"]}
The Estado Novo dictatorship (1933–1974) used architecture to project order and national pride. After an early modernist phase, it favoured a "Português Suave" style that dressed modern structures in traditional roofs, arches and stone.
Its legacy includes courthouses, schools, housing estates, the Padrão dos Descobrimentos and the bridge over the Tagus, now called 25 de Abril.
---
In the 1930s, the public works minister Duarte Pacheco commissioned strongly modernist buildings, such as the Instituto Superior Técnico in Lisbon by Porfírio Pardal Monteiro. From the late 1930s taste shifted towards a nationalist look, with pitched tiled roofs, stone arcades and towers.
### Building the nation
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Plano dos Centenários","b":"schools in set designs"},{"a":"Coimbra University","b":"old Alta demolished"},{"a":"Padrão","b":"Discoveries monument, 1960"},{"a":"Ponte Salazar","b":"Tagus bridge, 1966"}]}
### The 1940 exhibition
The Exposição do Mundo Português in Belém celebrated 800 years since the founding of Portugal and 300 since independence was restored in 1640. Its temporary pavilions shaped the Belém riverfront, including the gardens of Praça do Império. The Padrão was first built for it in light materials, then rebuilt in concrete and stone in 1960.
### A survey that surprised
In the 1950s, teams of architects travelled the country for the Survey of Popular Architecture in Portugal, published in 1961. The regime hoped they would find one typical "Portuguese house". Instead they found huge regional variety and practical, almost modern logic, which inspired a new generation, including Fernando Távora and the Porto School.

# Siza and Souto de Moura
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Álvaro Siza","b":"Pritzker Prize 1992"},{"a":"Souto de Moura","b":"Pritzker Prize 2011"},{"a":"Fernando Távora","b":"their teacher and mentor"},{"a":"Porto School","b":"local, precise, modern"}]}
Álvaro Siza (born 1933) and Eduardo Souto de Moura (born 1952) both won the Pritzker Prize, in 1992 and 2011. Both came from the "Porto School", shaped by Fernando Távora.
Their buildings are calm, precise and shaped by their sites: pools set among sea rocks, a stadium cut into a quarry, white walls that frame the light.
---
### Siza
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1966","l":"Leça tidal pools"},{"y":"1992","l":"Pritzker Prize"},{"y":"1998","l":"Expo '98 pavilion"},{"y":"1999","l":"Serralves museum"}]}
Siza's tidal pools at Leça da Palmeira sit among the rocks by the sea, so that concrete walls and natural stone frame the Atlantic. After the 1988 fire in Lisbon's Chiado, he led its reconstruction, keeping the old façades. His Portugal Pavilion for Expo '98 has a thin concrete canopy, about 20 cm thick, that hangs like a sheet between two porticos.
### Souto de Moura
He worked with Siza before opening his own office. His Braga Municipal Stadium (2003), built for Euro 2004, has only two stands; at one end the rock face of an old quarry closes the pitch. The Casa das Histórias Paula Rego in Cascais (2009) has two red concrete towers shaped like pyramids. He also led the design of the Porto Metro's stations.

In 2005 they designed the Serpentine Gallery Pavilion in London together, and their offices share a building in Porto.
!? The Leça tidal pools and Siza's nearby Boa Nova Tea House were classified as National Monuments in 2011, a rare honour for works by a living architect.

# Calçada portuguesa
@viz {"k":"list","items":["White limestone, black basalt","Hammer, sand and string lines","Templates for patterns","Laid by calceteiros"]}
Portuguese pavement is still laid by hand, stone by stone, by craftsmen called calceteiros. White limestone and black basalt are cut into small cubes and set in sand to make waves, stars and ships.
In 2021 the craft was added to Portugal's national inventory of intangible heritage. But it is slippery when wet, and cities debate where to keep it.
---
### The stones
Lisbon's white limestone traditionally comes from quarries in the limestone hills of Estremadura, north of the city, and the black basalt from the ancient volcanic rocks of the Lisbon area. Elsewhere local stone is used, such as granite in the north, with red or pink stones for colour.
### A craft of the knees
A calceteiro works kneeling, sets each stone by eye and taps it into the sand with a small hammer, using string lines for levels and templates for patterns. Joints are filled with sand and the surface is compacted. A good pavement can last for decades, and single stones are easy to replace.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"For","items":["beauty and identity","lets rainwater drain","easy to repair"]},"r":{"t":"Against","items":["slippery when wet","hard for wheelchairs","costly, few craftsmen"]}}
### The debate
Falls on polished, wet calçada are common, especially on steep streets, and uneven surfaces are hard for wheelchairs, prams and older people. Lisbon's plans have replaced some stretches with smoother stone slabs, while keeping artistic calçada in squares and historic areas. Supporters argue that rougher stones and good laying reduce the risk.
### Calçada in the world
@viz {"k":"tree","root":"Calçada abroad","items":["Brazil: Copacabana","Macau: Senado Square","Africa: Maputo, Luanda","Portugal: almost every town"]}

> Calçada is heritage you walk on, and keeping it means balancing beauty, safety and skilled hands.

# Carnaval
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Podence","b":"caretos in fringed suits"},{"a":"Torres Vedras","b":"satire and matrafonas"},{"a":"Ovar","b":"big parades of floats"},{"a":"Loulé","b":"Algarve, since 1906"}]}
Before Lent comes Carnaval, or Entrudo. Towns hold parades, children dress up, and Shrove Tuesday is often a day off, though not an official holiday.
Old rural customs survive alongside Brazilian-style samba. In Podence, in Trás-os-Montes, masked caretos in bright woollen fringes run through the village shaking cowbells.
---
Carnaval is the last party before the 40 days of Lent, traditionally a time of fasting. Its spirit is the world turned upside down: masks, men dressed as women, mockery of the powerful and, in villages, battles with flour, water or eggs.
### The caretos of Podence
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Masks of tin or leather","Fringed red-green suits","Cowbells at the waist","Run, leap, shake bells"]}
In Podence, near Macedo de Cavaleiros, young men become caretos and charge noisily through the streets. UNESCO added the Carnival of Podence to its intangible heritage list in 2019. Similar masked figures appear across Trás-os-Montes and in Galicia, and some link them with old winter rites.
### Satire in Torres Vedras
Torres Vedras calls its carnival, held since 1923, "the most Portuguese in Portugal". It avoids samba; instead, giant figures and floats mock politicians and celebrities, and groups of men dressed as women, the matrafonas, fill the streets.
### Samba by the sea
Ovar, Mealhada, Loulé, Sesimbra and Funchal stage large parades with samba schools and floats inspired by Rio de Janeiro. Loulé's, from 1906, is among the oldest in the country.
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Traditional","items":["masks and bells","village streets","mocking the powerful"]},"r":{"t":"Brazilian-style","items":["samba schools","feathered costumes","parades of floats"]}}
!? Shrove Tuesday is not a public holiday: each year the government decides whether to give its staff the day off, and many employers follow.
> Carnaval turns the world upside down for a few days, from masked caretos to satirical floats.

# Easter traditions
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Lent: fish, not meat, on Fridays","Holy Week processions","Folar from godparents","The compasso visits homes"]}
Easter is a major family feast in Portugal, especially in the north. In Braga, Holy Week brings solemn night processions that draw thousands of visitors.
On Easter Sunday, many villages receive the compasso, a small procession that visits homes with a cross. Godparents give their godchildren folar, a sweet or savoury Easter bread.
---
For practising Catholics, Easter celebrates the resurrection of Jesus and ends the 40 days of Lent. For many less religious families too, it is a time for shared meals, sugared almonds and roast lamb or kid. Good Friday is a national public holiday.
### Holy Week in Braga
Braga, an archbishop's seat since early medieval times, has Portugal's best-known Holy Week. In the Ecce Homo procession on the Thursday night, barefoot hooded figures called farricocos carry torches and rattles through the dark streets.
### The compasso
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Bells ring on Sunday","Doors open, petals laid","Cross offered to kiss","Table of sweets and port"]}
In the Minho and other northern areas, a group led by a priest or lay members of the parish goes from house to house with a cross. Families who want the visit leave their door open and cover the floor with flowers.
### Folar
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Sweet folar","items":["sweet, soft bread","whole eggs baked in","much of the country"]},"r":{"t":"Savoury folar","items":["bread with cured meats","Chaves, Valpaços","Trás-os-Montes"]}}
Tradition says godchildren take a gift to their godparents on Palm Sunday, and receive a folar in return at Easter.
• Sugared almonds (amêndoas) in pastel colours fill shop windows
• Roast lamb or kid (cabrito) is the classic Easter lunch
!? Easter Sunday can fall anywhere between 22 March and 25 April: it is the Sunday after the first full moon on or after the spring equinox.
> Portuguese Easter mixes solemn processions with open doors, shared tables and gifts between godparents and godchildren.

# Santo António
@viz {"k":"list","items":["12 June: marchas on the Avenida","All night: arraiais in Alfama","13 June: Lisbon's holiday","Weddings of Santo António"]}
Lisbon's favourite saint is Santo António. On the night of 12 June every old neighbourhood becomes an arraial, a street party with grilled sardines, bifanas, beer and music.
The saint is known as a matchmaker and a finder of lost things, and each year the city hosts weddings in his name.
---
Santo António was born Fernando de Bulhões in Lisbon, near the cathedral, where a church now stands on the site of his family's house. He took the name António when he joined the Franciscans.
### Thrones and coins
In the past, children built little altars to the saint, the tronos de Santo António, on doorsteps and asked passers-by for "a little coin for Santo António". The custom is said to date from collections to rebuild his church after 1755, and it has been revived in some neighbourhoods and schools.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Children build an altar","Statue, flowers, candles","Ask for a coin","Money goes to the party"]}
### The weddings
The Casamentos de Santo António began in 1958, sponsored by a newspaper for couples who could not afford a wedding. They stopped after 1974 and were revived by the city in 1997. Today a group of couples marry on the saint's day, in religious or civil ceremonies, and parade through the city.

### The sardine as symbol
The sardine has become the festival's emblem. Each year Lisbon holds a design competition for sardine illustrations, which appear on posters, shop windows and souvenirs.

# São João
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"23 June","l":"Night: hammers, leeks"},{"y":"Midnight","l":"Fireworks on the Douro"},{"y":"Dawn","l":"Walk to the beach"},{"y":"24 June","l":"Rabelo boat regatta"}]}
São João is Porto's biggest night. On 23 June the city stays out until dawn, tapping strangers on the head with plastic hammers, waving leeks, grilling sardines and eating caldo verde.
At midnight, fireworks burst over the Douro. Many people then walk to the sea at Foz to wait for sunrise. Braga also holds a huge São João.
---
The feast of Saint John the Baptist falls on 24 June, close to the summer solstice. Across Europe, midsummer night was linked with fire, water and herbs, and Porto keeps traces of these older customs.
### Night rituals
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Jump over small bonfires","Light paper hot-air balloons","Give a pot of manjerico basil","Gather herbs for luck"]}
• Cascatas sanjoaninas are miniature scenes of town life around a statue of the saint, built by neighbourhoods and judged in contests
• Paper hot-air balloons with a flame inside are now restricted, because they can start fires
• Roast lamb with potatoes is a classic lunch on the 24th
### The regatta
On 24 June the port wine houses race their rabelo boats, the flat-bottomed sailing boats once used to bring barrels of port down the Douro to Gaia. It is a reminder of the time before roads, railways and dams, when the river was the only highway.
### Porto and Braga

# São Pedro
@viz {"k":"list","items":["29 June: last June saint","Patron of fishermen","Big in Póvoa de Varzim","Also Sintra, Montijo, Évora"]}
São Pedro, on 29 June, closes the month of the popular saints. As Saint Peter was a fisherman, his feast is strongest in coastal towns.
In Póvoa de Varzim, neighbourhoods compete with decorated streets, cascatas and rusgas, groups of singers and dancers in fishermen's dress. Sintra, Montijo and Évora also hold big São Pedro fairs.
---
In the Gospels, Peter was a fisherman on the Sea of Galilee, and he is traditionally the keeper of the keys of heaven. Portuguese fishing communities adopted him as a protector, alongside local devotions to the Virgin Mary.
### Póvoa de Varzim
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Bairros decorate streets","Arches and cascatas built","Rusgas parade at night","Bonfires and grilled fish"]}
Póvoa, north of Porto, was one of the main fishing towns of the north. On São Pedro night its neighbourhoods, each with its own colours, compete for the best decorations and the best rusga, and the whole town stays up late. The 29th is the town's municipal holiday.
### The June saints together

### By the sea
• Boats are decorated, and in some harbours blessed

# Romarias
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Mass and procession","Brass bands and Zés Pereiras","Fair, food and dancing","Fireworks to finish"]}
A romaria is a festival built around a pilgrimage to a chapel or shrine. It mixes a religious procession with a fair: brass bands, drums, folk dancing, stalls of sweets and fireworks.
Summer is romaria season, above all in the north. Viana do Castelo's Senhora d'Agonia in August is one of the biggest, with streets carpeted in coloured sawdust and flowers.
---
The word comes from romeiro, originally a pilgrim to Rome. Many romarias began as gatherings at isolated hilltop chapels, where people came from miles around and stayed for food, music and dancing after the service.
### What happens
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Pilgrims arrive","Procession with the image","Bands, dances and fair","Late-night fireworks"]}
Some pilgrims keep a promessa, a promise made in a time of need, by walking the last stretch or bringing candles. For many others the romaria is mainly a reunion with family and neighbours, including emigrants who time their summer visit home around it.
### Famous romarias
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Senhora d'Agonia","b":"Viana do Castelo, August"},{"a":"Senhora dos Remédios","b":"Lamego, a huge stairway"},{"a":"São Bento","b":"Gerês, one of the busiest"},{"a":"Senhora da Nazaré","b":"Nazaré, September"}]}
In Viana, women in traditional dress parade wearing layers of gold, and fishermen carry the image of the Virgin to the sea.
### Giants and big heads
Gigantones, giant figures several metres tall, and cabeçudos, figures with enormous heads, dance through the crowds. Zés Pereiras, bands of big bass drums, sometimes with bagpipes, wake the town at dawn.
!? Every four years in Tomar, at the Festa dos Tabuleiros, young women carry trays of bread on their heads as tall as themselves; the last was in 2023.
> A romaria joins the sacred and the festive: a procession in the morning, a party until late at night.

# Autumn feasts
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Pão por Deus","items":["1 November","children knock on doors","bread, nuts, sweets"]},"r":{"t":"São Martinho","items":["11 November","roast chestnuts","new wine"]}}
On 1 November, All Saints' Day, children in many places go door to door asking for "pão por Deus", bread for God's sake, and receive bread, nuts, apples and sweets.
On 11 November, São Martinho, families gather for a magusto: chestnuts roasted over a fire, with young wine. A spell of mild weather around then is called the "summer of São Martinho".
---
### Pão por Deus
Children carry small cloth bags and recite rhymes at each door. In Lisbon a story links the custom with 1755, when the earthquake struck on All Saints' Day and survivors begged for food. In some regions it has other names, such as bolinho. The next day, All Souls', many families visit cemeteries and bring flowers to their relatives' graves.
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Cloth bag ready","Knock and say the rhyme","Bread, nuts, sweets","Share with friends"]}
!! Pão por Deus is a copy of Halloween => The Portuguese custom is centuries old and independent; Halloween has spread more recently alongside it
### The legend of São Martinho
Martin was a 4th-century Roman soldier. On a stormy day he met a freezing beggar and cut his cloak in two to share it. Legend says the sun came out at once, the origin of the few mild days often seen in November. He later became bishop of Tours, in France.
### The magusto
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Chestnuts scored and roasted","Fire, clay pot or old drum","Água-pé or jeropiga to drink","Faces smudged with soot"]}
Chestnuts were a staple food in the north and centre before potatoes arrived from the Americas. Água-pé is a light drink made by adding water to pressed grape skins, and jeropiga is sweet grape juice strengthened with spirit. A popular saying sends everyone to the cellar on São Martinho's day to taste the new wine.
!? All Saints' Day was dropped as a public holiday in 2013, during the financial crisis, and restored in 2016.
> Portugal's autumn feasts are about sharing: bread at the door on 1 November, chestnuts round the fire on the 11th.

# Christmas and consoada
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["24 Dec: consoada dinner","Midnight: Missa do Galo","Gifts at midnight or 25th","25 Dec: family lunch"]}
Portugal's main Christmas meal is the consoada, on the night of 24 December. The classic menu is boiled bacalhau with cabbage, potatoes and eggs, all drizzled with olive oil and garlic.
Then come sweets like rabanadas, sonhos and filhoses, and many families open presents at midnight. Bolo-rei, a ring-shaped fruit cake, stays on the table until 6 January.
---
Consoada comes from consolar, "to comfort": originally a light meal on a fast day, before the Christmas feast. Because the eve was a meatless day in the Catholic calendar, fish became the tradition. In parts of the north, octopus joins or replaces the cod. Midnight Mass is called the Missa do Galo, the "rooster's Mass".
### The sweet table
@viz {"k":"pairs","items":[{"a":"Rabanadas","b":"fried bread in syrup or wine"},{"a":"Sonhos","b":"light fried dough puffs"},{"a":"Filhoses","b":"fried dough, sugar, cinnamon"},{"a":"Aletria","b":"vermicelli milk pudding"}]}
### Bolo-rei
The bolo-rei (king cake), crowned with candied fruit, came from France in the 19th century; Lisbon's Confeitaria Nacional is said to have introduced it around 1870. It used to hide a dried broad bean and a small gift: whoever found the bean paid for next year's cake. The gift was dropped for safety reasons, and many bakeries now leave out the bean too.
### Fires and songs
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Madeiro: log fire in the square","Presépio: the nativity scene","Janeiras: carols in early Jan","Dia de Reis: 6 January"]}
In villages of the Beira and Trás-os-Montes, young people gather a huge pile of logs, the madeiro, in the church square and light it on Christmas Eve; it may burn for days. On the 25th, leftover cod is fried with cabbage and potatoes as roupa velha, "old clothes".
!? In parts of the north, families used to leave the consoada table laid overnight, with food for the souls of relatives who had died.
> Consoada puts the humble salt cod at the centre of Portugal's most important family meal.

# The rooster of Barcelos
@viz {"k":"list","items":["Black body","Red comb and hearts","Painted flowers and dots","Shaped from local clay"]}
The colourful clay rooster is Portugal's best-known souvenir, but it comes from a real craft tradition. Barcelos, in the Minho, has made pottery for centuries from the clay of the Cávado valley.
Besides roosters, its potters model whole worlds in clay, called figurado: musicians, devils, animals and village scenes, often full of humour and fantasy.
---
Barcelos potters once made mostly practical ware: pots, jugs and roof tiles, sold at the town's great weekly market. Small figures and clay whistles were a sideline for children and fairs, and they grew into an art form of their own.
### Rosa Ramalho

Rosa Ramalho (1888–1977) learned pottery as a girl, stopped when she married, and returned to it as a widow in her late sixties. Her fantastical figures, devils, beasts with human faces and strange musicians, were noticed by artists and collectors in the 1950s. She is now seen as one of Portugal's great folk artists.
### How a rooster is made

Every workshop has its own touches: the curve of the tail, the pattern of hearts, the colours of the flowers. Sizes range from tiny charms to roosters taller than a child. Many families have passed the craft down through several generations.
### City of crafts
In 2017 Barcelos joined UNESCO's Creative Cities Network for crafts and folk art. Its pottery museum holds thousands of pieces, from everyday pots to figurado.

# Saudade
@viz {"k":"flow","steps":["Something dear is absent","You feel the gap","Memory turns sweet and sad","Hope it may return"]}
Saudade is a longing for someone or something absent: a person, a place, a time, even something that may never return. It can feel sad and sweet at once.
The Portuguese often call it untranslatable and see it as part of the national character. It runs through fado, poetry and everyday speech: "tenho saudades tuas" means "I miss you".
---
The word comes from the Latin solitas, "solitude", probably influenced by saudar, "to greet". Medieval forms such as soidade appear in the songs of the Galician-Portuguese troubadours.
### A king's essay
In the 15th century, King Duarte gave saudade a chapter in his book of advice, the Leal Conselheiro. He described it as sorrow at being apart from someone loved, which can mix with pleasure in remembering them.
@viz {"k":"time","events":[{"y":"1200s","l":"In troubadour songs"},{"y":"~1438","l":"King Duarte's essay"},{"y":"1910s","l":"Saudosismo movement"},{"y":"Today","l":"Everyday speech"}]}
### Saudade as a national idea
In the early 20th century, the poet Teixeira de Pascoaes led saudosismo, a movement that made saudade the key to Portuguese identity, mixing memory of a great past with hope for the future. Critics saw it as romantic nationalism, and later writers questioned whether one feeling can define a people.
### Is it really untranslatable?
@viz {"k":"cmp","l":{"t":"Nostalgia","items":["longing for the past","mostly about time"]},"r":{"t":"Saudade","items":["longing for the absent","people, places, times","may include hope"]}}

!? Brazil even has a Day of Saudade, on 30 January.
> Saudade is the presence of an absence: a longing that hurts and comforts at the same time.
