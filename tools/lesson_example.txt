# 1 November 1755
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "9", "l": "40: earthquake strikes"}, {"y": "~10", "l": "20: tsunami hits the Tagus"}, {"y": "hours later", "l": "fires burn for days"}]}
On All Saints' Day morning, a huge earthquake struck off Portugal's coast. Churches were full and candles lit. Within minutes much of Lisbon collapsed.
---
The first shock hit at about 9:40 in the morning. Lisbon was one of Europe's richest ports, with perhaps 200,000 people, and on a holy day most of them were at Mass. Stone churches packed with worshippers were among the deadliest places to be.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "~9:40", "l": "First violent shaking"}, {"y": "+6 min", "l": "Shaking stops"}, {"y": "+40 min", "l": "Tsunami reaches the Tagus"}, {"y": "Days", "l": "Fires burn the city"}]}
### Minutes of chaos
Witnesses described three separate shocks lasting in total somewhere between about three and six minutes. Cracks several metres wide opened in the city centre. Roofs and vaults fell on the congregations, and narrow medieval streets filled with rubble, trapping people who ran outside.
• Many fled to the open riverside at Terreiro do Paço, the palace square
• The royal family survived because they were away at Belém, then outside the city
• King José I was so shaken that he reportedly refused to live under stone roofs again, and the court lived in tents and wooden buildings for years
### What was lost
The Ribeira Palace on the river collapsed with its royal library of tens of thousands of books, archives of the voyages of discovery and paintings by masters such as Titian and Rubens. A grand new opera house, opened only months before, was destroyed. The ruined Carmo Convent, still roofless above the Baixa, is the best place to picture the day.
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Ribeira Palace and royal library", "New opera house (Ópera do Tejo)", "Dozens of churches and convents", "Hospital of All Saints"]}
> The earthquake struck at the worst possible moment: a holy day, a full city and burning candles in every church.
!? The shaking was felt as far away as southern France and North Africa, and some lakes in Scandinavia were reported to slosh oddly that morning.

# Earthquake, tsunami, fire
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Earthquake", "Tsunami floods the Baixa", "Fires from candles and hearths", "City devastated"]}
The earthquake, estimated at magnitude 8.5–9, was followed by a tsunami up to several metres high and by fires that burned for days. Estimates of deaths in Lisbon range from about 30,000 to 50,000 or more.
---
Lisbon suffered three disasters in a row, each making the next worse. The epicentre lay in the Atlantic, probably a couple of hundred kilometres south-west of Cape St Vincent, where the African and Eurasian plates meet. Its exact location is still debated.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Undersea earthquake", "Buildings collapse", "Tsunami floods riverside", "Candles start fires", "Fires burn ~5 days"]}
### The wave
Around 40 minutes after the shaking, survivors who had gathered on the riverbank saw the Tagus draw back, exposing the riverbed and old wrecks. Then the sea rushed in. In Lisbon the waves reached about 5–6 metres; in parts of the Algarve, such as Lagos, they may have been higher still. The tsunami also hit Cádiz and Morocco and was recorded across the Atlantic in the Caribbean.
@viz {"k": "bars", "unit": "approx. arrival after quake, minutes", "items": [{"l": "Algarve coast", "v": 20.0, "t": "~15–20"}, {"l": "Lisbon", "v": 40.0, "t": "~40", "hi": true}, {"l": "Cornwall (UK)", "v": 270.0, "t": "~4–5 h"}]}
### The fire
Overturned candles, kitchen hearths and looters started fires that merged into a firestorm. It burned for about five or six days and destroyed much of what the shaking had left standing, including warehouses full of goods from Brazil and Asia.
• Estimates of the dead in Lisbon range from about 30,000 to 50,000 or more
• Thousands more died in the Algarve, Spain and Morocco
• Records were themselves destroyed, which is why figures remain uncertain
> Most victims died not in one event but in a chain: falling stone, then water, then fire.
!! Most people died in the tsunami => The collapse of buildings and the fires probably killed more people than the wave in Lisbon itself

# The Marquis of Pombal
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Bury the dead", "Feed survivors", "Stop looting", "Plan the new city"]}
Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, later Marquis of Pombal, led the response: "bury the dead and feed the living". He organised relief, controlled prices and planned the rebuilding.
---
Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo (1699–1782) was a former diplomat in London and Vienna who had become one of King José I's ministers in 1750. While others saw divine punishment, he treated the disaster as a practical problem. The famous phrase "bury the dead and feed the living" is attributed to him, though some historians credit it to another nobleman.
### The emergency plan
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Bodies", "b": "sunk at sea to stop disease"}, {"a": "Looting", "b": "gallows put up, troops posted"}, {"a": "Food", "b": "price caps, tax waived on grain"}, {"a": "Rebuilding", "b": "no new building till a plan"}]}
Within days, troops ringed the city to stop people leaving and to catch looters. Food was brought in by sea, and its prices were fixed. Building was frozen until engineers could prepare a new plan, so that landowners could not simply rebuild the old maze.
### Power and its price
The crisis made Carvalho e Melo the most powerful man in Portugal for 22 years. He became Count of Oeiras in 1759 and Marquis of Pombal only in 1769, so the name we use came much later. He ruled with a hard hand: after an attempt on the king's life in 1758, members of the noble Távora family were executed publicly in Belém in 1759.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1750", "l": "Becomes minister"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Leads the response"}, {"y": "1769", "l": "Made Marquis of Pombal"}, {"y": "1777", "l": "Falls from power"}]}
When José I died in 1777, Queen Maria I dismissed him. Hundreds of political prisoners were released, and he was exiled to his estate at Pombal.
> Pombal was both a brilliant crisis manager and a ruthless autocrat, and Portugal remembers him as both.

# The new Baixa
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Grid of straight streets", "Standard building designs", "Prefabricated parts", "Timber cage inside walls", "Praça do Comércio"]}
The Baixa was rebuilt on a grid of wide straight streets with uniform buildings, using standardised parts and the earthquake-resistant gaiola pombalina.
---
The flattened lower town between Rossio and the river was not patched up but redesigned. The chief engineer, Manuel da Maia, then in his late seventies, drew up several options, from rebuilding as before to moving the capital. The chosen plan, drawn by Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel, wiped the slate clean.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Before 1755", "items": ["narrow winding lanes", "mixed heights", "fire spread easily"]}, "r": {"t": "Pombaline Baixa", "items": ["straight wide grid", "uniform façades", "firewalls on roofs"]}}
### The gaiola pombalina
Inside the stone walls of each building stands a wooden "cage": a three-dimensional frame of posts and beams braced with diagonal crosses. Masonry holds it in place, but in an earthquake the timber flexes and keeps the building together instead of letting walls burst outwards. According to tradition, a model was tested by soldiers marching around it to imitate shaking.
@viz {"k": "stack", "items": ["Tiled roof with firewalls", "Floors on timber gaiola frame", "Ground floor of stone arches", "Pine piles in wet ground"]}
### An early prefab city
Windows, doors, iron railings and tiles came in a few standard sizes, made off-site and assembled quickly. Streets were named after trades: Rua do Ouro for goldsmiths, Rua da Prata for silversmiths, Rua dos Fanqueiros for cloth sellers. The palace square became Praça do Comércio, the "square of commerce", a sign that trade, not the court, would own the riverfront.
> The Baixa was one of the first city centres planned to resist earthquakes, built from standard parts on a rational grid.
!? The buildings stand on thousands of pine stakes driven into waterlogged ground. As long as they stay wet they barely rot, which is why lowering the water table is a real concern.

# The birth of seismology
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Questionnaire to parishes", "Data on timing and damage", "Compare across regions", "Early seismology"]}
Pombal sent a questionnaire to every parish: when did it start, how long did it last, what damage occurred? It was one of the first systematic scientific studies of an earthquake.
---
Before 1755, earthquakes were mostly explained as acts of God or by vague ideas about underground winds. Pombal's government did something new: it asked the same set of questions everywhere and collected the answers. That is the basic method of modern science.
### The questionnaire
Through the Church, every parish priest in the country received a list of questions. They included:
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["What time did it begin?", "How long did it last?", "Where did it seem to come from?", "How much damage and death?", "Did the sea or wells change?"]}
Many of the answers survive in the national archive, the Torre do Tombo, in a collection known as the parish memories of 1758. Modern seismologists have used them to map how strongly each place shook, and from that to estimate the epicentre and size.
ex: Collect reports of damage per parish | Draw lines of equal intensity on a map | Strongest shaking points towards the source offshore
### From stories to science
• In 1756 the young Immanuel Kant wrote three essays seeking natural causes for the quake
• In 1760 the English geologist John Michell argued earthquakes were waves travelling through the ground, partly using Lisbon data
• Seismometers came only in the 19th century, but the idea of systematic observation started here
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Same questions everywhere", "Answers collected", "Intensity mapped", "Source estimated"]}
> By asking every parish the same questions, Portugal turned a catastrophe into data.
!! The 1755 magnitude was measured at the time => Magnitude scales date from the 1930s; the 8.5–9 figure is a modern estimate from historical reports

# Shaking European thought
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Voltaire", "items": ["questioned divine goodness", "Candide mocks optimism"]}, "r": {"t": "Rousseau", "items": ["blamed crowded cities", "human choices matter"]}}
The disaster shocked Europe's thinkers. Voltaire questioned why a good God would allow it, in his "Poem on the Lisbon Disaster" and in Candide. Rousseau answered that city crowding made it worse.
---
Before 1755, a popular philosophy called optimism, linked to Leibniz and the poet Alexander Pope, held that we live in the best of all possible worlds and that every evil serves a larger good. Lisbon made that idea very hard to defend. Why would a good God destroy a pious city, on a holy day, while worshippers prayed?
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Voltaire (1756)", "b": "poem: why the innocent?"}, {"a": "Rousseau (1756)", "b": "humans built the danger"}, {"a": "Kant (1756)", "b": "look for natural causes"}, {"a": "Malagrida", "b": "God punishing sinners"}]}
### Voltaire against optimism
In his "Poem on the Lisbon Disaster" Voltaire asked whether Lisbon really had more vices than London or Paris, which still stood. In Candide (1759) he satirised optimism: his naïve heroes arrive in Lisbon during the earthquake, then watch the Inquisition burn people to prevent further quakes, while their tutor Pangloss insists all is for the best.
### Rousseau's reply
Rousseau wrote to Voltaire that nature had not built 20,000 houses of six or seven storeys in one place. If people had lived more spread out, and had not stayed behind to save their belongings, far fewer would have died. It is an early argument that "natural" disasters are partly made by human choices.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Old view", "items": ["God's punishment", "pray and repent"]}, "r": {"t": "New view", "items": ["natural event", "plan and prepare"]}}
The Jesuit preacher Gabriel Malagrida insisted the quake was divine punishment and blamed sinners. Pombal, who favoured practical measures, saw this as a threat; Malagrida was later burned by the Inquisition in 1761.
> Lisbon 1755 pushed European thinkers from asking why God allows disasters to asking how humans can reduce them.

# The Enlightenment
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Locke", "b": "natural rights"}, {"a": "Montesquieu", "b": "separation of powers"}, {"a": "Voltaire", "b": "tolerance, free speech"}, {"a": "Kant", "b": "\"dare to know\""}]}
The 18th-century Enlightenment championed reason, science, religious tolerance and individual rights. Thinkers like Locke, Montesquieu, Voltaire and Kant inspired revolutions.
---
The Enlightenment was a movement of ideas across 18th-century Europe and its colonies. Its core belief was that human reason, not tradition or religious authority alone, should guide how we understand nature and organise society. Kant summed it up in 1784 with the motto "dare to know".
@viz {"k": "pairs", "items": [{"a": "Locke", "b": "natural rights, consent"}, {"a": "Montesquieu", "b": "separation of powers"}, {"a": "Voltaire", "b": "religious tolerance"}, {"a": "Rousseau", "b": "the social contract"}]}
### Big ideas
• Government gets its legitimacy from the people, not from God
• Power should be split between those who make, apply and judge laws
• Torture and cruel punishments are irrational (Beccaria, 1764)
• Free trade and markets can create wealth (Adam Smith, 1776)
These ideas spread through books, newspapers, scientific academies, salons and coffee houses, where people of different ranks could debate.
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Scientific revolution", "Reason applied to society", "Books, salons, coffee houses", "Revolutions after 1776"]}
### Portugal's version
Portuguese reformers, often called "estrangeirados" because they had lived abroad, brought these ideas home. Luís António Verney's book on "the true method of studying" (1746) attacked old-fashioned teaching. Pombal, like Frederick the Great of Prussia or Catherine the Great of Russia, was an **enlightened despot**: he used reason to modernise, but kept absolute power.
> The Enlightenment taught that the world can be understood and society can be improved by reason, an idea behind modern science and democracy.
!! Enlightenment thinkers all wanted democracy => Many, like Voltaire, preferred a wise king to rule by the people

# Pombal's reforms
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1756", "l": "Douro wine region demarcated"}, {"y": "1759", "l": "Jesuits expelled"}, {"y": "1772", "l": "Coimbra University reformed"}, {"y": "1777", "l": "Pombal falls"}]}
Pombal modernised education and trade, expelled the Jesuits (1759), created the Douro wine demarcation (1756), one of the world's first, and reduced discrimination against New Christians.
---
Pombal wanted a stronger, richer state that depended less on Britain and on the nobility and the Church. His reforms touched trade, schools, religion and law, and many still shape Portugal today.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1756", "l": "Douro wine company"}, {"y": "1759", "l": "Jesuits expelled"}, {"y": "1772", "l": "Coimbra University reform"}, {"y": "1773", "l": "New Christian label ended"}]}
### Port wine and the Douro
British merchants dominated the port trade, and quality was falling as cheap wine was mixed in. In 1756 Pombal created a royal company to control production and marked the boundaries of the Douro vineyards with granite posts, many still standing. It is one of the world's oldest regulated wine regions, alongside earlier efforts in Tokaj and Chianti.
### Church and education
The Jesuits ran most schools and had great influence at court. Pombal accused them of plotting and expelled them from Portugal and the empire in 1759, before France and Spain did the same. The state then had to build its own school system. The University of Coimbra was reformed in 1772, gaining mathematics and natural science faculties, a botanical garden, an observatory and laboratories.
### Society
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Old/New Christian distinction ended", "Slave imports to mainland banned", "School of commerce founded (1759)", "State censorship board (1768)"]}
New Christians, descendants of Jews forced to convert in 1497, had faced discrimination for centuries; in 1773 the legal distinction was abolished. In 1761 Pombal banned bringing enslaved people into mainland Portugal, though slavery continued in Brazil and Africa.
> Pombal modernised Portugal from the top down, with reforms that were bold, lasting and imposed without consent.
!? The granite boundary markers of 1758–61 are known as "marcos pombalinos", and over a hundred survive in the Douro vineyards.

# The Encyclopédie
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "28", "l": "volumes"}, {"v": "~72,000", "l": "articles"}, {"v": "1751–1772", "l": "publication"}]}
Diderot and d'Alembert's Encyclopédie (1751–1772) gathered knowledge on everything from trades to philosophy, spreading Enlightenment ideas across Europe.
---
It began as a plan to translate an English encyclopaedia and grew into one of the great publishing projects in history. Denis Diderot and the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert edited it, with well over a hundred contributors, including Voltaire, Rousseau and Montesquieu.
@viz {"k": "nums", "items": [{"v": "28", "l": "volumes, text and plates"}, {"v": "~72,000", "l": "articles"}, {"v": "21 years", "l": "from 1751 to 1772"}]}
### Why it was radical
The Encyclopédie organised knowledge by human reason, following Francis Bacon's division into memory, reason and imagination, rather than putting theology at the top. Cross-references sometimes led readers from a pious article to a critical one, a clever way past the censors.
@viz {"k": "tree", "root": "Human knowledge", "items": ["Memory: history", "Reason: philosophy, science", "Imagination: arts"]}
### Respect for crafts
Eleven volumes were plates: detailed engravings of looms, forges, glassworks, printing presses and mines. Treating the work of artisans as real knowledge, worth studying alongside philosophy, was itself an Enlightenment statement.
### Banned and unstoppable
• Its licence was revoked in 1759 and the Pope condemned it
• Diderot carried on in secret; the printer even cut passages behind his back
• Cheaper editions in Switzerland and Italy spread it across Europe, including to readers in Lisbon
> The Encyclopédie aimed to put all useful knowledge in reach of any educated reader, and in doing so challenged who decides what is true.
!? One contributor, the Chevalier de Jaucourt, wrote about 17,000 articles, roughly a quarter of the whole work.

# Lessons for today
@viz {"k": "list", "items": ["Seismic building codes", "Tsunami evacuation routes", "Earthquake drills", "Reinforcing old buildings"]}
Lisbon still sits near active faults. Modern building codes, tsunami warnings and earthquake drills draw on lessons from 1755.
---
Lisbon sits in a seismically active region. Large earthquakes can come from offshore faults along the boundary between the African and Eurasian plates, as in 1755, or from faults closer by in the lower Tagus valley. The question is not if another strong earthquake will come, but when.
@viz {"k": "time", "events": [{"y": "1531", "l": "Lisbon quake, Tagus valley"}, {"y": "1755", "l": "Great offshore quake"}, {"y": "1909", "l": "Benavente quake"}, {"y": "1969", "l": "Offshore quake, M ~7.9"}]}
### What modern science adds
Seismologists of IPMA, the national weather and earthquake institute, monitor the ground constantly. If a large quake strikes offshore, a tsunami could reach the Algarve in perhaps 15–20 minutes and Lisbon in about half an hour or more, so people must act on the shaking itself rather than wait for an official alert.
### Building codes
Portugal introduced its first seismic building rules in 1958, and today follows the European standard Eurocode 8. Buildings designed to these rules are meant to bend without collapsing. The weak spots are older buildings, including Pombaline ones whose wooden cages were cut away to open shops.
@viz {"k": "cmp", "l": {"t": "Higher risk", "items": ["pre-1958 masonry", "gaiola walls removed", "soft ground near river"]}, "r": {"t": "Lower risk", "items": ["modern code design", "intact gaiola", "bedrock foundations"]}}
### What you can do
@viz {"k": "flow", "steps": ["Drop to the ground", "Cover under a sturdy table", "Hold on until it stops", "Coast: walk to high ground"]}
Portugal runs a national drill, "A Terra Treme", every autumn, in which schools and offices practise these steps for one minute.
> The best tribute to 1755 is preparation: strong buildings, fast warnings and people who know what to do.
!! Earthquakes can be predicted days ahead => No method can predict the time of an earthquake; we can only map risk and prepare
